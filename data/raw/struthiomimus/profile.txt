Struthiomimus
Struthiomimus was a fast, ostrich-like dinosaur that lived during the Late Cretaceous period, around 75 million years ago, in North America. Standing up to 12 feet long, it had long legs built for running and a beak without teeth, likely feeding on a mix of plants and small animals. Its speed and agility helped it evade predators, making it one of the swiftest dinosaurs of its time. Struthiomimus was a nimble and social creature, possibly living in groups for protection.

Offspring Behavior
All the females in a harem lay their eggs together, but it is the male who takes on the responsibility of incubating them. When the eggs hatch, baby Struthiomimus, known as chicks , are treated equally and will see each other as full siblings.
Mothers do not play an active role in raising the young, although they may assist in protecting them if necessary.
Once a Struthiomimus chick hatches, it must wait for its feathers to dry, remaining motionless in the nest under its father's care. After their feathers dry, hatchlings will stay close to their father, as they are fragile and rely on him for safety. Though cautious, they are naturally curious and will attempt to peck at anything within reach.
As chicks grow into juveniles, they become more adventurous and begin exploring farther from their father. The father, meanwhile, tries to keep the group together, though sometimes the chicks may wander into potentially dangerous situations. At this stage, young Struthiomimus enjoy playing with one another—chasing each other and picking up objects from the ground. It is also during this phase that the father starts encouraging them to forage for their own food, fostering their independence.
By the time the chicks reach adolescence, their father starts to pay less attention to them, forcing them to become more self-reliant. Though mostly independent at this point, they still remain near their father's harem. If the group is part of a nomadic flock, they will resume migrating around this time. Male adolescents begin to display more vocal and aggressive behavior, while females remain relatively unchanged in temperament.
Upon reaching maturity, Struthiomimus are forced to leave their father's harem, but remain part of the wider flock. Male offspring will attempt to attract females and form their own harems, while females will evaluate potential males and choose which harem to join.

Orphaned Behavior
Orphaned Struthiomimus chicks tend to be extremely cautious, constantly seeking out herbivore herds or groups that might accept them, even if they are not of the same species. If they are fortunate, they may find a flock with a harem willing to adopt them as their own.
Struthiomimus that grow up without the presence of other Struthiomimus may struggle with socializing within their own species. They may even develop a preference for the company of other herbivores. If they were raised in a herd of a particular species, they will often favor the presence of that species over others.
Struthiomimus may end up raised by broody Citipati hens, and as a result prefer to stay near Citipatis as adults.

General Behavior
Struthiomimus are generally nomadic creatures that love traversing vast lands. Their inherent curiosity leads them to peck at anything in sight—rocks, branches, roots, nuts, flowers, and insects. Often, they can be seen pecking at rocks and consuming them to use as gastroliths, aiding in their digestion.
In addition to their nomadic nature, Struthiomimus are inherently social creatures that thrive in the company of one another. It is common to observe them grooming each other, which helps strengthen their bonds. They remain vigilant, often acting as sentinels for their group and taking turns to stay alert. Struthiomimus can frequently be seen following other herbivore herds, which offers them both safety and social interaction.
Although Struthiomimus are not particularly fond of water and typically prefer to avoid it, they may develop a liking for bathing if they have been living alongside Deinocheirus. In such cases, they might venture into shallow waters to take baths, but only if they feel safe in their environment.
Struthiomimus are renowed for their sentry abilities. Always alert to their surroundings, they tend to be relied on by other herbivorous species nearby to sound the alarm if danger is approaching.

Social Behavior
Struthiomimus are highly social creatures that thrive in the company of others. They usually form large herds known as flocks, which consist of multiple males and their respective harems. Although males try to keep their distance from other males and their harems, they tolerate each other's presence within the flock, except during the breeding season, when they will aggressively defend their space. Females, on the other hand, coexist peacefully and often interact freely, even across different harems. It is common to observe males engaging in playful or aggressive sparring, delivering kicks to establish dominance. Meanwhile, females, whether from the same or different harems, often forage together, creating a more harmonious atmosphere.
Within these flocks, individuals may form strong social bonds. Hens in a harem often develop close pair bonds with their male, and chicks growing up together can also forge lasting connections. As males mature, they might lose these bonds or merely tolerate each other more than unfamiliar males, while female bonds tend to persist even after reaching maturity, even if they belong to different harems.
Male Struthiomimus enjoy spending time with their harem and can often be seen grooming, resting, or tending to their hens and chicks. When a male finds food, he will call his harem and offspring to eat first, even helping them reach food that might be out of their grasp. Males are also the first to leap into action when it comes to defending their harem from threats.
Males are generally more vocal and showy than females. They frequently practice their calls, whether for singing or alerting the group to danger, and take great pride in preening their feathers after any physical effort. In contrast, females are more laid-back, grooming themselves throughout the day but not to the same extent as their male counterparts.
Interspecies Behavior
Male Struthiomimus may display aggression toward smaller creatures such as Dryosaurus and Psittacosaurus, particularly during the breeding season. While both males and females are aggressive toward small or lone carnivores, especially baby carnivores, females are generally more tolerant of the presence of small herbivores even when they are alone. Males, however, may show increased aggression toward smaller herbivores during breeding times, especially when defending their territory or harem.
Male Struthiomimus defend a radius of 5 body lengths around their territory or harem against Dryosaurus and Psittacosaurus. This should be treated as a territory challenge.
Struthiomimus flocks may sometimes choose to settle with larger herbivores, such as Deinocheirus, becoming more sedentary in nature. By staying with these large creatures, the Struthiomimus benefit from their protection and social company, and may even adopt new behaviors like taking baths in shallow waters if they feel safe enough.
Solitary Struthiomimus, on the other hand, may be seen following herds of other herbivores, provided those herbivores tolerate their presence. This behavior offers them safety in numbers and better opportunities for foraging.
One unique interspecies relationship male Struthiomimus have is with Therizinosaurus. Male Struthiomimus find Therizinosaurus irresistable, and attempt to court any females they are lucky enough to come across. These behaviors usually amuse female Therizinosaurus - though males may not find it quite as entertaining - and  can result in the Struthiomimus grouping up with the female, acting as a pseudo-mate, and receiving her protection and affection.

Courting & Nesting Behavior
During the breeding season, male Struthiomimus become notably more aggressive toward other males and even small creatures. They also grow more vocal and display their feathers more prominently. When the flock comes to a stop, the males seize the opportunity to claim a small area for their mating ritual. These areas are fiercely defended from other males. In this space, the cobs will collect rocks and various objects they find appealing, using them to build a decorative scene. Rival males may attempt to steal these decorations in an effort to sabotage each other and improve their own displays.
Once their area is prepared, the males will begin calling out, attempting to catch the attention of the hens. This display not only highlights their vocal abilities, such as sounding alerts, but also showcases their vibrant, contrasting feathers through elaborate posturing. If a female is interested, she will approach and respond with her own vocalizations. If the male reciprocates her interest, they will interlock necks in a bonding display.
After a female has accepted the male and joined his harem, she is allowed to rest within his claimed temporary territory, along with the other females in the group. The male will not permit other females to rest in his area unless they have officially joined his harem. Once a female has joined, the male will return to his display, attempting to attract more hens if space in his harem allows.
When it’s time for nesting, the male Struthiomimus takes the lead by building a communal nest where all the females of his harem will lay their eggs. Once the eggs are laid, it is the male who takes on the responsibility of incubating them and caring for the chicks after they hatch. Once the chicks are born and their feathers have dried, they instinctively follow their father everywhere. The father is meticulous in his care, ensuring the chicks are well-fed and sheltered. It’s common to see Struthiomimus chicks huddled beneath their father, using him as shelter, whether it's raining or the sun is too intense.
As the chicks grow into juveniles, the father begins encouraging them to forage for their own food, gradually teaching them independence. By the time they reach adolescence, the father’s involvement significantly reduces, as he starts to ignore them unless they need protection. From this point, the adolescent Struthiomimus must begin to care for themselves, relying less on their father as they approach full independence.
Males may adopt orphaned chicks at any time, treating them as if they are their own.
Aggressive Behavior
Struthiomimus generally tolerate the presence of most herbivores. However, when faced with aggression, they will stand their ground unless outnumbered or overpowered, at which point they will retreat. In confrontations with other males, fights rarely end in death, though they may leave one of the Struthiomimus injured.
When encountering small or tiny carnivores, Struthiomimus will not hesitate to attack, often attempting to kill them unless they believe they can be overpowered. In all their fights, regardless of the opponent’s species, Struthiomimus primarily relies on their powerful kicks to both attack and defend themselves. If necessary, they will use their speed and agility to flee and escape from danger.
If Struthiomimus begins an engagement on a small or tiny carnivore, Struthiomimus does not respect a 2 call from the carnivore they are aggressing, but may not chase the carnivore into a different POI. All other rules apply.
Predator Reaction
Struthiomimus only group:
When Struthiomimus encounters a group of medium-sized or smaller carnivores, they will carefully analyze the situation. Most of the time, they will choose to flee if possible, trying to avoid confrontation, especially if they sense that the predator may overpower them. However, if they feel compelled to fight or need to defend a member of their harem, the entire harem will spring into action together.
In contrast, when faced with large or greater carnivores, Struthiomimus will always prioritize fleeing. If a single Struthiomimus becomes cornered, it will attempt to defend itself, but it will not receive any assistance from its companions.

In a mixed-species herd:
Struthiomimus become significantly bolder when part of mixed-species herds. When threatened by predators, they serve as effective lookouts, often being the first to alert the herd of approaching danger. If the predator is challenged by the species they are grouped with, some Struthiomimus may experience a surge of confidence, participating in intimidation displays by kicking their legs and hissing at the threat.
However, should the predator advance on the herd or target the Struthiomimus directly, their courage quickly crumbles. They panic, fleeing to the back of the group and using their herdmates as living shields to avoid confrontation.
