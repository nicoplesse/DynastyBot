Alioramus
    "Alio"
Alioramus are nomadic carnivores that thrive through mixed sex packs, composing a maximum of 2 females, and up to 6 males. Females long for a lifelong female companion, while males maintain a fluid, dominance-based hierarchy within a pack. They travel long distances, and run down their prey to the point of exhaustion, nipping at them frequently to wear them down over time.

Physical Dimorphism
Alioramus have slight color dimorphism.
This profile may have high and low T individuals. However, this only has an effect on appearance, and does not have females acting as males and vice versa.
♀️ Females are typically darker, with less bright colors across their bodies. They can show lighter colors, but the patterning is always low contrast and muted. Melanism and other very dark skins are highly desirable in females.
♂️ Males show brighter, flashier colors. This can include streaks of white that contrast well with the other colors in their coat.
Muted, dark and melanistic males are picked as a last resort as courting partners.
Physical dimorphism for Alioramus is not mandatory, but individuals differing from the typical dimorphism may face difficulties during courtship.
Males and females have different behavioral traits, read under Social Interaction for more information.

✦ Offspring Behavior (Nested)
Alioramus offspring, known as cubs, burst out of their eggs ready to take on the world. They hatch fully precocial, and stagger to their feet within moments of hatching, shaking off their damp feathers.
Cubs are usually immediately greeted by at least one - if not two - females: their mother and an auntie, who coo over the fresh young and get to work nibbling and preening stuck feathers.
Alioramus hatchlings do not differentiate between the adult females - both are equally regarded as a mother figure, and most cubs grow without ever knowing who their biological mother is out of the pair.
Cubs are quite often a handful. Innately curious, Alioramus young take to investigating almost everything they encounter - assuming they are permitted to do so by their caretakers.
Sticks, flowers, mushrooms and bones are common play toys, and often fetched up by eager cubs who guard them from their siblings fiercely. Adult females frequently have to intervene in little squabbles between cubs over these circumstances - while another sibling typically steals the possession amidst the chaos.
Alioramus cubs are escorted to almost all locations they go to - or attempt to go to - until Adolescent. Adult females take it in turns to ferry rambunctious cubs to water sources, and get anxious leaving cubs alone.
Cubs are very chatty, and use a wide variety of different noises with their mothers and clutchmates, mimicking the calls their parents use in hunts and throughout their adult lives. Cubs tend to be less noisy when fully satisfied and calm, when the pack has settled and they are surrounded by their siblings and friends.
Investigating the world around them is vital for a healthy cub’s development; a lack of interest or obliviousness to the world indicates illness. Cubs work together to investigate things that are larger than them in a sort of “mobbing” behavior, bobbing their heads and squeaking at curious things and new experiences.
However, cubs only investigate larger dinosaurs from a distance, quickly bolting away if the larger creature even slightly moves towards them.
Mothers strictly prevent cubs from approaching their fathers or other adult males within the pack. Adult males play too roughly and lack restraint with young offspring, often posing a genuine risk of serious injury - or even accidental death. 
This protective instinct can draw out an uncommon show of aggression from females towards males that approach their young.
However, as cubs reach Adolescent, mothers gradually allow them to roam further. This developmental shift coincides with heightened interest from adult males, who begin to investigate these new "playmates".
Hormonal shifts during adolescence lead to a clear behavioral divergence between males and females.
Females begin to settle in temperament, showing reduced interest in play and social chaos of the rest of the pack. They gravitate towards stability, usually in the form of a close bond with a sister or other similarly aged female. When no such companion is available, they trail behind the adult female pair - or their mother, if she is alone - and mirror their movements, as closely as they are tolerated.
If a female cub lacks sisters and her mother already has a female companion, she begins to experience increasing emotional - and physical - distance from the adult female pair. This aloofness usually escalates into aggression as she matures, frequently resulting in solitary females dispersing from their natal pack earlier than their peers.
Males, meanwhile, experience a rapid rise in testosterone - resulting in bold, dominant and increasingly aggressive behavior. They engage in frequent bouts of roughhousing with their brothers and other males within the pack, using physical play and sparring as a means of testing strength and hierarchy within the group.
At Sub-Adult, females are driven out by the adult female pair to prevent future competition - unless the mother lacks a bonded female companion. In such cases, she finds a new companion in a daughter, allowing her to remain as her permanent bonded female companion.
By contrast, Sub-Adult males are permitted to remain within the pack indefinitely, provided there is room for them. However, during periods of female dispersal, unrelated males to these females within the pack may choose to leave voluntarily, following these females to form new packs and courting opportunities elsewhere.
✦ Orphaned Behavior (Wild Spawn)
Orphaned Alioramus cubs initially display behavioral patterns similar to those raised by a solo female - in particular, heightened distress and instability. With their emotional needs not fully met, orphans are often visibly unsettled in early life.
Whereas offspring reared by a single female tend to become loud, demanding and persistent in their distress - frequently calling, following and attempting to demand care from their mothers - orphans quickly discover that such behavior goes unanswered - or is actively punished by drawing danger to them.
As a result, after repeated neglect or negative reactions, orphans reduce their vocalizations dramatically - often leading to much quieter adults. They learn early that noise draws unwanted attention - from both predators, and potentially dangerous adult males - and adapt by becoming quiet and observant far earlier than typical cubs.
Orphans become independent at a young age. Play - even between siblings that remain together - declines quickly, with curiosity changing to caution and more skittish behavior.
Orphans may attempt to scavenge from kills before Sub-Adult. They will quickly flee if aggressed by the owner of the body, and - depending on when this happens in the development - can result in a long lasting fear of the species for the rest of their lives if bitten.
Orphans may be adopted by female pairs that are interested, but are ignored by males. Males with no females do not group with orphans. Males never adopt. Females in same-sex pairs - or those with similar aged young - will adopt orphans easily. Solitary females do not adopt. 
For an orphaned Alioramus cub, the approach of unfamiliar adults is not immediately interpreted as comforting. Instead, it triggers acute fear responses - something which forces adult females seeking to adopt to tread very carefully.
However, whilst frightened at first, almost all cubs can be won over with gentle encouragement, and eventually settle into adopted life with their new caretaker.

Alioramus are intelligent, agile and swift carnivores, capable of traveling great distances in pursuit of the best hunting opportunities.
Unlike their more powerful relatives, they lack a strong bite force - and so avoid direct conflict with apex predators wherever possible. Their survival strategy favors speed, awareness and adaptability over brute strength.
Maintaining an active lifestyle, Alioramus are almost constantly on the move - pausing only to sleep, eat, or nest. They remain relatively quiet when traveling, only chattering briefly amongst each other so that they may scope their surroundings for threats or prey undetected.
Alioramus are highly vocal creatures otherwise, and communicate often in a series of growls, grunts and chuffs. Each pack develops their own unique vocal signature, often showcased by a particular greeting when pack members meet.
They only broadcast to alert the rest of the pack, call for others, or during hunts. Broadcast is a universal summoning call for other Alioramus, or displaying heightened excitement during a hunt.
Sunbathing is a cherished pastime for Alioramus. They often take brief naps in particularly sunny spots, relishing in the warmth against their feathers.
During these rest periods, they engage in deep self-grooming rituals. Closely bonded female pairs meticulously clean each other, removing insects, dirt, and remnants of past hunts from their feathers. Males, by contrast, groom themselves, lacking the same cooperative bond as their female counterparts.
When not sleeping or grooming, Alioramus are highly alert, observing their surroundings and commenting quickly to alert others if danger is spotted. They sniff frequently, especially when encountering a new object of interest. (This can be accomplished in game by using the :look: emote and cutting it off.)
Alioramus show an aversion to deep or fast moving water. While able to swim, Alioramus prefer to avoid entering rivers and lakes whenever possible. 
Sudden immersion in water is distressing for Alioramus - water weighs down their feathers and dulls their agility, leaving them temporarily sluggish. A dip in the water or a heavy onset of rain render Alioramus soaked through, prompting these carnivores to pause for rest, shaking their feathers dry.
Alioramus spend a lot of their time tracking and stalking herbivore herds. While rarely interested in immediate confrontation, they trail these herds at a distance for long periods, studying their migration routes and looking for potential weakness in the group. 
This behavior is as much an information gathering event as it is a precursor to a hunt - packs or individuals may shadow a herd for a long time without ever attempting a hunt, while others may stalk until the moment is right before striking.
While stalking in this manner, Alioramus keep their distance, careful not to trigger confrontation until the right moment. Some Alioramus never hunt the herd they stalk, and may move on to other prey as necessary.
✦ Downtime Behavior
Alioramus are extremely quiet in the nighttime, communicating primarily through soft clicks, huffs, and body language.
They stop migrating at this time, and often search for dens to take claim of in order to sleep in. If no dens are available, the next preferred choice is deep shrubbery or a cliffside.
During inactive periods, males and females often separate entirely to sleep and socialize; with females remaining with their close bonds and offspring and males taking positions in order to watch over them.
Males in a mixed-sex group do not spar at this time - those that attempt to are quickly corrected by the females.
Females are often seen cuddled up as close as possible together - even if it means incidentally forcing their offspring a bit further away.
Males may court females via giftgiving and preening during downtime.

Alioramus exhibit strong social dimorphism, with males and females fulfilling distinct roles that stabilize one another within a pack. Balanced packs rely on the presence of both sexes and when one is absent, social cohesion deteriorates.
Male-only groups trend toward escalating aggression and internal conflict, while female-only groups become overly passive, lacking the assertiveness required for defence and structure.
✦ Females
From the moment they hatch, female Alioramus are instinctively driven to find a lifelong female companion. These bonds - stronger even than those between mother and offspring - are most commonly formed between sisters raised together. However, unrelated females who cross paths and find comfort in one another can also develop this deep connection. 
Even orphaned or solitary females feel an undeniable pull toward companionship, and seek out another lonely female to form the bond they crave.
Females exhibit heightened anxiety when isolated from a female companion, and actively seek a bonding opportunity throughout their lives.
Bonding is reinforced through frequent physical contact. They walk close together, brushing against one another's sides, nuzzle faces and engage in quiet, cooperative feeding behaviors. They may hunt critters together, or take chunks from corpses and move a distance away to feed in privacy.
More reserved by nature, females thrive on gentle companionship, and never show aggression to their companion. Their relationship with their chosen partner is paramount, and they remain fiercely bonded for life. 
The loss of this bond is devastating - when one female dies, the surviving partner may enter a prolonged mourning state, refusing to eat and - in some cases - succumbing to grief herself. Others, though deeply affected, will eventually seek a new female companion to rebuild the bond.
✦ Males
Male Alioramus have a more fluid role within a pack. Naturally competitive and prone to aggression, they frequently engage in sparring matches with other males, keen to assert dominance and test their strength. While these encounters can appear violence, they are often mutual and function as structured outlets for tension, rather than true aggression.
Unlike females, males do not seek lifelong bonds with another, and are perfectly capable of living a solitary life if needed. However, most prefer to be grouped with others - if only for more chance of hunting success - and some go further, developing small bonds with others.
Brothers often stick together, preferring familiarity, but male Alioramus are also open to forming small, casual friendships with unrelated males, creating loose-knit social groups within the pack.
Brothers show the strongest male to male tolerance. Males raised together tend to spar more frequently, but utilize greater restraint, engaging in rough play that rarely escalates to serious injury.
Male Alioramus are highly sensitive to changes in pack composition. The arrival - or loss - of females dramatically alters male behavior within the pack. Without females present, all male groups become increasingly restless and unpredictable, often forming aggressive cliques and engaging in bullying behaviors.
Stress and excitement are expressed primarily through physicality. Increased pacing, higher rates of sparring and air snapping are common during periods of tension. 
Conversely, well balanced packs see males displaying more restraint. Sparring is less frequent and resolves more quickly - and males exhibit greater cooperation and tolerance.
In rare cases, older or socially experienced males develop a stabilizing influence within a mixed sex pack. These individuals intercept fights between younger males, intervene in disputes, and show increased tolerance. 
However, even these stabilizing males struggle to maintain order in the absence of females, becoming restless and volatile over time.
✦ Female-Only Packs
Female only Alioramus only ever consist of a bonded pair of females. Alioramus packs never exceed more than 2 females.
These packs are notably more cautious and skittish than mixed sex groups. Without males to provide assertiveness and physical pressure, female only pairs avoid confrontation whenever possible, and steer clear of perceived threats.
Hunting success is reduced without additional pack members. Females rely more on opportunistic feeding and critters, and are more likely to abandon hunts early if success does not seem guaranteed.
✦ Male-Only Packs
Male only Alioramus packs operate under a very different dynamic than mixed or female only groups. While they provide security through sheer aggression, they lack the cohesion and stability that females bring to a pack.
Males have no permanent leader in a male only pack. Dominance is tested regularly through sparring and aggressive posturing, with the 'leader' of a male only pack often changing between scuffles.
The strongest male - or "leader" - at any given time enjoys priority access to food and resting spots, but his authority is never set in stone - and liable to change as soon as another male bests him to claim his spot.
Males constantly engage in sparring matches where they lunge, snap and scratch each other to assert dominance.
The lowest-ranking male - essentially the one that loses the most sparring matches within the group - may suffer unintentional mistreatment from his packmates. More dominant members control access to food, forcing him to wait until they have eaten their fill, which frequently results in malnutrition and missed meals for these weaker males.
✦ Mixed Sex Packs
At the heart of every functional pack is a bonded pair of females, whose leadership dictates the group's cohesion, survival, and long-term success.
These females act as the foundation of the group, leading its movements, making key decisions, and ensuring stability among its members. They lead the way on migrations, and make key decisions on which prey to stalk or hunt.
Males contribute physical pressure and confidence, while females provide cohesion and restraint. Together, these roles create a balanced and adaptable group with the highest chance of success.
✦ Solitary Behavior
Solitary female Alioramus experience significant social strain. Driven by their innate need to form a lifelong bond with another female, they actively search for companionship throughout their lives. Even without a female partner, they will still form groups with males, though these are never enough to fully meet their emotional needs.
Male Alioramus fare considerably better alone. While still social animals by nature, males are capable of maintaining stable solitary lives without any signs of psychological stress.
✦ Mutated Behavior
Albino Alioramus are born with both social and survival disadvantages, due to their coloration. While not automatically shunned, they are viewed as high risk companions, particularly during hunts or when hiding from larger, more dangerous predators.
Albino females suffer little to no problems within a social grouping - but males are kept on the edges of pack life, with packmates sometimes refusing to sleep beside them at rest periods.
Melanistic females are highly desirable due to their dark patterning, allowing them to blend in better with the deep shadows and dark shrubbery of their preferred biomes.
As males display their colors within courtship, Melanistic males are much less preferred and are typically regarded as submissive males (unless having proven himself otherwise).
✦ High Testosterone and Low Testosterone Individuals
High testosterone females are much snappier in their anxiety than other females, and display brighter colors. Males often show very little interest in courting these females - only courting her if no other females are available.
Low testosterone males quickly fall to the bottom of the social hierarchy amongst males while within a pack. They struggle with fighting and submit when bitten much sooner, crouching and crying out. As a result, they are usually the last pick for females during courtship, and the first to be pushed out if more space is required for adult male offspring, or more suitable unrelated males.

✦ Females Encountering Females
When bonded female pairs encounter other females, they take the lead in responding to the situation. Females approach the new females while males remain behind them, observing but not intervening.
These encounters are tense, but restrained. The leading females posture, vocalizing warnings with sharp hisses and deep growls, and pace in front of them, showing a clear threat if the rival(s) do not leave.
If warning displays are met with aggressive responses - or ignored entirely - females launch into a rare display of violence, engaging with the rivals directly to drive them out.
Should the females lose this challenge, the pack destabilizes. Males may choose to disperse entirely and follow the new females instead, while others will stay loyal to the losing females and leave the area with them.
Solitary females may be tolerated if they maintain significant distance and do not attempt to approach or integrate into the pack. Never grouping with them, the bonded pair may turn a blind eye to their presence, but will immediately aggress them should they get too close.
This context - as well as during nesting and rearing offspring - represents the rare circumstances in which females display aggression to others.
Solitary females encountering other solitary females approach cautiously, but show affection quickly if they appear to have similar or complementary temperaments, enabling them to form a lifelong bond.
✦ Packs With Females Encountering Others
Encounters between 2 mixed sex packs are cautious and tense if neither are willing to leave the POI. Both groups slow, reducing their vocalizations, and allow their female pairs (or solo female) to assess one another from the front.
Bonded females lead all aggression in these circumstances. Males remain behind their respective female pairs and do not posture or advance.
If the other group is simply passing through, they may seek to avoid conflict and show non-hostile intent. Females lower their posture into crouches, offering soft coos to indicate submission rather than a challenge. 
Such displays are always respected. When submission is shown, the resident females accept the signal without escalation, and both groups disengage peacefully and continue on their way.
Should the rival Alioramus be a solitary male - or group of males - how they are considered depends on whether the pack has space for more males - and how willing they are to accept new members.
If the pack has no capacity for additional males and no males are willing to disperse, the bonded females take the lead in driving them off with warning displays - or direct the pack to leave the area, depending on the size of the other group. 
If these warnings are ignored or met with aggression, the males of the group step forward, launching into a vicious attack on the other group.
The outcome of the conflict determines the result - should the resident pack lose, they are forced out of the POI. If the rival males lose, they are chased out of LOS by the resident males.
If the pack has space for additional males, the females step forward to assess the males, and usually welcome them in quickly with soft shakes and gentle chuffs. Only when the females have accepted the males do other males allow them within the group.
✦ Male-Only Packs Encountering Others
Encounters between male only Alioramus groups are usually cautious, rather than immediately hostile. Males pace and circle each other, vocalizing to gauge one another's confidence - and intent.
If both packs have room, males usually tolerate one another and merge into a larger group without much issue. The temporary hierarchy in the pack changes, with the newest males placed at the bottom, and forced to work their way up by showing dominance, and sparring with their new packmates.
Should there not be enough room, males fight for control of the POI - posturing and aggressive calling three times before launching into battle. Either group can choose to move on if they do not wish to stay in the POI, but must do so quickly to avoid the other group chasing them out too closely.
✦ Sparring
Sparring is an almost ritualized behavior between males, used to alleviate stress, test strength, settle disputes and control social standing within a group.
It begins with quick nips and shoving, and escalates into brief, controlled fights. The first first male to reach half health is determined to be the loser, resulting in a temporary drop in social standing and an immediate end to the engagement.
✦ Food Aggression
In packs without females, feeding is a highly competitive event. Dominant males routinely bully subordinates to secure first access to meals, often forcing weaker individuals to wait until most of the food is gone.
In mixed-sex packs, females regulate this feeding behavior. They position themselves at kills and limit male bullying, ensuring all group members are able to eat before disputes escalate.
Alioramus are extremely possessive of food toward ungrouped individuals. Lone or unaffiliated Alioramus approaching a carcass are aggressively driven off to protect their claim.
✦ Maternal Aggression
Adult males are not permitted to approach or interact with cubs below Adolescent. Females actively position themselves between males and their young at all times, and will react aggressively if this boundary is not respected.
This extends to males who are perceived to be acting too aggressively towards offspring of Adolescent age - an act which is quickly reprimanded with an aggressive call and a bite.

✦ All Species
Alioramus enjoy tracking and stalking herbivore herds over long distances. They do not always hunt these species - and can sometimes trail a herd for several days without attempting to, before leaving to follow another species.
They make sure to leave enough room between them and the herd to avoid provoking aggression or fear in the group.
Alioramus do not tolerate scavengers near their kills.
Unlike some other predators, Alioramus are not territorial towards other species, and instead migrate between keen locations in search of available prey. The only time they defend a specific area is during nesting. 
In this phase, males take on the role of patrolling and guarding the area surrounding the nest site, with all females in the pack focusing entirely on raising their young.
They do not challenge away Apex carnivores or sauropods, and instead warn the nesting females and offspring to flee the area as soon as possible.
✦ Predator Reaction
Alioramus avoid Apex carnivores where possible. Whilst not outright fleeing on sight, they keep their distance, and flee from engagements or hunts started by these species - only defending themselves as needed.
When being hunted, Alioramus take the same coordinated and endurance based strategy they do when hunting.
If a packmate is clamped or restrained by a hunter - even an Apex one - the defenders rush to try and save them, preferring not to leave a member of their group behind. This is especially true for females - who will fight to the bitter end to save their female companion.

Alioramus are endurance hunters, enjoying long-lasting hunts where they follow prey and targets for long distances, aiming to exhaust them. Alioramus are also coordinated hunters, communicating with short cut off calls or long, loud calls. Each Alioramus in a hunting party moves in synchronization with others in the pack, tag teaming targets, and quickly moving out of reach of prey as soon as a bite has been landed.
Alioramus use loud calls to indicate: the presence of prey, threats or simply to indicate excitement during an engagement.
If faced with difficulty during a hunt, Alioramus will “test” different strategies in order to successfully hunt their prey.
Alioramus must hunt Medium tier or below prey or any (unattended or attended) offspring of any tier before attempting a hunt on anything else in LOS.
Alioramus may hunt up to Apex tier, but do not hunt Apex carnivores, or any Sauropods, such as Amargasaurus, Ampelosaurus, Apatosaurus, or Argentinosaurus, unless the sauropod is adolescent or below. 
Alioramus only hunts Apex tier species with 5 in the hunting party, and if no other options are available within line of sight.

✦ Courtship Within a Pack
Courtship occurs at rest stops, usually after a long migration. It is initiated by the males of the group, who begin seeking the attention of one of the females.
They approach with a crouched stance, calling softly towards her, and attempt to walk close, mirroring her movements. The ultimate goal is to earn permission to walk beside her and her female partner (if she has one), proving he can match a more restrained pace without disrupting the cohesion between the pair.
Movements are kept slow and deliberate, careful not to make the female nervous. Sudden movement or approaching too quickly can disqualify a male from a female's interest, and prompt her to turn her back and move away from him.
Males that attempt to separate a female from her pair - or deliberately block her path - are immediately rejected for this breeding season.
Should she be interested, she may allow him to travel beside her or her pair for a short distance, quietly assessing his suitability for courtship and nesting.
If the male is deemed suitable, the interested female will temporarily peel away from her partner and move away with the male, calling to him softly. They settle beside each other in a secluded spot, cooing and preening at each other's feathers.
Only one male may attempt to court a female or the female pair at a time. Multiple males wishing to court must challenge one another for the right to do so. 
Challenging males walk straight up to the courting male, maintaining a head on stance and issuing short hisses and low growls. They do not pursue or address either female at this time.
If the courting male has lost a spar against the challenging male the same day, he must forfeit his right and step aside.
Otherwise, a fierce battle ensues, with its outcome determining who has the right to court the females.
The fight ends when one male submits, retreats, or reaches half health. The losing male may not attempt to challenge another male for courtship rights again during that rest period.
Winning a challenge grants a male the right to attempt courtship only. Females may still reject him by turning away or leaving, and dominance does not guarantee acceptance. However, other males are not permitted to attempt until he has had his chance.
Females consistently appear to prefer dominant males - though others may prefer a male that is capable of showing great restraint.
One or both females may choose to court the same male if they wish, but some opt to choose different males.
✦ Courtship Outside a Pack
If a pack consists of no suitable males for courtship - such as packs composed solely of offspring or relatives of both females - the females may lead the pack to a universal courtship grounds in Star Ravine, a secluded waterfall area within Wollemi Forest.
Multiple packs may co-exist temporarily in this area. Females tolerate other females so long as distance is maintained, and interactions remain solely focused on courtship.
The courtship ritual for males outside the pack is the same as when courting within a pack, but the packs do not merge, and instead go their separate ways after courtship.
✦ Nesting
The females then lead the pack to a suitable nesting location. Both females place their nests very close together - even if only one is having a clutch.
Males are not permitted to remain in close proximity to the nesting site, and instead stay on the outskirts, defending the area from other species.
✦ Parenthood - 2 Females
In bonded pairs, both females act as mothers to offspring, regardless of who laid the eggs. Offspring from both nests - if applicable - are pooled together, forming a single creche, with cubs free to move between both nests.
Females rotate duties between guarding, resting, grooming offspring, and escorting cubs to water once mobile. Both females take it in turns to separate from their young and fetch resources, ensuring the cubs are rarely left alone.
Should females be required to join the rest of the pack to hunt, young may either be stashed in the nest and made to wait, or permitted to accompany the females a short distance, depending on their age and confidence.
Parenthood is significantly less stressful for bonded female pairs. Both mothers show great affection and tenderness to their young, and frequently coo at and preen their feathers gently.
✦ Parenthood - 1 Female
Solitary females are capable of raising offspring alone, but experience considerably higher stress levels throughout nesting and early cub rearing.
Solitary mothers spend significantly less time with their offspring than bonded females. They leave the nest frequently and for extended periods, often to feed, patrol, or simply distance themselves from the stress of constant care.
Cubs raised by a single female are frequently left unattended, resulting in heightened distress and increased vocalization when she returns - adding to her stress levels.
This stress manifests itself in inconsistent care. Solitary mothers may alternate between brief bouts of affection and long periods of outright avoidance, ignoring calls or physically distancing themselves from their young.
If stress levels are extreme, solitary mothers may abandon their young entirely, snapping at them should they follow her, and permitting males to interact with them.
Cubs abandoned by their mother are immediately considered orphaned, and trigger orphan behavior patterns if they survive.
✦ Parenthood - Males
Lacking nurturing instincts, males play no role in rearing cubs, but will still assist females by bringing them food as needed.
Males are not permitted to interact with or get near to the nesting site or cubs below Adolescent, and are met with fierce aggression from the female(s) if they attempt to do so.
While male Alioramus lack any nurturing instinct, they are intensely curious about cubs. As young begin to stray from their mothers at Adolescent, males - now finally permitted to do so - investigate them with interest.
Male interactions with offspring - including Adolescents - are often rough and poorly moderated, with cubs squeaking and rushing back to their mothers when bitten too harshly, or chased for too long.
This is quickly moderated by the females, who intercept any behavior like this and reprimand the male.
✦ Offspring Maturity
Once cubs are Adolescent, females permit males to come closer and interact with cubs, though keep a watchful eye over them.
Adolescent females gradually have affection from their mothers reduced, preparing them for when they reach maturity.
As cubs reach Sub Adult, clear sex based divergence occurs.
Females are driven out of the pack by both mothers in a series of warning calls and aggressive stances - a situation that escalates to biting and attacking if the warnings are not heeded.
An exception occurs when a mother lacks a bonded partner. Should she be alone, a single daughter may be permitted to remain as her lifelong bonded companion.
Males, however, are permitted to remain within the pack long term, provided space allows. Sub Adult males gradually integrate into male hierarchy through sparring and social testing, but start out at the bottom of the pack's rankings.
If there is not enough space, the other males drive the newly aging Sub Adults out. They may be permitted to trail behind the pack for a while, but are not allowed within the group.
✦ Adoption
Adoption is practised only by females.
Bonded pairs readily adopt orphaned cubs - with or without offspring of their own - but solitary females never do so.
Orphaned cubs are often timid and require coaxing, something which is usually achieved through offering scraps of meat to win them over.
Once cubs are adopted, they are treated no differently to biological offspring.

Elderly Alioramus are rare, but elderly females and female pairs are more common. They are less likely to initiate hunts, and allow the males to provide for them, showing less interest in nesting.
Elderly males move just as they did in their youth, but show higher exhaustion and less interest in nesting. This means that they might struggle with maintaining their role of dominance when males are constantly sparring with each other in order to impress. Additionally, their colors may fade.
✦ Death
If a female within a female pair is lost, the other female falls into a period of intense grief - going completely still and refusing to leave her body, staring down at it. She may call for her, nuzzling her in encouragement to rouse. She will not feed from the body of her fallen companion and will aggressively defend it from the other members of her pack, refusing to let them close.
Though males do not mourn as deeply as females, the loss of a familiar packmate - especially a brother, long-term companion, or female in the group - often results in short-lived agitation. Affected males become more aggressive, roam further afield - or disperse from the group entirely in search of others.

Profile - Quick View
Alioramus are Medium Diurnal Terrestrial Carnivores.
Alioramus can group up to 8 sub/adults, unlimited offspring, unlimited adoptees. Only females with a bonded female companion adopt. Females without a female partner and males do not adopt offspring.
Alioramus are tolerant.
Alioramus can have up to 5 in an engagement or hunting party.
Alioramus may hunt up to Apex tier when in a full hunting party of 5. Otherwise, Alioramus may hunt up to Large tier. Alioramus must prioritize Medium tier or lower if multiple prey options are within LOS, and cannot hunt Apex carnivores, or sauropods.
Alioramus require a mixed sex pack for ultimate stability within a group - a bonded female pair provides a calming influence, soothing the pack's harmony, while males provide considerable strength and assertiveness to help a group's success.
Alioramus stalk herds and travel over long distances, keen to learn migration patterns and discover new hunting opportunities.
Alioramus run down their prey, utilizing tag-teaming tactics to alternate their attacks on their victims, ensuring that they dart out of harm's way after landing a vicious bite.
Alioramus avoid Apex carnivores.
