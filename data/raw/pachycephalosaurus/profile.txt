Pachycephalosaurus
    "Pachy", "Pachycep"
Pachycephalosaurus are small terrestrial herbivores that thrive on the mountainous regions of Gondwa. Congregating in large groups, females socialize in personality specific hierarchies while males challenge for rights to all the females. Though confident when faced with danger, Pachycephalosaurus are inherently flighty on open terrain and retreat to perched rocks, cliffs and caves when threatened.

✦ Offspring Behavior (Nested)
Hatchling & Juvenile
Baby Pachycephalosaurus which are between the growth stage of hatchling and yearling are known as kids. Kids hatch in a concealed nest away from prying eyes and other Pachycephalosaurus. Usually, their start to the world is in turbulence if their mothers nesting spot is challenged by another nanny.
As soon as they hatch, kids chirp, rumble and call loudly for their mothers attention. Responding in turn, she nudges them to their feet, almost pushing them out the nest. Kids follow their mother loyally, calling to her and sticking close.
When their mother leaps out of reach, kids panic and attempt to follow in vain, crying out at the last spot she stood. Experienced mothers know to lead her kids along cliffs they can follow without being separated, but first time mothers take time to understand why their offspring do not follow her.
A young Pachycephalosaurus's early life consists of loudly calling to their mother and following her as she leads them to food and water. Offspring do not bond with one another in their early lives - their entire focus is staying close to the nanny who hatched them or adopted them.
Offspring Pachycephalosaurus will attempt to eat everything they are led to. Their bellies are designed to digest specific food types, but their minds do not comprehend what's acceptable food and what is not. Anything inedible will come right back up and the youngling will quickly learn which food types satiate them and which do not.
Adolescent
Around adolescence, Pachycephalosaurus are given the full freedom and encouragement to move around as they please and are introduced to other kids in the band.
Both males and females play through butting heads and leaping after one another. Kids begin to form bonds as their personalities start to show - some take a liking to a particular individual and follow them around, mimicking their behavior, while others are independent spirits and explore their immediate vicinity with confidence and interest.
Nannies virtually lose all interest in their offspring around this time, and happily teach any kid who dotes on them.
From early adolescence, to late yearling, Pachycephalosaurus begin to settle into their personalities. As a species that matures slower, a kid may take longer to mature than their peers. The time varies based on personality. See General Behavior for more.
 
Yearling Behavior (Wild Spawn & Nested)
Pachycephalosaurus grow to their adult size fairly quick, but mentally mature much slower than their bodies. Before transitioning into full maturity, Pachycephalosaurus kids experience a short time as yearlings before becoming fully independent and shedding into their adult coloration. 
🌟 After spawning as a new Pachycephalosaurus, players must first pick the DR Yearling skin. They must keep this skin equipped until they complete their full adult growth stage. Only after fully grown, may the player shed into another skin. It is optional to shed out of the yearling skin once reaching full adult - you may stay as a yearling for longer than the required time.
Like awkward teenagers, yearlings are paired with the carefree bliss of a juvenile and the lanky body of a fully grown Pachycephalosaurus. Their days are spent learning, following behind adults and tussling with other yearlings.
Reaction to aggression: While some may be boisterous and demanding, a corrective attack from an adult frightens even confident yearlings. Recognizing their imminent danger, yearlings opt to scurry out of harm's away, quick to cry for help and seek comfort from another reliable adult they are familiar with.
Predator reaction: Whether in a group or alone, yearling Pachycephalosaurus always flee from threats when attacked. A yearling might take part in initial aggressive displays, but the moment the interaction becomes physical, yearlings abandon their once confident guise and flee - often crying out during their escape.
Yearlings who grow up in isolation are eager for socialization and are always accepted by any Pachycephalosaurus they encounter - even lone males, who mistake them for adult females.
✦ Orphaned Behavior (Wild Spawn)
Without the attention of a mother, orphaned kids will pester another Pachycephalosaurus nearby in hopes of being adopted.
If none are in the vicinity, the orphan will call out in hopes of being noticed. When the pangs of hunger grow unavoidable, the orphaned kid is forced to roam in search of food.
Instinctively, kids will try to eat anything they can carry. It's during these early days of survival where an orphan will learn what food types are filling and what are not.

Pachycephalosaurus live a primarily diurnal lifestyle, active during the day when foraging for food, or traveling to new feeding grounds or courting.
Cautious and alert, Pachycephalosaurus frequently scan their surroundings. When threatened, they prefer to flee vertically - climbing swiftly to elevations that provide safety.
Raised rocks, cliffs and cave structures are their ideal perches and once atop or within the safety of these areas, Pachycephalosaurus are fiercely territorial and readily push away anything that investigates closer.
Horn-scraping, stretching and trotting along migratory paths are common daily rituals that maintain hygiene and muscle tone. Even solitary individuals display a strong awareness of escape routes and rarely stray far from defensible terrain.
✦ Downtime Behavior
At night they retreat to safety - ideally a raised structure built of rock still warm from the morning sun. Using these elevated areas for protection, Pachycephalosaurus sleep either together or separately, with followers and leaders resting in their own little cliques.
The buck of the group never has allies in a band and sleeps separately from his females, usually in quick bursts atop the tallest rock for a better view of the immediate area. His position in the band is challenged relentlessly, and night time is the only time he can rest unchallenged.
When not facing off rivals, his sleep is disturbed by every rustle in the trees or crunch of twigs. His keen senses alert his females of incoming danger, should the group be approached.
Rival males or solitary females tend to linger on the outskirts of these band groupings. At night, females, yearlings and orphaned kids approach quietly and clamber up to where the band sleeps, hoping for the safety of the group in this time of rest.
Rival males, knowledgeable of the risks when approaching a band off guard, rest on nearby cliffs and wait till day before approaching.
If no Pachycephalosaurus are in the area, they are known to rest nearby other herbivore species, perhaps to use these larger animals as temporary warmth and protection while they rest.
 
✦ Personality Type
Pachycephalosaurus, despite their quick growth, mature extremely slowly. Even when the physical size of an adult, a yearling will only fully mature much later.
It's during this age that their personalities begin to fully emerge as they navigate the shifting hierarchy of Pachycephalosaurus social structures.
A yearling's personality fits within 2 categories: a leader or a follower, which typically shifts throughout their life, even after fully maturing or progressing into their elder years.
Leaders are pushy, loud and entitled. They are outgoing, social and assert themselves at the top of the social hierarchy through games of play and consistent chattering with the group. They are followed by those who take a liking to them, and have learnt through engaging with others that some are better off leading the band while others are suited as supportive followers. 
Pachycephalosaurus with this personality type are the first to investigate a new thing or accept a new female member into the band. Whether it be an object or a species, the leaders will assess the possible danger first.
They do this by circling the new thing, watching carefully and waiting for it to move. If it's deemed safe, leaders will call out to their band to come see. Some followers might not heed the invitation and will watch from a safe distance.
Leaders, being the more pushy individuals, take the first pick of food, drink, and resting spots and often demand space when entering an area. They seemingly expect followers to make way and will bully others to assert themselves.
Followers are peaceful and cooperative. These individuals are perhaps not as social or outgoing and happily exist at the bottom of the pecking order. Their personality is better fit as companions to outgoing Pachycephalosaurus. They spend their time resting beside other followers, finding food and following those they take a liking to. 
When cornered or approached by an unfamiliar or untrusted face, followers will sit and rumble :danger_call:  to ward away any advances. This odd posturing display will always be respected by the more outgoing individual. Over time, a shy Pachycephalosaurus can learn to be more trusting and may grow beyond their rank.
✦ Non-Dimorphic Males
Joining a band
Non-dimorphic males, lacking the bright coloration of males, are subdued due to their mutations, and infertile. These males have no desire to breed, or assert themselves within band groupings. Instead, they approach a band purely for the protection a group provides.
Approaching a band, the non-dimorphic male calls softly and, being perceived as a female by other Pachycephalosaurus, are accepted in without hesitation or fuss.
Behavior within the band
Non-dimorphic males display the personality type of followers. Happy to just be part of the group and avoid conflict where possible. Unbothered, they do not feel the instinctual desire to challenge the residing buck, push away rivals or partake in courtship. If aggressed by a leader-type female, these non-dimorphic males simply chirp :danger_call:and move away. Avoiding conflict and attempting to remain a peaceful member of the band.

✦ Solitary Behavior
Solitary Pachycephalosaurus females instinctively crave the companionship of their own kind and even if not particularly friendly, will always prefer to exist within a band than live on their own.
For males, however, solitude is a forced reality. Once their adult colors begin to show, the band rejects them, driven off by the residing buck or pressured by the females to leave.
From that moment on, his life becomes a quiet pursuit. He lingers on the outskirts of the group, far enough to avoid confrontation but always near enough to watch, wait, and follow. His goal is singular: to challenge the buck and replace him as the bands adult male.
Occasionally, he crosses paths with yearlings - too young yet to show their true color dimorphism. Unaware of their developing maturity, he mistakes them for females.
When that happens, he lowers his posture and issues a soft, friendly call. If they accept, they're welcomed as band members. They remain unaware of the mistaken identity until time reveals the truth.
It's only when the yearling's adult markings emerge that the once-accepting buck now sees a rival, and quiet companionship is replaced with instinctive rejection.
✦ Band Grouping
Pachycephalosaurus females form loosely structured social groups known as bands. These gatherings can include a mix of adult females, juveniles, adoptees, and yearlings.
Rather than being led by a single dominant individual, the group follows a soft hierarchy shaped by personality -particularly those referred to as “leaders.”
Leader Social Behavior
Leaders tend to be the most outgoing and proactive members of the band. They seek out other leaders to sleep beside during the night, and when active, will be the first to encounter a new member of the group. 
Sometimes bands have one leader, sometimes many. As a species that breeds often, bands are in a constant state of change.
Yearlings can have outgoing personalities which move them up to leaders as they mature, while others will remain as followers until they have settled enough to be more assertive and can defend their space from more confident or skilled leaders.
Follower Social Behavior
Comfortable in their lower-ranking role, followers avoid confrontation and are quick to yield when approached by a more dominant individual. If another draws near, the follower will quietly rise, offer a soft chirp in acknowledgment, and move aside.
During migrations, followers tend to trail closely behind a confident individual. They don't take the lead, but their quiet loyalty is unmistakable. They rely on their chosen leader to decide when and where to move.
When under stress, whether from predators or aggressive herbivores, more reserved Pachycephalosaurus usually hold back, waiting to see how their leader reacts before responding themselves.
The constant need to relocate and avoid conflict can be stressful, so shy individuals often seek comfort by resting alongside other quiet companions. If their approach is rejected and the other moves away, followers respect the boundary and soothe themselves by shaking their bodies, a calming gesture.
Patience often wins out, and followers tend to warm up over time, especially once the band has settled into a new safe spot. They seek out familiar faces to share warmth during rest.
As yearlings mature, many maintain their follower tendencies, though some gradually become more assertive and take on leadership roles. It's common for this transition to occur sometime after reaching adulthood, while others retain a quieter nature well into their elder years.
Bild
Disputes between females, typically between those with the "leader" personality,  often escalate into physical scuffles.
A well-aimed kick may send a rival stumbling, while a headbutt punishes those who linger too close. Often, being knocked from a ledge is enough humiliation to make a challenger retreat. However, some are too proud to accept defeat and will return to fight again.
Fights between females end once one reaches death scars. At this point, a 30-minute cooldown is applied to both individuals.
No other females may issue a challenge to the winner while this timer is active.
The Residing Buck
Just as females can have personalities, so too does the buck within the band.
Some bucks prefer to avoid conflict and follow the same personality type as followers. 
Non-dimorphic males always avoid conflict and never partake in challenges.
Others burst with the same fiery confidence of leaders and even stand up to females who bully them. A buck who initiates and defends themselves from spot challenges by leader-type females engage in 1 on 1 fights. A 30-minute cooldown is applied to both individuals who took part.
Male Challenges 
Rival males  wait for the right opportunity to challenge the current buck for his position in the Band. When the Band has found a suitable rock or cliff to rest on - the challenger will make his move (caves are never suitable locations for buck challenges, and the challenger will wait for the right opportunity before approaching).
When ready, the challenger approaches with purpose, bellowing loudly three or more times to announce his intent.
The current buck will never leave his spot on the cliff or rock to approach the challenger, and always stands his ground until his rival is close enough to hit. Nearby females move away to avoid being caught in the challenge.
The duel is physical and intense. The goal is simple: to knock the other off the ledge, and inflict significant damage. The first male to reach death scars must yield.
Victory is announced with a triumphant scream, while the loser must retreat 10 body lengths from the group. If the defeated male refuses to leave, the females themselves will step in to drive him off. (The loser must be removed from the in-game group, and the victor added immediately.)
Females do not form lasting bonds with adult males and never intervene in male challenges. However, if a defeated buck remains too close after a contest (within 10 body lengths), they will not hesitate to chase him off and enforce group boundaries. (removing the loser from the group, and chasing them away if they return on a timer).
Yearling males outgrowing their baby skin and entering their adult phase face the same rejection. As soon as their mature coloration begins to show, they are exiled, even by their own mothers. These maturing males are chased from the band to begin life as solitary bucks. (Yearlings shedding into male coloration must be kicked from the group.)

Pachycephalosaurus are naturally curious and often trail behind other herbivores, keeping a cautious distance.
They show interest in movement and activity, but never attempt to join or bond with other species. Their involvement is passive - observing from afar without seeking contact or offering assistance.
If confronted or attacked, they retreat without resistance -  unless claiming a territory.
Even when alone, Pachycephalosaurus may follow other herbivores purely out of boredom or curiosity. But despite their presence, they remain outsiders - never integrating into the social structure of other herds.
Only at night, when isolation feels most vulnerable, will a solitary Pachycephalosaurus break this pattern. Drawn to the safety of a sleeping herd, it may quietly settle near a resting herbivore, using their bulk as a shield against the dark.
If met with aggression or rejection, the Pachycephalosaurus does not retaliate. Instead, it moves off respectfully, continuing its search for warmth and safety elsewhere.
Bild
When confronted by a predator, Pachycephalosaurus insinctively respond with aggresive displays if given enough time to react. As a species well adapted to steep cliffs and towering rocks, being caught on flat ground can sometimes be a death sentance. 
When bitten, Pachycephalosaurus react by kicking or charging to force the predator away, and open up an opportunity for them to flee to a more defendable position. A raised rock, cliff, or the safety of a cave is ideal.
Once secure, their posture shifts from prey to aggressor. With renewed confidence, Pachycephalosaurus bellow loudly, smacking their skulls against stone in a noisy show of force, aimed to ward off further pursuit. Should the predator approach the chosen spot, Pachycephalosaurus leap from their perch to drive the predator away, using the raised terrain to their advantage.
Knowing their skill on this type of challenging terrain, Pachycephalosaurus have adapted to battering attacks aimed to knock the predator off their perch.
When ambushed by predators, yearlings often lag behind. Naive and prone to wandering, they rarely grasp the danger until it's too close. They are the last to scramble to safety - only fleeing once attacked or bitten by the predator - and rush to nearby cliffs or rocks for protection.
Offspring and yearlings are the most vocal when in danger, and squeal and call out in distress. They do not partake in physical attacks -  and never defend themselves when targeted and bitten/attacked. Instead, they flee for cover or hide behind older group members - freezing or fleeing depending on the space available.

✦ Courtship
Females have no favorite buck and do not form bonds with adult males. To females, a buck's role is simply there to breed with and protect them from hungry predators.
Should a buck manage to best a rival and take control over the band, he either courts with females without young, or watches over the band protectively. 
Courting is a submissive demonstration. Crouching in front of a chosen female, the male coos softly and stretches his neck forward, hoping to impress her with this act of tenderness.
If impressed enough to pair with him, she will sit down and call softly in return.
✦ Nesting
Once arriving at a favorable cliff, the nesting female isolates herself on a ledge of her choosing.
Nesting females and females with young are referred to as "nannies".  If the nanny is a leader, her presence alone typically frightens residing females off the ledge that she approaches. If a female doesn't move, the nanny will challenge for the right to nest on that spot.
Once a ledge or spot in a cave is secured, the nanny will construct her nest. Decorations of these nests vary from female to female - but a common structure is seen amongst nannies when there are more than one in the band.
To protect her young from stray females looking to climb the social rankings, a nesting nanny will cover her nest with large objects to conceal her eggs from view.
✦ Parenthood
When offspring, known as kids, hatch, they rumble and call to their mother. She immediately relocates them to a more defendable spot if her chosen nest location is compromised.
Her young follow her closely, recognizing their own vulnerability when unable to leap as high as their mother. Following her, they are led away from other nesting nannies and back towards the nest for food when hungry. Once reaching adolescence, nannies pool their offspring together and co-parent young as a group.
The buck has no desire or patience to deal with offspring. He does not contribute at all to raising young. His priority lies in chasing away rival males and winning the respect of females.
If a buck finds himself in a situation where the mother of a kid has died, he will chase the kid relentlessly, attempting to drive it out the band. If no female adopts the orphan, the kid is exiled (removing them from the group).
Only young that are larger in size avoid harsh treatment from adult bucks.Yearlings are tolerated by males and treated as adult members of the group.
Yearling males are always mistaken for being female and will be accepted into a Band with any buck nearby.
✦ Offspring Maturity
It's only when yearlings mature (shed out of the yearling skin) is the understanding made by the adult male and the youngling is promptly exiled from the Band (removed from the group immediately and aggressed away by all adults).
Even females will participate in shunning these young males, the behavior always initiated by a lead and supported by followers. Females do not permit her sons to remain within the band to avoid inbreeding.

Elderly Behavior is mandatory for females, but optional for males.
The likelihood of a Pachycephalosaurus surviving beyond 20 clutches is slim, but those who do grow demanding in their twilight years, hardened by experience and loss.
Scarred, deliberate, and respected, these elders no longer yield - they embody the authority of a leader, whether or not the role was once theirs in their youth.
✦ Death
Given their cliff-dwelling, hill-scaling lifestyle, uncoordinated individuals may misstep on steep terrain or unstable rock.
A bad fall - especially during territorial disputes or predator chases - can result in fatal fractures or internal injuries. A loss of stamina or failure to reach high ground in time leaves them vulnerable to predators, particularly at night when vision and response time falter.
Solitary individuals, or those pushed from groups, face a much higher risk of being picked off without the protection of the band.
Yearlings are the most vulnerable - unless they are skilled jumpers, they often fall victim to predation if they cannot flee to safety in time.

Profile - Quick View
Pachycephalosaurus are Small Diurnal Terrestrial Herbivores.
Pachycephalosaurus are sedentary, but also territorial over cliff sides.
Pachycephalosaurus can group in unlimited adults, unlimited yearlings, unlimited offspring, unlimited adoptees. These groups are known as bands and can contain a maximum of 1 adult male, not including non-dimorphic males, which are treated as female.
Pachycephalosaurus are defensive and readily defend their small territory, which consists of an elevated rock, a cliff, or cave structure.
Pachycephalosaurus can have unlimited adults in an engagement. Offspring and Yearlings cannot participate, and must flee.
Though nomadic, Pachycephalosaurus rely on raised rocks for defense in challenges and predator attack. This profile has adjusted stats to better situate Pachycephalosaurus as a rock jumper.
Pachycephalosaurus have a unique growth stage in between Sub-Adult and Adult known as "Yearling". This is the Adult stage in game, but with the Yearling skin. Pachycephalosaurus mature slightly later than other species, and spend additional time as a Yearling before fully maturing.
