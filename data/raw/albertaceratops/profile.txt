Albertaceratops
Albertaceratops was a fascinating species that roamed North America during the Late Cretaceous period, around 76 million years ago. A member of the ceratopsid family, this herbivorous dinosaur is known for its distinctive frill and unique horn arrangement, which set it apart from its close relatives. With its robust build and specialized teeth, Albertaceratops was well-adapted for grazing on the lush vegetation of its environment, making it an intriguing subject for paleontologists studying dinosaur evolution and diversity!

Overview
Details
Medium Cathermal Terrestrial Herbivore
Albertaceratops form promiscuous pairbonds.
Albertaceratops are nomadic but claim small territories in their courting grounds around nests.
Albertaceratops have a tolerant nature unless cornered, ignored or with young - where they become aggressive.
Albertaceratops may group with: Pachyrhinosaurus, Nasutoceratops, Styracosaurus and Psittacosaurus.
Albertaceratops may defend these species.
Albertaceratops shun albino, albino-appearing, melanistic, and melanistic-appearing individuals from their groups. This includes other species grouping.

Group Limits (In-Game Group)
Unlimited Sub/Adults, led by a dominant female and a dominant male
Unlimited Offspring
Unlimited Adoptees (including other ceratopsian adoptees under subadult)

This group is known as a sett.

Engagement Limits (Defence & Hunts)
Predator Reaction: ⁨Defensive⁩ / ⁨Headstrong⁩
Unlimited Albertaceratops in the same sett may form a defensive circle or flee. 
They stand their ground in the circle, but can move as far as 1 body length forward from the circle to land a hit, before resuming the formation.
Up to 4 Albertaceratops may actively engage threats outside of the formation, which include chasing down to land hits - but not further than line of sight of the herd. 
These 4 defenders may chase the predator even after the predator forfeits until they leave line of sight of the herd.
Other species they group with may be defended inside the protective circle, or accepted to join the formation.
Psittacosaurus of any age may be actively defended.

When no young are present: Albertaceratops flee from Apex predators and move away from other ungrouped herbivore species if contested off food bushes.

When offspring (including other ceratopsian offspring) and adoptees are in the group: Albertaceratops are aggressive and capable of defending themselves and their herd from all carnivore and herbivore threats. Additionally, Albertaceratops are possessive and the entire sett may claim bushes to push rival herbivore species off their chosen food.

Albino and melanistic individuals are never defended by the herd.

Preferred Diet
Flowers, nuts, roots, fruit.

Nesting Limits
🌧️﻿ Rainy Season: 3 🥚﻿ per 2 hours.
☀️﻿ Dry Season: 2 🥚﻿ per 2 hours.
Albertaceratops nest with other females communally in woodlands and grasslands, with their nests placed together to create a cove in their preferred POIs. Their nests are constructed out of reeds, branches and roots.
Albertaceratops coves have different appearances in the dry and rainy season, see General Behavior.
Albertaceratops do not form same-sex pairs, but may adopt and protect species in their herding list (see Predator Reaction and Cross-Species Interaction).

Physical Dimorphism
♀️ Females, known as sows, are muted and dull in color. 
Sows who are nested in are more likely to hatch with the same skin and colorations as their mothers - an added perk which may help her win favor with the dominant female if she is her daughter.
♂️ Males, known as boars, have bright and contrasting colors around their crests/bodies.
Albertaceratops dimorphism is not mandatory, however individuals differing from the typical dimorphism may face difficulties during courtship.
 
Growing Up
Offspring Behavior
Young Albertaceratops, referred to as cubs, are born weak and vulnerable. They spend their first moments lying in the nest, waiting for their clutchmates to hatch.
The chirping sounds of nearby cubs pique their curiosity, prompting them to stand on shaky legs and explore. Excitedly, they venture out of their nest, greeted warmly by nearby sows resting near their own nests, celebrating the arrival of the new generation.
The dominant female, perched on her elaborately decorated nest, is often the first to receive visits from the newborns. Cubs eagerly investigate her trinkets and play with other hatched young nearby.
Cubs exhibit different behaviors based on gender. Young sows engage in playful chases and friendly calls, often gravitating toward the dominant female and other nearby sows. Young boars, on the other hand, are boisterous and engage in mock fights, instinctively butting their crests together. If a young sow is accidentally included in these rough games, her surprised cries prompt a swift response from the dominant sow. With a firm snort, she reprimands the offending boar, teaching him his place within the group. The chastised boar, undeterred, redirects his energy to playing with other boars while carefully avoiding sows who might land him in further trouble.
Adolescence is marked by the shedding of juvenile skin. Young sows often mirror their mother’s coloration, becoming near replicas. The dominant female shows particular affection toward these daughters, allowing them to rest close to her during naps and forming strong, lasting bonds. These look-alike daughters are more likely to step into leadership roles in her absence, earning the respect of the herd due to their shared appearance with the dominant female.
Even as offspring reach early adulthood, their mothers continue to watch over them. Mothers guide them to food and water and provide support, especially as young females prepare for their very first clutch. Related Albertaceratops females maintain the strongest bonds, creating a tightly knit social structure within the herd.
Orphaned Behavior
Orphaned cubs instinctively seek out the dominant female or another ceratopsian species to fill the void left by their missing maternal figures. They linger near these individuals, mimicking the behavior of other cubs to blend in as seamlessly as possible.
Sows within the sett are known for their nurturing nature and will readily accept orphaned cubs that approach them, integrating them into their own broods or treating them as their own. Even offspring of mixed-species herd mates are warmly welcomed and fiercely protected by the sows until they are mature enough to survive independently.
Orphans raised without the guidance of a social structure often grow into dependent adults, displaying submissive behavior in their quest to be accepted into a new sett’s hierarchy.
Orphan boars frequently miss out on early breeding opportunities, as their lack of resolve makes them less successful in courtship battles. They often go too far in battle, resulting in reprimands or fatal wounds.
In contrast, orphaned females gravitate toward the dominant sow, eager to support her and earn her approval, reinforcing their place within the social fabric of the herd.
General Behavior
Dry season behavior:
Albertaceratops behavior shifts with the seasons, becoming more defensive and protective during the dry season. The scorching heat and shrinking water sources drive the sett to live closer to rivers.
Areas with large, overturned roots and mud pits become preferred nesting grounds, as they offer shade and relief from the heat. Young cubs, lacking the thick, protective skin of adults, depend on wallowing in shallow water and resting in mud pits to stay cool.
Coves are constructed from dried bushes and trees, often built around or within mud patches. During the cooler evening hours, the entire sett rests together in these puddles, taking advantage of the lower temperatures during their downtime.
The dry season also brings out more aggressive behavior, particularly among sows who initiate possession over food sources.
It's common for the entire sett to form a protective circle around a food bush, using their numbers and intimidating displays to drive off competing herbivores and secure the resources for themselves. This ensures the dominant boar, sow, and offspring have priority access to food, while the rest of the sett stands guard.
When grouped with other ceratopsians, Albertaceratops may still display this pushy behavior, especially if they outnumber the other species. In larger herds, this assertiveness can extend towards non-threatening species, with enough members ensuring success in controlling resources.

Rainy season behavior:
The rainy season triggers increased movement within Albertaceratops herds. Both boars and sows migrate farther inland, venturing into areas that are typically barren during the dry months in search of fertile, resource-rich regions suitable for courtship.
Coves during this season are made from lush reeds and tall grasses, with vibrant ferns often sprouting around the nests.
Unlike the dry season, branches are rarely used in nest construction. Instead, the focus shifts to thick vegetation for added concealment, as the risk of predation is higher during this time.
Social Interaction
Hierarchy within the sett:
Albertaceratops groups are called a sett and are led by a dominant sow and a dominant boar. The dominant boar is always a fit breeding partner chosen by the dominant sow.
Rivalry between boars earns the dominant boar rights to the dominant female, and her choice in him is his greatest accomplishment.

The Dominant Sow
The dominant sow is the driving support of the sett. Her age and experience guide the group within their complex social hierarchy.
Adorned with scars, she is well respected by sows and seen as the matriarch of Albertaceratops groups. She chooses where the nesting cove will be placed and teaches other sows and young how to forage, where to migrate, and which males are best suited for breeding.

 How a dominant sow is chosen 
In a new sett, where the sows' social hierarchy is not yet established: 
The dominant sow is decided by whoever has the most allies and is the strongest in the sett. This female is naturally confident, pushing other sows off food and showing clear leadership skills during migration.
In a perfect grouping environment, her pushy nature is supported by her close allies and look-alike offspring who help reinforce her position in the pecking order by submitting to her more dominant behavior.
They show submissive headshakes and heed her decisions to eat first, migrate now, sleep here, or nest in this spot. The reinforcement from sows helps the dominant sow claim her position in a new herd.
Similar sows with the same appearance as the dominant sow can lead other herd members to mistake them for her, which helps establish respect within the group.

Challenges for the dominant sow position
Sows refusing to submit to the dominant sow will stand their ground and fight back. With a single smack on their rival, the challenging dominants will square up and roar in defiance at each other.
Both sides are supported by allies who join the intimidation displays of the sow they respect most. Even boars will pick a female, as this show of support increases their chances of being chosen as the dominant boar - should their chosen female win.
The winner of this confrontation is whichever sow has the most allies. Once sides have been chosen, the loser is forced to turn away, signaling defeat.
If both sides have equal allies or a losing dominant sow refuses to remain with the winner, the dominant sows will disband their group and start a new sett with their allies, often bringing other groupmates fond of their company.
With two separate sett groups, each considers the other a rival and will push them off food bushes.

Sows
Within the sett, sows may bicker over food and resting spots, but once a dominant female emerges, she enforces group order.
In small setts with only one or two sows, a dominant sow often does not exist, as both females rely heavily on each other for companionship and protection.
Bickering is only common in larger setts, where multiple females feel they deserve respect.
 
New sows joining the group 
New sows joining the group and offspring joining the sett immediately rank lowest in the social hierarchy.
They earn favor by befriending other sows and staying on the good side of the dominant sow.

The Dominant boar
The dominant boar earns his rank by competing with other boars in mock fights and dominance displays to prove his skill and earn respect.
Only the choice of a dominant female solidifies the dominant boar's rank.
The dominant sow chooses the dominant boar based on his prowess - boars that win a lot of engagements are highly attractive. Once chosen, she affectionately calls him to her side to lead the sett on their migrations.
The dominant boar uniquely aids the rest of the sett in adoption of orphaned offspring. If the sett encounters an orphan - or worse, loses a member that has offspring - the dominant boar steps forward to welcome the young, and aids to pair them with a suitable sow as a surrogate mother.

Boars 
All other boars - alongside the other sows - walk behind the dominant pair during migrations.
Though they don't contribute significantly to the group's leadership, boars play key roles in guarding the herd during migrations and rest hours.
Boars eager to rise in the sett will compete with those not currently on a courting timer.
Boars often wait until a nesting ground is reached to challenge for courtship rights or when rival dominant females contest the top position, as new female leadership can increase their chances.

Solitary behavior:
Solitary Albertaceratops behave similarly whether grouped in a sett, among other ceratopsians, or alone.
Albertaceratops with mutations often face solitude from an early age, either hatching from abandoned nests or being shunned from their herds.
When alone, their aggression toward threats remains the same, with individuals weighing the risks of standing their ground or chasing predators. Orphaned offspring from other herds are often accepted by solitary Albertaceratops, who value the company and responsibility. If other species nest nearby, a solitary Albertaceratops sow may instinctively create a cove to shelter hatchlings.
 
Aggressive Interaction
Play fighting between boars
Boars frequently bicker while under the watchful guidance of the dominant boar, who spends most of his time resting with the sows.
Boars are quick to initiate a play fight, testing each other's strength to determine who is better.
The dominant boar, secure in his rank, never participates in these mock fights. His vitality and energy are reserved for protecting the sett.
Oftentimes, play fights between other boars go too far, with individuals continuing to battle beyond safe health levels. During these moments, the dominant boar steps in between them, deterring further hits and diffusing the situation with a head shake. The winner of the contest is the one with the least injury, while the loser respectfully falls into place behind the winner during travels.
In smaller setts lacking a dominant boar, it is often the dominant sow who steps in to reinforce these rules.

Dominant pair reinforcing behavior
Besides disallowing lower-ranking boars from walking in front of the dominant boar, the pecking order also establishes who gets permission to assist the dominant boar in defending the sett from threats and challenging rival herbivores over bushes.
For all inner conflicts, the dominant pair resolves most issues, with preference given to the dominant boar.
If a sow becomes too aggressive and demanding, the dominant sow will step in to correct the behavior. 
If the situation escalates into aggressive calling or a smack, the dominant sow's allies will rush to her side to push the aggressive sow aside, forcing her to recognize she is outnumbered and should back down or risk being shunned.
 
Cross-Species Interaction
Albertaceratops act as sentries when herded with Pachyrhinosaurus, Styracosaurus, and Psittacosaurus. The dominant pair take turns standing guard over their sleeping herd mates during the night and watching over the young while their parents rest.
Other boars assist the pair in sentry duty, taking it in turns so that no more than 3 Albertaceratops are keeping watch at any one time.
They are often the first to spot a threat and alert their herd to the intruder. Albertaceratops protect all individuals that they herd with, and form cross-species defensive circles.
Young cubs see playmates in other herd mates and readily run headfirst toward the species, wanting to chase and play with them. This play continues until the Albertaceratops get tired or its mother or the dominant boar calls them back to the sett.
Albertaceratops are aggressive toward scavengers.
 
Predator Reaction
Albertaceratops are a bold species, quick to announce the presence of any carnivores in their vicinity. When a predator is spotted, the first individual to notice alerts the herd with a broadcast call, prompting other sett members to turn toward the threat and echo the warning.
In mixed-species herds, Albertaceratops are usually the first to announce a threat. With better vision than their herd mates, they can spot the shape of a carnivore from some distance away.
Once the alarm is raised, most individuals group together, bunching closely if young are present or standing nearby loosely for smaller predators.
A group of up to 4 individuals, which always includes the dominant sow & boar, move to the outer edges of the herd to act as a buffer between the danger and the rest of the group, performing as active defenders.
If the predator draws closer, the active defenders instinctively mock charge toward it to intimidate and drive the predator away from hunting the sett. 
Issuing warning calls, they attempt to draw attention to themselves in an effort to distract the predator from their groupmates and young.
When outmatched, the dominant sow directs the rest of the herd to flee, prompting them to abandon their defensive stance and move away from the engagement.
As many as four skilled individuals create a barrier between the herd and the predator, charging around the outskirts, headbutting, and kicking to keep the danger at bay. This always includes the dominant pair.
Unlimited Albertaceratops form a defensive circle formation when faced by a predator or threat. Up to 4 Albertaceratops may actively chase predators while in LOS of their herd. When choosing to abandon the formation, the 4 defenders may still continue to chase down predators while the herd flees.
✦ Mixed Species Protection in times of danger
When traveling alongside Pachyrhinosaurus and Styracosaurus, Albertaceratops instinctively fall into a defensive formation when predators appear within 10 body lengths of themselves. They press shoulder to shoulder with their allies to form a defensive circle or line.
Any individual separated from the herd is dangerously vulnerable. These stragglers act on pure instinct, driven by a single purpose: reach the herd. When a predator takes interest, the separated flee relentlessly toward the safety of the formation, ignoring all else.
In times of danger, Pachyrhinosaurus, Albertaceratops, and Styracosaurus share a rare moment of a unified herd mindset. When threatened by predation, they respond as one, all movement and effort focused on regrouping and restoring the safety of the herds defensive formation.
A targeted individual may attempt to outrun the threat, but if cornered or forced into close quarters, it will lash out with horn and bulk, fighting only long enough to break free and continue its retreat to the herd.
Up to 4 adult individuals from any species may patrol beyond the defensive ring. These sentinels charge, chase, and aggressively drive predators away, pushing them back up to ten body lengths from the formation. The circle is never willingly broken and is only abandoned if a herd member falls.
Psittacosaurus, while valiant sentries, do not partake in engagements and seek shelter behind the defensive formation.
 
Courtship & Parenthood
Albertaceratops sows begin courtship by traveling to nesting grounds, where they work together to create a protected area for their future offspring.
Females place nests close together, forming a circular "cove," surrounded by thick bushes and grass. 
Even females who do not breed contribute to the cove, helping to support their herdmates and being taught by the dominant sow how to construct the herd's specific cove requirements.
The center of the cove is reserved for the dominant sow, who decorates her nest with trinkets as a visible symbol of her rank. Unlike the other nests, hers is exposed, signifying her status as the central figure.

While the sows work on the cove, boars patrol the exterior, warding off rival species who come too close.
The dominant sow is the only one permitted to nest with the dominant boar, and these two only nest with each other.
For other sows, only winning boars are considered fit and strong enough to sire the next generation.
These boars engage in courtship fights, with the dominant boar overseeing the battles and stepping in if a fight becomes too intense.

Dominance fights
Dominance fights occur at any time to establish rank or prove vitality in siring the next generation.
A confident boar will boldly approach a rival, broadcasting his challenge. 
If the rival ignores his display, he may be met with a sharp smack and an aggressive bellow.
Boars that choose to submit will perform a body shake, diffusing the tension and establishing their lower rank beneath the victorious dominant.
However, if the rival refuses to back down, he will respond with a roaring challenge and stomp the ground loudly, signaling the start of the confrontation.
The two boars begin circling one another, bellowing loudly and stomping the ground.
During this display, the dominant boar oversees the event from a safe distance near the sows.
Once both rivals are prepared, they face off, issuing a challenge broadcast. Following the aggression call, they charge at each other, colliding in a powerful clash. This sequence of circling, displays, and charges continues until one boar reaches death scars or the dominant bull intervenes to end the fight.
Courtship fights are not without risk. In smaller groups, challenges can result in the death of younger males with no self-preservation.
The dominant boar, experienced and wise, carefully manages his aggression. He intervenes when a boar shows visible injuries (bleeding death scars), signaling that the fight has escalated too far. In such cases, the victor is determined by which boar has the most remaining health.

Courtship
The dominant pair descend into the cove, with the dominant boar bringing the dominant sow gifts of food. Together, they lay over the  nest, keeping each other company and showing affection.
After eggs are successfully laid, the dominant boar retreats to the outside of the cove to monitor the other boars.
Only victorious boars - those who have won a dominance battle against another boar this nesting season - are permitted the right to approach the other sows.
The rest of the sows lay in wait in the cove, resting on top of their nests.
Boars enter, one at a time, bringing a gift of their own to grant to the sows. If a sow is receptive to an incoming boar, she will pick up the gift he drops by her, or turn away pointedly to reject him.
Sows can accept the advances of multiple boars and offer split paternity in their nests, if they wish.
Boars that wish to nest with multiple sows must leave the cove and return with multiple gifts - at least one for each sow he hopes to impress.
 
Parenthood
If their nesting cove is encroached by ungrouped herbivores or predators, the entire sett will defend their small territory around the cove and any food sources nearby.  Water is not defended or challenged for. 
These challenges are always initiated by the dominant boar and supported by the other boars.
In smaller setts, even the dominant sow and other sows will stand to rise, forming the protective circle formation around their cove.
The raising of the young is a communal effort by all the sows in the sett. Typically, smaller clutches are born, and the offspring are initially cold and fearful, hiding quietly until all their clutchmates have hatched.
The collective sounds of nearby cubs prompt the young to venture out of the nest and explore the cove. The dominant female’s nest is often the first to be inspected, with the cubs curiously playing with her nest items and nearby young. Sows remain lying down while the young play, content in the boars’ protection and happily calling to cubs as they interact.
When the time comes to move on, the dominant female stands and broadcasts a loud call to rally the cubs back to their mothers.
Leaving the cove, the dominant sows walks ahead, accompanied by her dominant boar. The young stay close to their mothers during the early stages of life, only separating once they shed their juvenile skins and transition into adulthood.
Cubs shedding into mutations (albinism & melanism) are promptly pushed out by their mothers and the dominant female.
Once rejected, the dominant boar intently inspects the shunned young, cautiously sniffing them over to assess the issue before shunning them himself, solidifying their exile from the sett.
 
Elderly & End of Life
Elderly Albertaceratops, marked by scars and the wear of age, often show signs of a more difficult temperament. The dominant female and male typically reach this stage of life more quickly, as the stress of constant leadership duties wears on them, and the harsh environments these medium-sized herbivores endure shorten their patience.
The dominant female, seasoned beyond her years, is always given the prime nesting spot within the cove. Her nest, adorned with various trinkets, stands as a testament to her experience. Her high rank within the herd provides her brood with enhanced protection, making their survival odds far greater than those of other females.
The dominant boar proudly displays his age through his heavily scarred hide and short temper—traits that have solidified his place at the top for many years. Other boars who have spent considerable time by his side often hold great respect for him, occasionally competing over the privilege of defending the sett and patrolling the herd with him during quieter times.
The loss of elder members in the sett is deeply felt, leaving a significant gap in the group's morale. Illness or weakness is rare in this species; instead, the most common cause of death is predation or engaging in conflicts their bodies can no longer withstand. Following the death of a dominant individual, the herd will often avoid the area where it occurred as a sign of respect and mourning.
Notes
In challenges for the dominant sow position, a loser may choose to leave the group and start a new one. Now separate, they consider each other rivals and can push each other off food bushes. 
If challenges for the dominant sow position result in the group having equal allies on each side, both sides will leave the group and start separate ones.
If a sett splits, other species that herd with Albertaceratops can choose which group they want to herd with.
If there are 4 or less Albertaceratops in a group, and all members of the group are active defenders, they may not chase predators who have forfeited for more than 5 bodylengths.
