Amargasaurus
    "Amarg", "Amarga"
Amargasaurus is a docile species and happily integrates into other sauropod herds. Their wailing cries can be heard from almost every biome. Both genders have friendly depositions and show no jealousy, even during courtship. When preparing to lay eggs, Amargasaurus uniquely flush into bright colors, caused by an over indulgence of berries.

✦ Offspring Behavior (Nested)
Young Amargasaurus are delicate and highly vulnerable to accidental trampling. Immediately after hatching, they are mobile and bleat loudly for a response.
Hatchling in dense forests, hatchling Amargasaurus are temporarily separated from their caregivers. After locating the direction of their calling herd, the hatchlings scamper towards the friendly sounds of adult individuals.
As social animals, young Amargasaurus frequently call out for reassurance. Once introduced to their caregivers and herdmates, they begin establishing their place in the group through playful activities such as chasing, mimicking adult behaviors, and picking up objects with their mouths. These early bonds are vital for their social development.
Offspring that struggle to play or interact with others often elicit frantic cries from attentive adults, which can alarm the entire herd. This causes the group to investigate in confusion and concern. Other herd members may attempt to help the frantic adult, taking turns encouraging the seemingly sickly offspring to eat and move.
Amargasaurus grow exceptionally quickly, transitioning from Hatchling all the way to Sub-Adult in a short time. By early adulthood, young Amargasaurus are already well integrated into the herd. 
Both male and female youngsters exhibit feisty personalities during this stage, using head-rearing and tail-whipping to draw attention.
Females, in particular, tease others - whipping their tails and calling affectionately before darting away to entice a chase, seeming to enjoy the attention when pursued.
As they mature into adulthood, this playful flirtation may persist or diminish, depending on the individual.
✦ Orphaned Behavior (Wild Spawn)
As a friendly species, orphaned baby Amargasaurus cry out in search of other sauropods or herbivores it can keep up with. When another herbivore species is identified, it rushes towards them, quickly falling in line behind an adult.
Unless aggressed, Amargasaurus orphans are persistent at following whatever herbivore species will accept them. If they manage to come across other sauropods, they will ditch other herbivore species in favor of any sauropod species that welcomes them.
Orphaned Amargasaurus follow herbivore migration routes, constantly searching for companions to bond with.
As an adult, Amargasaurus orphans face no social or behavioral difficulties.

Amargasaurus thrive in a variety of biomes, from floodplains to semi-arid regions and dense woodland forests. When not traveling alongside other species, they tend to migrate along sauropod migration routes.
Preferring to graze during the day, Amargasaurus have teeth well-suited for pulling berries from low bushes and swallowing their food whole.
Young Amargasaurus often struggle with foraging and rely on their caretakers for guidance. Adults will bring small mouthfuls of food to their calves, almost mimicking hand-feeding, and encourage them to pick off smaller portions, making it easier for the young to swallow.
Grazing Amargasaurus are highly social and vocal, frequently calling out to one another. They are uneasy when alone and require constant reassurance of their herd's presence.
During the warmer parts of the day, they particularly enjoy walking through and wallowing in shallow water, letting it cool their skin, and usually choose to nest nearby.
✦ Downtime Behavior
Amargasaurus are very vocal, even in downtime. They constantly coo to each other, almost constantly making constant noise and chatter when not sleeping.
Amargasaurus enjoy wallowing in shallow water and going for brief swims. Amargasaurus enjoy the sensation of water running down their necks and tail so they dunk their heads in the water often.
At night, the herd constantly cycles through those who are sleeping and those who have stayed awake to socialize with other members of the herd.

✦ Herd Behavior
Amargasaurus are herd animals, typically traveling in large groups alongside other sauropod species. They prefer the company of larger sauropods, relying on their size for protection by using them as a "meat shield" against predators.
Both male and female Amargasaurus are friendly with each other and harbor no ill will or resentment.
Individuals bond with those they spend the most time with, and readily respond to their calls. The closer the bond between individuals, the more likely they are to forage side by side, sleep with their backs resting together,  and play games of chase. This can be between females, males or a mixture.
Amargasaurus does not necessarily have a single, dedicated leader. The herd roles shift, with different individuals taking the lead in different situations.
Some are relied on during times of predator reaction, while others are followed to food and water. Amargasaurus that form close friendships with others readily follow their friends and the herd movements fluidly shift as allies rejoin the migration.
✦ Solitary Behavior
When traveling alone, Amargasaurus instinctively seek out dense woodlands,  similar to the environments where they hatched. The close-packed trees and thick undergrowth provide excellent camouflage, helping them avoid detection by predators.
On occasion, solitary Amargasaurus may attract the company of other herbivore species. While these are not their typical herd mates, they enjoy the presence of these strangers until they reunite with a sauropod group.
Bild
Amargasaurus do not show aggression toward their own kind unless protecting offspring.
Those caring for young do not tolerate another moving too quickly around their fragile young and may rear their head and whip their tails to demand space.
Uncharacteristic behaviors in an individual, however, can result in shunning. This is often marked by repeated tail slaps, and backing away in visible displeasure.
When aggressed by another herbivore species, Amargasaurus always moves away to avoid conflict.

✦ Ampelosaurus, Apatosaurus, Argentinosaurus, Camptosaurus, Citipati, Struthiomimus
Amargasaurus are generally sociable toward their own kind and other herbivores.
When approached by a welcoming herbivore, both males and females greet with gentle neck shakes and friendly calls. They are especially receptive to Sauropods and Citipati, often inviting them to stay nearby.
As a smaller sauropod species, Amargasaurus frequently follow the migratory patterns of larger sauropods for safety and resources.
However, Amargasaurus with adoptive young can be far less tolerant of other herbivores. Protective caretakers quickly use their tails to whip or rear their heads to signal displeasure. 
This behavior stems from the fragility of their young, who are at risk of being trampled. Until their offspring grow sturdy enough to safely navigate within the herd, caretakers keep other creatures at a safe distance.
Bild
When faced by Allosaurus or Torvosaurus specifically, Amargasaurus immediately issue fear calls - raising their heads while walking or running from the known danger. 
All Amargasaurus flee from these species. They may defend themselves as they flee, but must focus on escape.
If another predator approaches, Amargasaurus only respond if they know the predator is a threat. This varies based on individual experiences and some might not respond with fear until attacked.
Once the alarm is sounded for a threat, Amargasaurus mimic the cries of their herdmates and coordinate. 2 Amargasaurus emerge as the primary defenders, while the others try to get out of the way of the engagement, desperate to avoid accidental friendly fire from their herdmates.
Those on the outskirts of the group rear their heads in displeasure and whip their tails if the predator approaches within smacking distance.
The herd either stands their ground or flees. The behavior shifts depending on what hunts them, or how fearful their herdmates are.
The 2 defenders differentiate between pacing anxiously and standing their ground. They smack their tails and stomp their feet, each performing the role of aggressor and defender while the predator is close by.
Amargasaurus do not chase fleeing predators, but may charge forward to try and intimidate the predator into backing away. The intent here is to create distance. Sometimes this is accompanied by a smack of the head, but more often than not is employed to get predators to back off.
When an individual is targeted and severely wounded, the sound of pain and sight of vicious predation causes Amargasaurus to panic. The wounded might be left in a stampede or shoved behind defending bodies. 
Young are often trampled in these situations and sometimes the herd is split between caretakers luring their babies away from danger and those choosing to stand their ground and fight.
✦ Solitary Predator Reaction
When alone, an Amargasaurus may use another sauropod species as a meat-shield if they are available - keeping them between the Amargasaurus and the predator. 
If they encroach on another sauropod's personal space and are aggressed away, Amargasaurus always distance themselves to avoid being hit - often opting to try to flee or hide in nearby woodland.
If no other sauropods are nearby, a solitary Amargasaurus will react anxiously, rearing their head and whipping their tail in displeasure.
If attacked by a predator, the individual will respond in their own unique way; some turn and flee, while others stand their ground.
Amargasaurus - when alone - do not chase down fleeing predators, but may charge to drive them away from them, backing off once they retreat. They sometimes take the opportunity to move towards a more defensible position if it might increase survival chances.

✦ Courtship
When ready to breed, Amargasaurus fill their bellies with berries in preparation for laying eggs and courting. The berries contain astaxanthin, which is transferred from their stomach to their flesh and skin. This biological release of chemicals is what makes broody individuals flush into vibrant colors along their skin.
When their colorful skins are fully flushed, Amargasaurus begin the instinctual migration to their nesting grounds. All Amargasaurus return to the same location each time and instinctively follow those leaving to lay their eggs.
Albino and melanistic Amargasaurus, unable to flush into vibrant colors, experience difficulty during courtship, but still display with calls during the migration and may still be accepted as mates.
Not every Amargasaurus participate in nesting, depending on how much food they managed to consume beforehand.
Along the way, males mingle between migrating females, hoping to impress them with their bright colors and calls.
If a female is impressed by an approaching male she will move slightly away from the herd - if he follows, the two briefly pair with affectionate calls and walk together for a while, before rejoining the herd again. 
Amargasaurus form promiscuous mating bonds, with females often accepting the advances of multiple males. As a result, a single clutch of eggs can hatch offspring with different sires, increasing genetic diversity within the herd.
Amargasaurus are not picky with whom they bond with, and do not grow jealous of each other. Even if a friendship is between individuals of the opposite sex, this does not hinder their herdmates chances of nesting with others.
Even if arriving at the nesting grounds unpaired, a courting individual may still find another within this area, sometimes even waiting multiple days before moving on unsuccessfully.
✦ Nesting
When choosing a nesting spot, females dig their nests next to one another and carefully arrange thick bushes around their nest for camouflage and protection.
The size of a female's egg cluster often reflects the abundance of food in the area - areas rich in berries spawn a full clutch, while an area lacking berries might result in fewer eggs.
After laying her eggs and regurgitating food into the nest, females leave the wooded areas and linger nearby. If enough members are in the herd, they surround the nesting site with watchful eyes.
 
✦ Parenthood & Adoption
When the first offspring hatches, its initial movements in the nest prompts its siblings to begin emerging from their eggs. Once all hatchlings have pipped, they call out for adults, who respond with excited wails to guide them.
Following the herd's voice, the hatchlings stumble out from the dense foliage and greet their waiting herd. All adults rush to greet the new generation and particularly broody individuals or pairs may try to lure young away with them.
Not every Amargasaurus is fond of offspring, but those who are happily mimic the chirps of young. Both males and females adopt these newly hatched Amargasaurus and form no paternal bonds with the eggs they laid. All hatchlings are an equal opportunity to parenthood.
Hatchlings closely follow their chosen caretaker, who provides constant guidance until they reach adulthood.
Adoptive caregivers play an active role in teaching young how to forage. They begin by “hand-rearing,” gently nipping off small clusters of berries to feed each offspring, helping them learn how to gather food on their own. This attentive care ensures the young develop the skills necessary for survival.
✦ Offspring Maturity
Even when adoptive young grow into adulthood, caretakers remain steadfast in their duties when broody. Some may grow tired of caring for young, while others fondly continue caring for their chosen young well into adulthood.
Bild
Elder Amargasaurus are individuals who have lived long lives, often siring or raising numerous offspring. Once they reach the milestone of approximately 20 clutches, their physical condition begins to decline, though the rate of deterioration varies between individuals.
Common signs of aging include slower reactions to predators and an increased need for rest, with elders often spending more time sleeping.
✦ Death
As an elder nears the end of its life, feeding becomes increasingly difficult due to diminished strength and mobility. Starvation is the most frequent cause of death among these aging individuals, marking the natural conclusion of their life cycle.

Profile - Quick View
Amargasaurus are Large Diurnal Terrestrial Herbivores.
Amargasaurus can group with unlimited individuals, offspring and adoptees.
Amargasaurus have a passive nature.
Amargasaurus can have up to 2 actively in an engagement.
Amargasaurus hand feed their babies at hatchling and juvenile age by picking berries and foods for them. Babies struggle to chew, so opt to swallow food whole, aided by their caretakers.
Amargasaurus live in huge groups called herds and prefer the company of any sauropod species that will accept them - but will also group with various sentry species as well. They are passive and docile creatures, and happy to socialize with almost all species.
Amargasaurus spend a lot of time grazing and socializing together.
In courtship, Amargasaurus females may accept the advances of multiple males, resulting in split paternity in their nests.
