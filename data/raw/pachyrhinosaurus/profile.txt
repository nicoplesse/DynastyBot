Pachyrhinosaurus
    "Pachyr", "Pachyrhino", "Pachy"
Pachyrhinosaurus are large, relatively docile ceratopsians that group with other, similar species, and migrate over long distances throughout Gondwa in search of food. Led by an elder female, males form defensive bands known as shields, consisting of related males that work together to protect the whole group - known as a migration.

Pachyrhinosaurus form harem bonds.
Pachyrhinosaurus are nomadic. Males become territorial in their nesting ground. When courting and nesting, males defend small territories around food sources.
Pachyrhinosaurus have a tolerant nature.

Group Limits (In-Game Group)
Unlimited Sub/Adult individuals, lead by an elder female.
Unlimited Offspring
Unlimited Adoptees

These groups are known as a migration.

Additionally, Pachyrhinosaurus may group with Albertaceratops, Nasutoceratops, Styracosaurus, and Psittacosaurus.This grouping is called a mixed-species ceratopsian herd.


Engagement Limits (Defence & Hunts)
Predator Reaction: ⁨Defensive⁩
Unlimited Pachyrhinosurus form a defensive formation when a predator the herd deems as dangerous approaches within 10 body lengths of themselves. 
Individuals may step forward a total of one body length to land a hit on the predator, but then must step back into the formation.
Up to 4 adults can break the formation to charge the predator.

Mixed species protection
When migrating: Unlimited Pachyrhinosaurus, Styracosaurus and Albertaceratops form a defensive formation when a predator the herd deems as dangerous is within 10 body lengths. Up to 4 adult individuals (of any species) are permitted to step outside the formation to attempt to chase away the predator. Once the predator is chased 10 body lengths from the herd, the defenders must retreat to the herd. 
Psittacosaurus may use the herd as a meat shield to hide from threats.
When in nesting grounds: Pachyrhinosaurus that have claimed a bush do not permit other species into their small territory. Other species they herd with are shoved outside of hitting distance, and are not protected.
 
Territorial Defence
When challenged in their nesting grounds, Pachyrhinosurus harems and courting males form separate formations around their resources/nests. Females actively nesting with these males can choose to retreat into his formation, or join the main herd.
If a harem or courting male(s) is attacked, the rest of the herd may leave the nesting grounds and begin their migration earlier.
If non-nesting individuals or the matriarch is attacked, they form a defensive formation in much the same way as their predator reaction. 
However, those uninvolved (males and harems in a territory) do not flee and remain in their territories during the engagement.

In all engagements; hatchlings, juveniles and adolescents do not count towards the engagement limit, and their biological or adoptive mother becomes the 4th engaged.

Preferred Diet

Nesting Limits
🌧️ Rainy Season &  ☀️ Dry Season: Up to 5 eggs per female.
Pachyrhinosaurus females nest in harems near the food bush claimed by their chosen male in their nesting grounds.
Females may nest again once all her offspring reach Sub-Adult, or she has no offspring below Sub-Adult in game currently.
Females must update their nesting posts to include the names of male offspring hatched. The name displayed on the character creation menu must match the name on the nesting post. This is important proof of relation for shields.
Pachyrhinosaurus may form female same sex pairs. Males do not form same sex pairs. Female same sex pairs may adopt. Females in a same sex pair may also opt to court and nest with a male.

✦ Offspring Behavior (Nested)
Born into a harem, young Pachyrhinosaurus are entirely dependent on their mothers for survival. At birth, they are deaf, close to the ground, and helpless, instinctively staying near their mother's familiar feet.
The father - a dominant male in a shield - is allowed to approach and sniff them, familiarizing himself with their scent before stepping back from any further parental duties.
As they grow, the daunting world beyond the nest becomes less overwhelming, and their siblings grow more playful. Both males and females engage in play fighting, facing off head-to-head and standing their ground.
Larger, older offspring tend to shove their younger siblings aside, using their size to push to the front of the group when following their mother or getting to food first.
Young Pachyrhinosaurus are playful and often mischievous, engaging in shoving matches, snatching food, and chasing each other. Even the smallest among them bravely confronts new things, roaring defiantly as they stand their ground, embodying their defensive instincts.
The mother, patient and gentle, encourages her young to move ahead of her as they head to food, which is guarded by their father.
When they begin shedding their hatchling skin, the females leave the harem to join other females into a migration again. The eldest female welcomes them with a bellowing roar and initiates the migratory lifestyle once again.
Both male and female offspring stay close to their mothers until they reach Sub-Adult. At this age the males develop more distinct traits and are nudged to the back of the migration by adult females.
When pushed to the rear, young bulls - once confident - shrink under the authority of experienced older bulls. Following from a respectful distance, they mature with a strong regard for their father. If they meet his standards, they may be allowed into his shield if space permits. 
By the next breeding cycle, these younger males contribute to the breeding efforts within their father's shield, solidifying their role within the group and the social structure of the Pachyrhinosaurus.
Unlike their brothers, young females form strong bonds with other females of similar age, all subordinate to the elder female. They observe how respect and support are shown to elders within the group.
✦ Orphaned Behavior (Wild Spawn)
Orphaned Pachyrhinosaurus, guided by instinct, seek out other ceratopsians in hopes of being adopted. If initially rejected, they persist, trailing from a distance with hopes of eventual acceptance into the herd.
When completely alone, juveniles tend to remain in familiar areas, surviving on dried nuts and drinking from ponds. However, with limited foraging exposure, they may develop a narrow diet, relying on a single food type throughout their lives.
Bild
Pachyrhinosaurus are nomadic, spending their lives traveling through various biomes. They often prefer the company of other ceratopsians, following their migrations or calling them over with friendly grunts.
Female Pachyrhinosaurus are nurturing and often adopt stray ceratopsians, readily integrating orphaned young into their groups.
Among their closest allies is the ever-loyal Psittacosaurus, a small species with excellent eyesight that helps detect danger.
Male Pachyrhinosaurus are attentive to their migrating herd of females, always on the lookout for potential threats. Traveling at the back of the cows, they move silently, concentrating on sounds around the migration rather than each other. When alone, a male will seek out other ceratopsian groups to herd with until he finds a migration.
Female Pachyrhinosaurus travel in large groups, following paths learned from their elders. They follow seasonal migration routes or trail behind other ceratopsian species. During rest, they enjoy mud rolling to soothe itchy skin, and when near rivers they walk in the shallow water, cooling their calloused feet in the mud.
✦ Blind individuals
When a Pachyrhinosaurus loses its sight - visualized by a sudden tint of blue in its eyes - it struggles to find the safest route to travel on and cannot tell friend from foe. 
In these cases, a male will fiercely guard a particular bush against any approaching Pachyrhinosaurus within their nesting grounds. Their impaired vision often leads to misidentification, so what might be a gentle female is mistaken for a rival. 
If the would-be challenger dismisses the warning call, the male wastes no time in ejecting it from his territory.  Even close kin - such as brothers or even fathers - will sometimes step in to block the injured member, inadvertently curbing his ability to fend off potential mates.
Blind females, by contrast, manage far better. They are typically trailed by a guiding relative who leads them to food and water, ensuring their basic needs are met despite the handicap.
✦ Downtime Behavior
Pachyrhinosaurus spend their downtime grazing and lounging, finding particularly soft spots in grass, mud, or sand.
Both males and females sleep to try to keep their energy up. They primarily rely on Psittacosaurus to act as sentries for them at night.
Pachyrhinosaurus will scratch themselves on rocks and trees in order to clean themselves. Males are known to be impatient, scratching their heads against terrain and trees to release stress.
Pachyrhinosaurus herds yawn often during downtime- and these yawns are contagious!

✦ Migration
The migration is led by the eldest cow - oftentimes it's the one who has the most allies in the herd. Pachyrhinosaurus females follow those they trust to guide them through rough terrain and cows will follow the majority of the group.
Sometimes an older cow joins an existing migration. It is not uncommon for the herd to temporarily split for some time before regrouping at their nesting grounds.
While traveling, males follow the migratory females from the rear of the group. If a female or offspring lags behind, males paw the ground :aggro_emote: in the direction they want the slow individual to move towards. 
If ignored, a frustrated bull may call aggressively to force the individual to listen. Oftentimes this does the trick, but bold younglings often roar defiance and charge in a game of play. However, they are quickly subdued by an aggressive roar from the irritated bull or the call of their mother.
Males ignore all herbivores that aren't Pachyrhinosaurus females, related males or grouped ceratopsians. Related bulls bond closely, whilst non related bulls barely acknowledge the existence of each other, often rubbing sides accidentally during migration or permitted to sleep nearby for shared warmth. Only related bulls (brothers, or father and sons) form the closest male bonds and call to each other during their travels.
A bull always prefers the company of his closely bonded sons or brothers in his shield, but other male relatives are treated with kindness in the herd. Related bulls have friendly dispositions to each other, walking together and assessing each other's strength with playful bullfighting during down time.
Females, meanwhile, are passive to all within the herd and enjoy bonding through drinking and eating side by side. Cows that have remained in the same herd for many nesting seasons form close friendships and prefer to walk near those they consider allies. 
If an ally is trailing behind, a cow does stop to call to them, turning her head from side to side to try to spot her absent ally. When reunited, cows shake their shoulders together and grunt - a sound mimicked by nearby females, and ignored by bulls.
✦ Low T bulls and high T cows
The following behaviors are observed exclusively in individuals with hormonal imbalances, these behaviors are not found in normal Pachyrhinosaurus individuals.
Low Testosterone bulls , easily identified by a meager twin horn on their crest and quilled shoulders, do not partake in challenges for territory during nesting season alone. 
Rather than stand their ground, they withdraw when challenged, often only defending a territory when following the lead of more assertive relatives in their shields. These bulls only secure nests when no rival bulls confront them. 
Curiously, offspring born to these low testosterone bulls often rise to become the main nesting bulls within their shield. With no rivalry over females between these fathers and their sons, the shield forms the strongest bonds.
High Testosterone cows develop a bristled, quill-like fringe along their tails and a lone horn jutting from the center of their crests - the result from hormonal imbalance. 
These females are notably more demanding than others, often dictating the herd’s path and pushing for specific routes with stubborn persistence. They're typically the first to confront predators with vocal defiance and will physically shove slower herd members forward.
While their offspring do not share the same fiery temperament, they tend to follow their mother’s lead, even preferring the same bull she chooses to nest with.
 
✦ Solitary Behavior
When alone, Pachyrhinosaurus are nervous. When scenting or spotting a predator, the individual will always try to flee or find somewhere defensible to stand near, rather than engage immediately.
They migrate in search of companions, specifically other ceratopsian species they herd with.
As a solo, they are quiet, often unheard while they travel between nesting grounds in search of company.

When traveling outside their nesting grounds, male Pachyrhinosaurus are passive, and tolerate traveling near other males at the rear of the migration. 
A father and his sons typically walk together, with the younger bulls relying on his wisdom and guidance. During times of rest, related bulls spar in mock bullfights.
This tolerant behavior lasts until they step foot in their nesting grounds. A bull will only become testy and pushy when nesting. Outside of that, they work together to defend the migration, resting side by side and feeding together while they trail behind the females.
A bull will only become defensive and aggressive towards other bulls when in a nesting ground. Outside of that, they tolerate each others' mutual goal in defending the migration and walk together at the rear of the females.
COWS
When at a nesting ground, females that participate in nesting are defensive of their nests, readily stepping in front of other species, herdmates and predators to protect her newly laid eggs and hatched young. Females rely on the bull to protect their nesting site, while she stands guard and issues warning bellows. 
When the Elder female calls to migrate, bulls remain stubbornly at their bush - only abandoning their vigil once their females leave his territory.
On their migration, cows are not aggressive with each other and rely strongly on those they consider allies. Only females with a hormonal imbalance react differently. See Social Interaction, high T females.

When migrating, Pachyrhinosaurus bulls are indifferent to the existence of everything else. Cows might call to other certatopsians they herd with, but otherwise do not form any bonds with these species.
Outside of nesting grounds, Pachrhinosaurus do not approach other herbivores that do not group with them; instead, they watch from a distance, giving the occasional grunt to alert each other that another species is spotted. 
When challenged over resources, Pachyrhinosaurus stubbornly remain where they eat, pushing the rival herbivore away after issuing threatening bellows to ward away conflict.
If solitary or grouped with other Pachyrhinosaurus, they show little concern for other species tagging along. However, when it comes to their territory within nesting areas, the dynamic shifts dramatically. Both males and females become fiercely protective of their eggs and hatchlings, aggressively using their shove ability to push away any intruders - friendly or not. 
When attempting to push away other species, Pachyrhinosaurus must aggressively call :threaten_call: before using any attack.
✦ Albertaceratops and Styracosaurus
Outside of nesting areas, Albertaceratops and Styracosaurus are tolerated within a mixed-species migration.
Pachyrhinosaurus typically follow their own path, but in the absence of an elder cow to lead, a herd or individual is known to travel with these smaller ceratopsians.
Understanding their instincts of herding behavior and tolerance with each other is beneficial should a predator target them.
✦ Psittacosaurus
Serving as living alarm systems, Psittacosaurus are essential to the herd's defence.
Their quick, urgent calls alert the larger, less sharp-eyed Pachyrhinosaurus, triggering swift, defensive formations after their call.

✦ Instinctual First Response
Once danger is sensed, adults become alert, raising their heads, sniffing, and bunching together in a tight, defensive formation. The formation type varies between a wall of armoured crests or a circle protecting the vulnerable.
✦ Defensive Formation
Pachyrhinosaurus stand their ground, moving together to form a defensive formation, depending on the terrain around them. 
Cows with young use their bodies to block the predator's view from their offspring. Some cows drop their heads towards the ground in a block position, hoping to deter damage to her and those she defends.
Bulls, especially those in a shield, roar in challenge to the predator, standing shoulder to shoulder with his sons or brothers, who stick close to his side.
When in an open area a circle is preferred, but a wall formation is also used if they are able to back up against terrain such as cliff faces.
✦ Active Defenders
When an engagement has started, up to 4 adults (usually bulls, and preferably shields),  charge forward to land crushing blows on the predator outside of the formation.
Others stay within the formation, only stepping forward a maximum of one body length to land a hit on the predator, before stepping back into the formation.
The active defenders barrels into the predator in an attempt to cripple their foe, then pace outside the formation, staying close enough to properly protect their herd and protect their exposed bodies.
Other herd members remain firm in the formation to keep a strong defensive front. If the predator makes the mistake of treading too close, Pachyrhinosaurus within the formation will not hesitate to step forward and land a killing blow before retreating back into the safety of the formation.
✦ Mixed Species Protection in times of danger
When traveling alongside Styracosaurus orAlbertaceratops, Pachyrhinosaurus instinctively fall into a defensive formation when predators appear nearby: pressing shoulder to shoulder to form a defensive circle or line.
Any individual separated from the herd is dangerously vulnerable. These stragglers act on pure instinct, driven by a single purpose: reach the herd. When a predator takes interest, the separated flee relentlessly toward the safety of the formation, ignoring all else.
In times of danger, Pachyrhinosaurus, Albertaceratops, and Styracosaurus share a rare moment of a unified herd mindset. When threatened by predation, they respond as one, all movement and effort focused on regrouping and restoring the safety of the herds defensive formation.
A targeted individual may attempt to outrun the threat, but if cornered or forced into close quarters, it will lash out with horn and bulk, fighting only long enough to break free and continue its retreat to the herd.
Up to 4 adult individuals from any species may patrol beyond the defensive ring. These sentinels charge, chase, and aggressively drive predators away, pushing them back up to ten body lengths from the formation. The circle is never willingly broken and is only abandoned if a herd member falls.
Psittacosaurus, while valiant sentries, do not partake in engagements and seek shelter behind the defensive formation.
 
✦ When hunted by powerful predators
Pachyrhinosaurus, while poor in eyesight, have long-lasting memories. Previous encounters with powerful predators (apexs, multiple larges, semi-aquatics, etc..) are not forgotten easily. Instead of forming a defensive formation, individuals might opt to flee, putting as much distance between themselves and the perceived threat in a frantic stampede. 
It's during these fearful moments that sometimes Psittacosaurus gets caught under thundering feet, falling prey to the confusion.
If the predator begins an engagement while the migration attempts to flee, Pachyrhinosaurus might halt and form their signature defensible circle or wall, or opt to abandon bitten individuals. 
Males and bonded females are known to turn and defend their bitten ally, but once the target has taken significant injury (half health) and is already being defended (3 are already defending it), they are forced to retreat. They anxiously watch their fate from some distance away (10 body lengths).

✦ When Solitary
Solo Pachyrhinosaurus, without the protection of ceratopsians they group with or their own kind, opt to put on an intimidation display to deter predators.
If the display fails and the predator approaches within hitting distance, the solitary Pachyrhinosaurus adapts an offensive strategy - charging forward to cripple and roaring loudly to frighten.

✦ Courtship
Migrating cows court around the same time. Led by the matriarch, the migration changes their path towards a well liked nesting ground when the eldest female feels ready to lay her eggs.
When they arrive at her chosen nesting grounds, cows take to relaxing in the warm sun, cooing to each other or following loud bulls with interest.
Males claim small territories to impress females:
Bursting with confidence, bulls become competitive over food resources nearby, and shields sniff out the best bush to claim as their own. 
A shield (up to 3 related males) will work together to claim a small territory around a bush. Males not in a shield work alone to claim a bush territory.
Territorial when nesting, shields forcefully challenge rival shields from their chosen nesting site. The size of these territories vary based on the amount of females in the migration - with smaller harems splitting into tinier spaces, and larger harems claiming more space.
Once bulls have established their territories, they bellow loudly, pacing the length of their chosen spot. Rival shields, seeing the quantity of resources claimed, will approach the shield and paw the ground, scraping dirt with their beaks and sizing their competition up.
If the challenge is accepted, a whole shield of up to 3 bulls will work together to defend their territory from rivals. 
Charging forward, they each target a male in the rival shield - or, if the rival is solo - the whole shield targets the challenger. These bullfights are loud and often end sooner when crippling blows land on exposed hides.
The fight consists of charging towards their rival and landing heavy blows, then retreating backwards for a distance to repeat the forward charge. These bullfights are always fought with the challengers head facing their rival. Looking away or turning tail signals defeat, and the loser is forced to walk away, seeking a secondary territory to claim.
During these challenges, other bulls may watch, assessing the skill of these shields. 
A territory will only ever be fought over once per nesting season, as males do not wish to wound their collective herd defenders, but rather stand firm on their space when nesting.
If a territory has already been challenged, shields find alternative spots, preferring not to upset the fragile group by overstepping their bounds.
During bullfights between shields, cows wander between the territories, assessing each shield's chosen spot. Typically, it's the resources that win them over - but a cow always prefers more heavily scarred males. These males are seen as stronger and more likely to defend the territory while her young are weak and fragile in their hatchling phase.
Cows may choose any male within a shield to nest with. More often than not it is the most active male; favoring his scarred hide - an indication of age and wisdom.
✦ Nesting
Once a cow has chosen a bull, she shows him great affection, and proceeds to construct a nest within his territory. She will remain there until her offspring are juvenile.
Other cows choosing the same bull place their nests nearby.
✦ Parenthood
When hatchlings break from their eggs, the territory owning bulls are permitted to investigate - but afterwards, they leave all parenting duties to the mother. In the awful event of her death, the bull will reluctantly raise them himself - at least until another cow is willing to adopt them.
While young are hatchling, mothers stand guard over their brood. Other cows in the harem who have chosen the same bull will dig their nests nearby, benefiting from the companionship and shared protection over young.
Usually, when all of the young have reached juvenile, the cow will leave the territory and follow around elder females. When enough females are nearby, the eldest will signal to the rest of the migration to resume their nomadic nature, leaving their nesting grounds behind.
✦ Offspring Maturity
When a mothers young shed completely out their juvenile skin and begin to look like subadults, she will promptly shove her sons to the rear of the group. Nearby females follow her lead, pawing the ground and grunting dismissal.
Daughters are permitted to remain and travel in the inner most part of the migration. Protected by older cows and led along familiar paths.
✦ Adoption
In the unfortunate event of a cow dying, Pachyrinosaurus females are seen adopting orphans.
This maternal behavior extends to orphan young of other ceratopsians they group with.
With a soft call towards the frightened calf, the cow sniffs over their shivering body and invites them to walk beside her.
All cows in the migration care for the collective young and put great emphasis on keeping their young together. If straying too far, a mother will retrieve them, shaking her crest and gently encouraging them to rejoin the main group.
Bild
Young females instinctively stay close to the elder Pachyrhinosaurus cows, offering support and diligently following one well versed in navigating challenging terrain.
Blind elders may rely heavily on vocalizations to communicate with younger members, guiding them during migrations or foraging. Their deep, resonant calls establish a sense of safety and direction.
Despite their blindness, older Pachyrhinosaurus can recall safe routes and feeding areas from their youth, leading the herd based on these memories and often pausing to orient themselves with the sounds and scents of familiar places. When threatened, they instinctively move closer to the center of the herd, forming a protective barrier for the younger members while relying on the herd's other senses to detect danger.
As bulls become elders, the desire to breed lessens. A shield will remain bonded to an elder individual, relying on his experience to support them during courtship and leading him to food when his eyes blur with age.
✦ Death
When a member falls - whether to predators, injury, or natural causes - nearby Pachyrhinosaurus often remain in the area instead of instantly moving on.
If the death is due to natural causes, bonded individuals briefly investigate the body before moving from the corpse a safe distance away.

When challenged by another species, only the male and his shield members (3 related males total) may accept the territory challenge.
