Deinocheirus
Deinocheirus was a bizarre, large theropod dinosaur that lived around 70 million years ago during the Late Cretaceous in what is now Mongolia. It had an unusual combination of features, including massive arms with large claws, a long snout, and a humped back. Despite its fearsome appearance, Deinocheirus was an omnivore, feeding on plants, fish, and small animals. It could grow up to 35 feet long, making it one of the largest members of its group, and its strange, mixed diet and anatomy have puzzled paleontologists since its discovery.

Growing Up
Offspring Behavior
Hatchling Deinocheirus are highly developed from the moment they hatch. They are capable of walking, swimming, and finding food and water on their own just minutes after hatching. Despite this early independence, they remain very dependent on their parents - especially their mother.
Hatchlings follow their mother closely, never straying far from her side, as she offers both guidance and protection. Both parents actively participate in raising their offspring, though the young often show a stronger attachment to their mother. However, they still enjoy spending time with their father, engaging in playful interactions and bonding together as a family unit.
As Deinocheirus grow into juvenile, they begin to exhibit more independence and curiosity about their surroundings. However, they still show close attachments to their parents and continue to follow them almost everywhere.
During this stage, their parents start to grant them more freedom, allowing them to wander and explore their environment more freely, though never too far from their protective watch. Juvenile Deinocheirus are encouraged to interact with other members of their species, especially the offspring of other pairs that might be nearby. This socialization helps them develop the skills they will need as they grow, both for survival and for future social dynamics within their species.
When Deinocheirus reach adolescence, a noticeable shift occurs in both their behavior and their relationship with their parents. The parents begin to distance themselves, encouraging their now-teenage offspring to take more responsibility for their own needs, particularly in finding food.
Although the parents are less involved, they still keep a watchful eye on their young, ensuring their safety from predators or other threats. Adolescent Deinocheirus tend to explore the bodies of water where they reside, often wandering farther from their parents in search of food and entertainment. 
They may even chase or scare other herbivores out of sheer curiosity, though their intentions are playful rather than aggressive.
At this age, Deinocheirus parents often opt to return to the wider group with their rapidly maturing young, enabling their offspring a better chance to socialize with others of their species.
During this stage, males start to become louder and more assertive, developing their vocalizations, while females remain relatively calm and laid-back, observing rather than participating in the boisterous behavior of the males.
Once they hit maturity, Deinocheirus leave their parents to seek mates and start their own families. Some may venture to entirely new bodies of water, exploring different environments in search of suitable conditions for raising their future offspring, but many opt to stay within the wider gathering, keen to find a mate.
Orphaned Behavior
Orphaned Deinocheirus are naturally very skittish and cautious, and usually constantly on high alert to avoid attracting the attention of any nearby carnivores.
Despite their emotional dependency on their parents, orphans quickly adapt to their circumstances and become quite self-sufficient. They are capable of foraging for food and finding water on their own, using their instincts to navigate the environment. However, their heightened caution often means they prefer to stay hidden, blending into their surroundings to avoid danger.
Even though orphaned Deinocheirus are highly self-sufficient in terms of survival and exhibit asocial behavior, they still need to fulfill their social needs. These young creatures crave the company of others to feel a sense of family, especially during their formative years. This need for social interaction often drives them to seek out the companionship of other orphaned Deinocheirus. It is common for these young individuals to gather near bodies of water, forming loose dispersal groups that exist primarily to meet their socialization needs.
Although they enjoy each other's company, orphans lack the skills or strength to defend themselves or others in the event of danger. When faced with a threat, their natural instinct is to scatter and run, relying on their speed to escape rather than attempting to confront or ward off predators. This instinctual behavior highlights their vulnerability as they navigate the challenges of survival without parental protection.
General Behavior
Deinocheirus are intriguing creatures that exhibit aloof behavior, yet have a strong need for socialization. While they primarily form strong pairbonds rather than large groups, they enjoy spending significant amounts of time together near bodies of water with other members of their species.
Pairs engage in a variety of water-related activities that enrich their lives, such as swimming and diving in search of food and resources or simply for the joy of it. They also forage for fruits and berries along the shores, explore the coastline for shellfish, fish in the water, and take leisurely naps on the shore. During these moments, they often bask in the sun or enjoy the tranquility of moonbathing at night.
Deinocheirus are cathemeral, meaning they are active both during the day and at night. However, they tend to be slightly more energetic during sunrise and sunset, when the light conditions are optimal for foraging and socializing. This adaptability allows them to make the most of their environment and resources, ensuring they have ample opportunities for feeding and interaction.
It is quite common to observe pairs of Deinocheirus grooming each other and spending quality time together, which deepens their bonds. Despite their aloof nature, multiple pairs can often be seen mingling in the same area, engaging in social interactions without forming a cohesive group. These interactions serve to reinforce social ties among the pairs and create a sense of community in their environment.
In terms of parenting, Deinocheirus excel at caring for their young and any adopted offspring. They are attentive and dedicated parents, often seen spending extensive time with their babies, nurturing and protecting them as they grow. Their commitment to their offspring is evident in the way they prioritize the well-being of their young, ensuring they have everything they need to thrive.
Deinocheirus also have unique dietary habits, particularly enjoying chewing on lakeweed to absorb essential nutrients. It is not uncommon to see these creatures with strands of lakeweed in their mouths throughout the day. In addition to this, they may occasionally peck at rocks to ingest small stones that aid in their digestion.
Deinocheirus have a strong inclination for scratching themselves frequently. This behavior is not only a form of self-care, but also a way to bond with their companions. They often seek the help of other Deinocheirus, especially their lifelong mate, to assist in scratching hard-to-reach places using their claws and beak. 
In addition, they may turn to their Struthiomimus companions for assistance, further strengthening the social bonds within their small community.
When no help is available, Deinocheirus make use of their surroundings, rubbing against trees, logs, branches, and rocks to relieve any itch or discomfort. Over time, they tend to develop a preference for specific scratching spots in their environment, returning to these familiar places for comfort. This behavior becomes a daily routine and a common sight near bodies of water where Deinocheirus gather, blending practicality with social interaction.
Although Deinocheirus are sedentary, they are sometimes forced to leave their usual water hangouts - either from a lack of resources, or high levels of predation.
When migrating, Deinocheirus opt to move as one large group where possible, hoping that their numbers might dissuade predators from hunting them while they locate to safer waters.
Deinocheirus may migrate both on land or in water, and may make short stops in smaller water sources on the way to their new destination.
Social Interaction
Deinocheirus are aloof creatures that form monogamous pairs for life. Despite their aloof nature, they have a strong craving for social interactions. They spend nearly all of their time with their mate, engaging in activities such as grooming each other, foraging for food, basking in the sun, and chewing on lakeweed together. This close companionship helps them develop a deep bond that strengthens their partnership.
Throughout the year, it is very common to observe multiple pairs of Deinocheirus congregating around bodies of water, especially during the breeding season. These gatherings are often peaceful and harmonious, with adult pairs relaxing on the shore while their young play together in the shallows. This shared environment fosters a strong sense of community among the offspring, who learn important social cues and behaviors through their interactions with one another. These playful exchanges not only strengthen their bonds but also prepare them for future social encounters.
Males tend to be louder and more energetic than females, moving around more frequently and being more vocal in their behavior. They often vocalize loudly at any potential threat to alert their mate and other Deinocheirus in the area, acting as protectors of their shared space. This heightened alertness helps ensure the safety of their family unit and reinforces their role as vigilant guardians.
In contrast, females are generally calmer and more reserved than males. They often prefer to stay in one place for longer periods, taking time to relax and enjoy their surroundings. This difference in behavior allows them to create a stable environment for their offspring while still engaging in nurturing activities with their mate.
Deinocheirus can sometimes be seeming gathering together in massive groups, consisting of multiple different adult pairs, in a grouping known as a gathering. These groups are relatively harmonious, with pairs socializing with other pairs without many squabbles.
Deinocheirus split from these groups when looking to nest or adopt young, and swim off with their partner to form a smaller group while they build a nest.
Unpaired females congregate together inside the gathering, and tend to be more social and affectionate between each other than small pools of bachelor groups. Though social, if attacked, they will not defend one another.
Unpaired males do similarly to unpaired females, forming small bachelor groups of no more than 5 males at a time within the gathering, and choosing to spend time together, until they find a mate of their own. They spend their time posturing, and practicing for eventual courtship. They do not defend one another if attacked.
Aggressive Interaction
Conflicts between Deinocheirus are quite rare, as they tend to prioritize avoiding confrontation and respecting each other's boundaries. However, if an individual exhibits unprovoked aggression towards its own kind or towards the Struthiomimus they live alongside, the entire community at that body of water will shun the aggressor, and, if necessary, they may even exile them. This same rule applies to aggressive Struthiomimus, who will be ostracized by both the Deinocheirus and their own species' communities.
When confronted with aggressive behavior from other herbivores, Deinocheirus prefer to avoid conflict by calmly retreating into the water, showing little urgency as they try to defuse the situation. However, if another herbivore attempts to steal their food, continuously harass them, or pose a threat to their offspring, these usually peaceful giants will not hesitate to display their powerful claws in a defensive stance. Though they aim to avoid lethal confrontations, they are more than capable of standing their ground when necessary.
When encountering carnivores, Deinocheirus will generally try to stand their ground if the situation appears favorable. They assess the danger carefully, and if the threat seems too great, they will swiftly flee, retreating into the water or on land depending on where they feel safest in that moment.
Cross-Species Interaction
Deinocheirus are peaceful giants, known for their tranquil disposition and gentle presenece among most other fellow herbivores. However, their demeanor shifts dramatically when carnivores are nearby, becoming extremely defensive - especially if they have young present.
Despite their size and strength, Deinocheirus are intelligent enough to recognize the power of other apex-sized creatures, particularly predators, and know how to pick their battles wisely. They prefer to avoid unnecessary confrontations, prioritizing the safety of their family over pride.

Struthiomimus
Sometimes, Deinocheirus mistakenly identify Struthiomimus to be offspring of their own kind, believing these smaller dinosaurs to be Deinocheirus with some form of growth hindrance or stunting. This misunderstanding often results in adoption of the Struthiomimus, with the smaller dinosaurs welcoming them into their family units. Remarkably, a Deinocheirus may even go so far as to defend Struthiomimus fiercely.
Struthiomimus, in turn, play a valuable role within the group, further strengthening the bond within the group. Their agility and vigilance make them excellent lookouts, acting as alarm systems for potential threats to the family. They are quick to alert their Deinocheirus "parents" if they sense danger, and also help keep an eye on the younger members of the group, ensuring they do not wander off too far into unsafe territory.
Bonding between the two species is often reinforced through grooming rituals and playful interaction. One common behavior observed is splashing - all Deinocheirus love water, but young ones especially delight in play sessions at the water's edge, and often try to coax their Struthiomimus companions into deeper water, splashing at them with joyful enthusiasm.
Sadly, this sometimes has the opposite effect - leading to panic from the less aquatically inclined Struthiomimus, who often get into struggles when drawn too far out into the water.
Older Deinocheirus are typically more sensitive to the needs and limitations of their companions, and remain content to play with them in shallower areas of water. They invite their Struthiomimus friends to cool off in the shallows, positioning themselves protectively between them and the deeper waters.
Even after a clutch matures, Struthiomimus may choose to remain with the Deinocheirus, assisting them with their next clutches and continuing to enrich the dynamic of this unique family unit.

Deinocheirus often extend their playful splashing behavior to other herbivores in their vicinity, flicking water at passerbys in a cheerful attempt to draw them in to play. Reactions to these antics vary by species, and over time, Deinocheirus learn which neighbors enjoy the game, and which ones would rather be left alone.
Deinocheirus are unusually fond of Halszkaraptor, forming playful, peaceful associations with these smaller, semi-aquatic relatives.  In particular, Juvenile Deinocheirus  enjoy chasing them through shallow waters in lighthearted bursts of activity. Food is willingly shared and Halszkaraptor are unusually welcomed near nesting or feeding sites. These interactions seem to bring mutual enrichment, offering both species companionship, entertainment, and safety in numbers.
Despite their enjoyment of Halszkaraptor, they do not actively defend them. They may opt to allow them to use them as meatshields, but will not rush to their defence.
Predator Reaction
When a predator attempts to hunt Deinocheirus offspring, they won't face just one defender - but both parents, who act in unison to protect their young.
One exception is with Adult Spinosaurus. Deinocheirus flee with their offspring, and only turn to fight back when escape is impossible.
This fierce loyalty even extends to their adopted Struthiomimus companions. Deinocheirus rise without hesitation to defend these smaller allies as if they were their own - though this is limited to 1 Adult, as opposed to 2 for Deinocheirus offspring.
The moment danger strikes, one (or both) adults instinctively move to shield the vulnerable, positioning themselves between the attacker and their charges to form a living wall of defense.
In most cases, Deinocheirus are discerning enough to assess a threat before engaging. When confronted by an Adult apex predator like Spinosaurus, they opt to flee as quickly as possible, recognizing the immense danger such a rival presents. Only when escape is impossible will they fight back, with just 1 protector.
Suchomimus provoke a defensive response in Deinocheirus - especially if they have young present. Suchomimus are one of the few semi-aquatic predators who might threaten a Deinocheirus, and put these species on edge.
Though they're generally ignored by healthy Deinosuchus and Sarcosuchus, Deinocheirus remain cautious around these ambush predators. Their watchfulness increases significantly when near water or when caring for young, aware that crocodilians may seize any opportunity to strike at the injured or vulnerable.
If a terrestrial predator approaches near water, Deinocheirus may employ aggressive splashing - rearing up and slamming their forelimbs into the surface to send up powerful sprays of water. These soaking strikes can drench and disorient land-based hunters, giving the Deinocheirus time to escape or reposition.
In some cases, Deinocheirus can be observed fleeing to land, where they shake off water and dry themselves in preparation for a counterattack. With their feathers no longer waterlogged and footing restored, they may turn and charge at the predator - especially if it continues to pursue or threatens the group further. While not naturally aggressive, Deinocheirus are fully capable of delivering a punishing defense when their family is endangered.
 
Courtship & Parenthood
When the Deinocheirus breeding season begins, unpaired females start to gather along the shorelines of lakes and rivers, forming loose-knit groups where they rest, forage and observe.
Unpaired males, meanwhile, driven by instinct and anticipation, become more vocal and energetic, positioning themselves around the female gathering.
In attempt to stand out from the competition, males stand up tall, spreading their feathered arms wide, and stretching their necks skyward in exaggerated postures. They bellow deep, echoing calls across the water, interspersing these with dramatic splashes using their arms - sometimes startling fish or other semi-aquatic species in the process.
These water displays not only demonstrate strength and vitality, but also highlight the male's alterness, suggesting he would make a capable protector during nesting season.
Females watch carefully, evaluating each male's stamina, presence, and awareness of his surroundings. If a female shows interest in a particular male, she selects a rock as a symbolic offering, akin to a wedding ring in humans.
The female then approaches her chosen male calmly, presenting her gift to him and calling softly.
If the male accepts the gift, the pair gently intertwine their necks in an affectionate bonding ritual, sharing low vocalizations and grooming one another.
Together, they then bury the rock in a special place along the waterline, forming a lasting memorial of their union, and marking the area as a special place.
Males that do not accept the female's advances, however, will simply ignore her and continue his display.
When it's time to nest, the pair always split off from the communal group, and form a separate group with just the two of them, and any adopted Struthiomimus or Halszkaraptor companions. They drift away from the main group, and select a well-hidden location near water to build their nest, which they construct together.
The mother incubates the eggs while the father stands guard, vigilantly protecting their territory and keeping watch even when the mother needs to leave the nest.
Once the babies hatch, both parents tirelessly work together to raise their young. It's common to see several pairs nesting near the same body of water, allowing the young to socialize and interact with one another, fostering a sense of community among the offspring.
If the pair have Struthiomimus in their group, Struthiomimus will build their nests close to those of the Deinocheirus. This proximity allows them to assist in surveying the area for potential dangers while also benefiting from the protection offered by their larger companions.
Once their offspring reaches Adolescent, the nesting pair opt to travel back towards the wider gathering group, and integrate their offspring with the others.
Deinocheirus are renowned for their nurturing instincts, making them exceptional parents. It is quite common for adult pairs to adopt orphaned Deinocheirus they encounter and raise them as their own.
This parental instinct to care for and protect young, even those not biologically related, speaks to the deep familial bonds these creatures form. Orphaned individuals who are adopted receive the same care, protection, and guidance as the biological offspring of the pair, giving them a second chance at survival within a supportive family structure.
Same sex pairs within Deinocheirus are also seen, with all male pairs and all female pairs equally common. Their courtship starts similarly to opposite sex pairs, with either individual taking on the "male" performance or "female" gift giving, depending on individual personalities.
Same sex pairs willingly adopt orphans and rear them together, often building a nest for their adopted young to settle in.
Elderly & End of Life (Optional)
As Deinocheirus age and enter the twilight of their lives, they develop into Elders. Their feathers lose vibrancy, and they adopt duller hues, something which is most notable in males, whose bright, saturated colors dull significantly over time.
Elder Deinocheirus often take to the water for long, gentle swims. These sessions are not just a comfort, but also a necessity: swimming eases the aches in their aging joints and helps them maintain mobility. Elders who neglect these soothing routines may begin to stiffen and slow, a quiet signal that their journey may soon come to an end.
Losing interest in nesting as their fertility declines, Elders maintain close bonds with their lifelong mate and Struthiomimus companions. Much of their time is spent relaxing by the shore, basking and enjoying their company.
Struthiomimus show great loyalty to their aging friends - bringing them food, grooming them with gentle care, and watching over them vigilantly, as their companions may have done in their younger years.  Deinocheirus continue to adopt Struthiomimus companions even when no longer laying eggs themselves.
If one member of an elderly pair dies, the surviving individual remains alone until their own death, mourning their mate deeply. In many cases, the death of one partner can trigger the impending death of the other, as they cannot bear the loss.
In contrast, adult Deinocheirus (who are not yet elders) mourn the loss of their mate for at least three days before possibly seeking a new partner. However, they always carry the memory of their lost mate - and some adults may choose to mourn their mate for the rest of their lives, never pairing again.
Elder pairs are sometimes seem resting alongside younger pairs, particularly those formed by their own offspring. They lend their wisdom gently, watch over the other pairs, and help teach social behaviors, maintaining a quiet but reassuring presence.
With age often comes stubbornness, and elder Deinocheirus grow increasingly reluctant to leave the water body they inhabit, even if it means their eventual demise. Only the persistent harassment of a Spinosaurus is enough to drive them to migrate, and even then, they do so grudgingly.
Solitary Elder Deinocheirus without a mate or Struthiomimus companions gradually give up on caring for themselves until they pass away.
When they sense the end is near, bonded Elder pairs will withdraw from the communal group and seek a peaceful, hidden place to pass - often returning to the site where they once buried their commemorative stone. 
Elder Struthiomimus may follow, choosing to spend their final days with the companions they’ve known for life.
In these final moments, one partner will often sit close and embrace the other in a final farewell. The death of one is frequently followed soon after by the other, as their bond is simply too strong to survive separation.
With age, personalities shift somewhat, with elder males becoming more irritable and set in their ways, while elder females tend to grow calmer, more nurturing and serene - offering balance to their mate's growing gruffness.

Profile - Quick View
Deinocheirus are Apex Cathemeral Semi-Aquatic Herbivores.
Deinocheirus can group up to unlimited sub/adults, with no offspring or adoptees. This grouping is known as a gathering. When nesting or adopting offspring, a pair splits off to form a separate group to raise their young.
Deinocheirus are passive.
Deinocheirus can have up to 2 in an engagement. They only defend their mate, or offspring under Sub-Adult.
Deinocheirus flees from Spinosaurus, and only defends itself when cornered. Its mate may help defend it if they cannot flee.
Deinocheirus are a highly social, passive species that gather together in large groups with other adults. They form lifelong pairbonds, which are symbolized by a gifting of a rock, which the pair will bury together in a cherished location.
Deinocheirus are extremely good parents and highly protective of their young. Both sexes show keen parental attitudes, and enjoy nurturing their offspring.
Deinocheirus form bonds and friendships with Struthiomimus - who they may mistakenly adopt as smaller Deinocheirus at first, which can sometimes panic the less aquatically inclined Struthiomimus. If allowed to bond, they develop strong relationships that benefit both species.
Deinocheirus enjoy splashing other species from the water. When used against herbivores, this is expected to be a form of play - one that can be either well-received or draw out annoyance from their potential companions.
When employed against carnivores, this is almost always used as a form of defence, aiming to spook the predator away from the water.
