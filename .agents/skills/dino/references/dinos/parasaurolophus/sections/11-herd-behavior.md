# Parasaurolophus – Herd Behavior

Parasaurolophus herds maintain strong internal harmony, where individuals share space with one another peacefully and offer respect, regardless of gender. Bonded individuals - such as harem members, pairs, close kin or tight friendships - often travel side by side together, taking time to groom, nuzzle and call to each other during migrations. Both males and females seek out bonds with others, regardless of sex. They engage in gift giving behaviors, play games of chase, and show affection. Oftentimes, stags take care to offer additional gentleness towards does. They step aside to enable does and their young to eat first, and instinctively walk on the outer edge of the herd to shield them from danger. These gestures are considered deeply respectful and attractive by does, and play into their decisions when it is time to choose a partner for nesting. Mutual grooming is also something observed between particularly close individuals, grooming partners face one another - head to tail - nuzzling and brushing against the other. When resources are plentiful, herds readily accept other Parasaurolophus into their group when they encounter strangers, excited to meet new faces. While Parasaurolophus are social towards their own species, they may tolerate another herd in the same POI for only one full day–night cycle before moving on to seek fresh resources. During this time, herds may interact and even exchange members. Once a herd departs, the remaining herd cannot follow; understanding that the herd's need for resources is too great and rather than cause any inter-herd disputes, will simply part and seek out their own path. If the second herd attempts to leave with the first, the departing herd may sneeze them away in an attempt to deter them from following further and protecting their resources along their chosen migration route. Parasaurolophus also play games of chase even in adulthood, initiated by excitedly calling out and running ahead of the other. One or several others may wish to engage in the game, chasing after the initiator. These games do not ever become physically aggressive and should playmates bite or hit, they are promptly corrected by other herdmates. Once corrected, Parasaurolophus are highly unlikely to display this behavior again to keep from upsetting their position within the herd and risk being shunned. Both does and stags participate in correcting other herdmates when overly aggressive behavior is displayed. Does prioritize correcting unruly fawns, beginning with a sneeze - and then following up with a nip. Stags use their bodies to block other aggressive individuals, and intervene when individuals appear to be going too far.
