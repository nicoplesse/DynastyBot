# Parasaurolophus – Offspring Behavior (Nested)

Baby Parasaurolophus, known as fawns, hatch out of their eggs precocial, but remain nest-bound for the first part of their life, hiding among the dense cover of their nest. Fawns stay very quiet, even as their parents approach them, careful not to make noise in order to draw predators close. Though their mother is often close by, this "hider" survival strategy is particularly useful when adults are grazing elsewhere. Towards the end of the Hatchling phase, fawns start to wobble to their feet and explore the confines of the nest - though only with a parent nearby to supervise them. As soon as Juvenile hits for the youngest fawn in the group, offspring are nuzzled onto their feet and expected to begin migrating with the rest of the herd. This sudden change to a mobile life is overwhelming for many, and juveniles are often clumsy and slow. They are prone to calling out in alarm if they fall behind. Luckily for fawns, mothers are highly attentive to their offspring's needs and rarely let their young stray far. At the first sign of distance building, mothers quickly turn back to round up stragglers, nosing them forward and encouraging them with soft calls. Fawns like to remain close to their mothers at nearly all times, and often panic if they lose sight of her. However, even the most cautious fawn is driven by a strong streak of curiosity. Easily distracted, fawns find themselves fascinated by fluttering insects, bundles of objects, and other species within the herd, and usually find themselves unwittingly landing in trouble! Play games emerge quickly among fawns, with chasing games being a favorite. Fawns race ahead, calling in delight as a "chaser" fawn speeds behind them. Eagerly, the chaser nudges the runner's tail with their muzzle before squealing and switching roles. If play becomes too rough - especially near younger or more delicate fawns - it isn't long before a watchful mother intervenes. An irritated sneeze is given first as a warning, and fawns that ignore this can find themselves nipped by the parent. Strong social bonds start to form between fawns, strengthened through mutual enjoyment of play. Fawns naturally clump together by shared temperaments and interests, regardless of sex. Stags and does play together without friction, forming early friendships that can persist into adolescence and even adulthood if they remain within the same herd. At adolescence, stags begin to test boundaries by sparring with other young stags - and sometimes even adult stags when brave enough. Rushing hormones drive them to practice stagfights, and both individuals seem to take great enjoyment in doing so. Stags peel away from their mothers at this stage, taking a far greater interest in the adult stags of the group - but especially their own father. Young stags begin to shadow these larger males, observing them carefully to learn and mimic their behaviors. This practice extends to non-dimorphic stags and their offspring as well. Does remain close with their mothers, but expand their social circles to all other members of the herd. They learn social cues, spend time showing affection and grooming to other members, and understanding important methods of communication. Stags begin performing in front of does of all ages, often strutting in front of them and shaking their crests to show off. Older does sometimes humor these young stags, offering gentle encouragement with a shake of their head, but some turn their backs deliberately, sneezing to show their disapproval. Due to the strong bonds built throughout their youth, both sexes usually remain within their natal herd unless resources are especially slim. This can also occur when crossing paths with another herd that seems to have better opportunities for them, though is less likely. Once offspring reach Sub Adult, they are recognized as adults within the herd, and are expected to behave as such. This includes night patrols, defensive circles, and active defending as needed. Offspring tend to remain within their natal herd, preferring to stick with those they have closely bonded with their entire young lives. Some may wish to branch off if numbers get too high or resources are too thin for their personal comfort, but are likely to return if circumstances change.
