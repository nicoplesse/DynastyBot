# Pachyrhinosaurus – Offspring Behavior (Nested)

Born into a harem, young Pachyrhinosaurus are entirely dependent on their mothers for survival. At birth, they are deaf, close to the ground, and helpless, instinctively staying near their mother's familiar feet. The father - a dominant male in a shield - is allowed to approach and sniff them, familiarizing himself with their scent before stepping back from any further parental duties. As they grow, the daunting world beyond the nest becomes less overwhelming, and their siblings grow more playful. Both males and females engage in play fighting, facing off head-to-head and standing their ground. Larger, older offspring tend to shove their younger siblings aside, using their size to push to the front of the group when following their mother or getting to food first. Young Pachyrhinosaurus are playful and often mischievous, engaging in shoving matches, snatching food, and chasing each other. Even the smallest among them bravely confronts new things, roaring defiantly as they stand their ground, embodying their defensive instincts. The mother, patient and gentle, encourages her young to move ahead of her as they head to food, which is guarded by their father. When they begin shedding their hatchling skin, the females leave the harem to join other females into a migration again. The eldest female welcomes them with a bellowing roar and initiates the migratory lifestyle once again. Both male and female offspring stay close to their mothers until they reach Sub-Adult. At this age the males develop more distinct traits and are nudged to the back of the migration by adult females. When pushed to the rear, young bulls - once confident - shrink under the authority of experienced older bulls. Following from a respectful distance, they mature with a strong regard for their father. If they meet his standards, they may be allowed into his shield if space permits. By the next breeding cycle, these younger males contribute to the breeding efforts within their father's shield, solidifying their role within the group and the social structure of the Pachyrhinosaurus. Unlike their brothers, young females form strong bonds with other females of similar age, all subordinate to the elder female. They observe how respect and support are shown to elders within the group.
