# Tyrannotitan – Offspring Behavior (Nested)

Newly hatched Tyrannotitan, known as whelps, hatch fully precocial, prepared for the rest of their lives with a full set of well-developed teeth. These teeth constantly fall out and are replaced as the whelp continues to grow, even into adulthood. As soon as they hatch from the egg, they immediately call towards their mother, lifting their noses to brush the undersides of her chin and throat, memorizing her scent and the unique sound of her calls. Once this initial greeting is completed, whelps find a comfortable place in the deep foliage and shrubbery to await the arrival of their siblings and half-siblings, feeding from the regurgitated meat their mother places in the nest. Once Juvenile, whelps have fully recovered from the efforts of hatching and have grown a sizable amount to not be accidentally trampled by mother, or plucked up by predators. They begin to make more sound, testing their own voices (albeit with some voice cracks), and begin to initiate games of chase with their siblings. Whelps become interested in exploring newly found objects with their mouths, particularly eager to chew on them: this includes everything from the chunks of meat their mothers feed them, to rocks, to their siblings, and even their own mothers (though, this is rarely tolerated). Whelps quickly learn that chewing food is unnecessary - and while they may tear smaller chunks from food presented to them - naturally swallow their food whole. As they continue to grow into Juvenile, they begin to express more explicit interest in their siblings and mothers, and start to form bonds. The most common bonds come from same sex individuals within the same clutch, if not with their own mother (if she is interested and has no pair). Whelps bond with their siblings and mothers through a multitude of behaviors, the most common being “mirroring.” Tyrannotitan whelps who are interested in each other naturally copy one another’s behaviors, walking alongside one another in parallel, often with extended periods of eye contact. Female whelps often take part in this behavior towards their own mothers, regardless of whether or not she has a pair. These behaviors are praised, especially if the whelp copies hunting behaviors, displays of confidence, or sparring strategies. The second most popular - and typically the most favoured among offspring - is sparring. Whelps compete with one another for dominance. Temporary alliances may form to rally against larger siblings, which quickly end with a misplaced bite, or simply a satisfying win. Whelps always stop fighting if their mother intervenes while they are Juvenile. Whelps, if consistently hungry (at risk of near starvation - 10% hunger or below) and faced with a severe degree of competition for food, may attempt to kill other whelps in their clutch. This only occurs once per clutch, and stops if the mother interrupts. As whelps near adolescence, they begin to express more natural independence, and show more preferences for specific whelps in their clutch - or their mother. They stop interacting as widely with the rest of the whelps, and often find their own places further from their mother to play and socialize with their bonded sibling. They also begin to display proper gender greetings, by instinct and by copying the behaviors of their parents. Sparring matches with their bonded sibling are quickly interrupted, if not joined in on. Some whelps may begin to show possessiveness - this is most common in female whelps, but may be observed by a particularly dominant male whelp towards a more submissive male whelp. Female whelps form stronger and often healthier bonds, with male whelps naturally preferring other males that are more submissive than them. Additionally, male whelps may be closely observed by the father of the clutch - who maintains a distance, only peeking through foliage and behind trees until properly introduced to the whelps by the female. He naturally prefers the male whelps that show more submission, but may pick another dominant male whelp if choices are slim. Dominant whelps are much louder and more physically assertive than other whelps in their clutch, using their bodies and tails to shove in the way of food, water, and the attentions of their mother. Adolescent whelps begin to move away from their mothers entirely, no longer reliant on their mothers to feed them. However, if granted the opportunity to feed, or threatened, they will show affection - even if only brief - towards their mother. The mothers of the clutches may call the offspring to them and their father(s) temporarily, in order to introduce them. This is when whelps learn and use their gendered greetings, with females mimicking their mothers, and males mimicking their fathers. Some Adolescent males are directly encouraged to follow their fathers. One whelp may group with their father once properly introduced, and continue to travel with him as his bonded partner. Some Adolescent whelps disperse on their own entirely without their mother’s approval, moving into another biome in pursuit of a critter, or purely out of curiosity. Their mothers do not follow, and do not look for them once they are gone. Adolescent whelps in pairs may defend each other if attacked, but will focus on running away if their mother arrives to defend them. Adolescent whelps in pairs may also attempt to hunt solitary Large tier and below. As they reach Sub-Adult, Tyrannotitan often disperse on their own terms with their bonded pair - or remain with their mother or father to act as their pair. However, if whelps linger in the territory for too long, they risk being challenged out by their own siblings or parents. With one final bellow, whelps disperse from their families with their bonded pairs or on their own.
