# Kentrosaurus – Offspring Behavior

Baby Kentrosaurus, known as crias, are on their feet and mobile just moments after hatching, much to the encouragement of their parents. Crias are shy, often hiding behind their parents' legs and keeping to themselves in the hatchling phase. They explore the world by nudging and prodding objects with their snouts, often resulting in their siblings and parents being poked or bumped, as well as other species brave enough to approach them. At juvenile, they start to shed their initial fearfulness and become more confident, straying further from their parents to start more exploration of their environment. This curiosity, led by gentle prodding with their snouts or swiping with their tails, often gets them into trouble, even if it is well-meaning. Crias that are part of a stegosaurid herd with Stegosaurus present quickly learn not to bother their larger cousins - and it is usually these species that they stop "investigating" first. However, crias that herd with Miragaia have a different relationship. Miragaia's patient natures tend to tolerate Kentrosaurus young's cheeky behavior for longer, and naturally they gravitate towards them, happy to push boundaries and receive care from these tolerant dinosaurs. Over time, this relationship only strengthens. Crias instinctively drift towards Miragaia for affection and guidance - especially when their own parents are otherwise preoccupied. Parents and siblings often tolerate their inquisitive nudges and prodding, with crias occasionally bumping spines, tails, or limbs out of curiosity. Other bristle members may not be so tolerant - depending on their individual personality. Crias are incredibly alert, and are quick to recognize safety cues from members of the stegosaurid herd. They scatter when their parents make alarm calls, diving into the nearby undergrowth to hide. This instinct allows them to dart to safety in the surrounding environment, while adults handle any potential threats. Crias often chew on bark, rocks, or even the tails of larger herd mates - sometimes provoking exasperated responses from adults. Like other stegosaurids, Kentrosaurus juveniles begin engaging in play as a means of learning important skills. They often spar with one another, turning their small, flexible tails towards each other in mock displays of strength. These sessions are brief and rarely escalate to physical harm, with most tail swings sweeping far enough way to ensure they don't hit one another. Should a cria land a hit against another, both crias will call out in alarm and rush to the side of a caregiver for reassurance. Crias also mimic their parents, trying to copy feeding patterns, tail movements, and even defensive postures. Their attempts are often clumsy but met with encouragement from adults. By the time crias reach adolescence, they become far more coordinated and confident in their abilities. With more pronounced spines, and the beginning of physical maturity starting, they begin testing their tails in earnest, using them as a tool for both defense and communication. Adolescent Kentrosaurus spend more time with others of their own age, forming loose, mixed-sex groups for social interaction and grazing. Though still bonded closely to their parents and caregivers, they approach the world with more independence, and seek reassurance much less frequently. As they approach Sub-Adult, Kentrosaurus offspring are driven out of the bristle by other adults unless there is room for them to remain, and they are not related to all others in the group. If there are no viable future breeding partners for them, Kentrosaurus offspring themselves will willingly leave the bristle. This behavior is reasonably amicable, with both parents and other adult members shaking at the newly matured young and swiping their tails in the air. They are not outright aggressed unless they are especially persistent. Other recently maturing stegosaurids may be grouped with, forming a new stegosaurid herd. Recently mature offspring may or may not group together when they leave at Sub-Adult. They usually splinter off into smaller groups of no more than 2 or 3 related individuals, leaving enough room for them to encounter others for breeding opportunities.
