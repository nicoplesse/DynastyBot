# Ceratosaurus – Courtship & Parenthood

Ceratosaurus are promiscuous courters and no jealousy exists within a pack; males do not challenge one another for courting rights. Courting takes place at dusk when the sky is pink and Ceratosaurus feel an excited with the incoming night as they start to wake and ready themselves for patrolling or hunting. Courting is initiated by females, which pick a male with qualities they admire; this may be a male she has formed a close bond with, one with particularly striking patterns with contrasting colors, bright crests, or one who has proven himself an experienced hunter. Both male and female Ceratosaurus find scars to be an attractive quality. Females start to exhibit more flirtatious behaviour such as stalking low to the ground towards a male she is interested in, and catching his attention, before stretching out to display herself. A male that is interested in accepting her advances to court will stretch himself out in response to her, and perform excited head-bobs repeatedly. Females scrutinise the display carefully and if they choose to accept, they move closer into his personal space with soft friendly-calls. The male leads the female away to a more private location where they sit together and exchange affectionate calls before building their nest. Females will not approach other males to court once they have chosen and been accepted by one male. They remain loyal to their chosen partner for the duration of the courting event. Males do not have any particular preference in the appearance of their chosen mate, choosing instead to respond favorably to females that have shown skill in hunts and dedication to the pack. However, both male and female Ceratosaurus find scars to be an attractive quality. Ceratosaurus with melanism are considered female, regardless of gender. Males with melanism are never courted due to their lack of bright crests. Melanistic females are treated without prejudice. Albinism is considered sickly and individuals are shunned. They are never accepted into new packs and they are never courted. Once mated, the pair may form a temporary bond and while in the process of building the nest and incubating the eggs, the male will sometimes offer choice pieces of meat to the female to ensure the healthy gestation of their young. When the female is ready, she lays her eggs at the den-site, ideally within a cave or sheltered rock formation. Once eggs are laid and hatchlings are born, the male and female end their pairbond and resume the normal social behaviour of pack life. When hatchlings are born, adults within the pack approach carefully to inspect the new brood and greet their new pack members. This behaviour helps strengthen the pack bond, and allows each member to learn the new scents of their whelps. The mother and father of the clutch have no special part in raising the young, this responsibility is shared equally among all pack members. Biological parents keep some awareness of their bloodline, and will refuse to mate with individuals that are too closely related. Multiple nestings are preferred, as the raising of offspring is shared. If the pack notice that a pair has decided to mate, they often feel encouraged to choose a mate themselves. Nests are always built side-by-side to encourage whelps to mix together and bond. As offspring reach Sub-Adult, the pack prefers to keep on as many members as it can. If the group limit is reached, the existing adults feel the pressure of having too many mouths to feed and will decide unanimously to evict aged-out offspring if necessary. Pack adults gather together and face off the new adult, calling out aggressively to make sure they are not misunderstood. If the individual does not understand, the adult may push forward and snap at the air to get their message across.
