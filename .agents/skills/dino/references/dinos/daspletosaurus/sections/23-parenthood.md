# Daspletosaurus – Parenthood

Mothers and females in a pack are the primary caregivers of offspring and form loose bonds to help raise young. Females are typically more affectionate and understanding than their male counterparts. Mothers do not stay near their nest, and in fact may join hunting parties even if their offspring are newly hatched. This is an effort to draw predators' attention away from their offspring and clear the area of any lingering scents. Fathers do not partake in caring for offspring. Instead, they will provide food for the females of his pack and protect the area around the nest. Mothers will deliver chunks of food for their cubs and often spend only a brief amount of time checking on the hatchlings before leaving the nest. Once cubs are Juvenile, mothers become more involved, coaxing them from the confines of the nest to explore the immediate area. Any critters nearby may be herded by the mother towards her offspring who are encouraged to hunt the - often harmless - creature. Unwilling to remain in one spot for long, the pack resume their nomadic lifestyle once all the cubs have reached juvenile. During the pack's travels, all females within the pack help rear cubs, delivering guidance and corrections. Males - while remaining mostly aloof to the cubs - do not hesitate to step in to protect them if they are attacked. If a cub is recognized as Albino by the dominant males in the group, the males rush to separate it from the rest of the cubs with fierce snarls. Mothers may attempt to get between the cub and the males, standing strong and snapping at the faces of the males' in attempt to deter them. If they continue, or nuzzle her in order to get her to back down, she eventually reluctantly backs off, returning to her other cubs and shooing them away from the sight of the albino cub. After this, the males cannibalize the cub. Males only take specific interest in their cubs if they do not already have a dominant male pair, and will attempt to bond with a male cub. If the cub takes no interest or flees to its mother, the male will not pursue. Cubs who attempt to interact with males are ignored, often shown by the male looking up and away from the cub; making themselves as uninteresting as possible. Adolescent cubs are permitted closer to the adult males to observe hunts and to chase small tiers and below creatures that cross their path. Mothers will still keep on eye on their cubs at this age, but may be more willing to let them explore further. Cubs who chase or explore too far are called or herded back, redirecting the cub back to the safety of the pack.
