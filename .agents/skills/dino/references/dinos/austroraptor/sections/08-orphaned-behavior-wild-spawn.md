# Austroraptor – Orphaned Behavior (Wild Spawn)

Hatchling orphaned Austroraptor struggle severely with survival; with often no siblings to assist keeping them warm, and no parents to tend to them. This continues into Juvenile, where chicks are able to move around and look around on their own, but are unable to swim and therefore cannot fish for themselves. These chicks rely on stomping on crabs and other small aquatic critters, as well as feeding on shells in order to supplement their diet. Orphaned Austroraptor struggle to survive and thrive in any capacity; and miraculous survival to adulthood often results in a highly anxious and more aggressive individual. Some orphans are fortunate enough to discover an unattended Austroraptor nest. In these circumstances, chicks can force their way into the nest, rubbing against their new "siblings" to absorb their scents, and hopefully achieve adoption. Whether or not the potential parents accept them depends on the adults' temperaments and suspicion levels. Nests that suddenly gain numbers can prompt stress and confusion in parents. If, with an orphan, a nest does not exceed 4 offspring, orphans may be accepted if the parents are not overly suspicious, and treated as a late hatcher of the Austroraptor's own clutch. However, if the orphan pushes the nest to 5 or more offspring, this newcomer will quickly find themselves regarded with suspicion, aggressed, exiled - or worse, slaughtered outright. In adulthood, orphans struggle with socialization and may require extended periods of observation at the courtship grounds to learn how to socialize and court. Once paired with another individual, they are possessive of their partner. Austroraptor are feathered dromaeosaurids who migrate the entirety of Riparia and Gondwa, primarily along waterways and beaches. Austroraptor are active both at night and during the day, but are often heard issuing long whistle calls at night, and seen huddling together around offspring and the most fragile of their groupings. Due to an Austroraptor’s incidentally high-salt diet, Austroraptor must expel salt out of their bodies through spitting. In addition, they may do this as a show of distaste towards predators and other Austroraptor, and is often used as a method of correction for incorrect behavior. Austroraptor huddle together to preserve warmth often, especially when raining, snowing, or at night. Austroraptor may “claim” herds or carnivore packs to follow, keeping a careful distance, in order to steal their offspring. Every individual Austroraptor has a unique voice that is used for recognition amongst family members and courted pairs; even at a great distance, bonded pairs are always able to recognize each other’s voices. Each Austroraptor generally exhibits more dominant or more submissive behaviors; however, this may shift over their lifetime and from situation to situation, depending on their individual personalities. Austroraptor enjoy deep diving under the water to fish and to root for shells, as well as to play with their family members. Austroraptor stand motionless in water to observe for fish and other small critters and on the beaches, using their wings as shade to attract them. Fallen trees above the water are prime hunting spots. Austroraptor stand atop these logs, waiting for fish to swim by and dive directly into the water to snap up their prey. Sometimes, Austroraptor will linger around a dead tree, occasionally calling in hopes to attract attention to it, waiting for a larger creature to force the log over. Same-sex female pairs are common in Austroraptor, given the higher ratio of females that hatch in comparison to males. Austroraptor are highly intelligent and learn hunting strategies quickly, working together with their pair to develop unique hunting patterns. Austroraptor examine new things and new species very closely, and even may take the time stalking a new species or herd in order to locate weaker prey. Austroraptor are aloof about most others, but sometimes play with each other by chasing and nipping at one another’s tail feathers. At times, Austroraptor may drown prey, and comparatively, offspring. See Aggressive Behavior - Infanticide. Austroraptor stomp on eggs in nests that they find while migrating. Lifelong bonded Austroraptor spend a great deal of quality time together, sitting, cleaning and fishing together, nearly always at each other’s sides. Austroraptor are most comfortable when with others of their kind. Though they will not group with more than one adult Austroraptor, several pairs and individuals may remain within a POI together as a siege.
