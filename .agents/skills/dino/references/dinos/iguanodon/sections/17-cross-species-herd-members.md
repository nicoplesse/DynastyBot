# Iguanodon – Cross-Species Herd Members

Parasaurolophus and Lambeosaurus are an Iguanodon's favorite species to group with, and they are always accepted into the herd when encountered. They show affection to these individuals and seem to enjoy their company - but other Iguanodon are always put first. Iguanodon prioritize themselves and their own species over others. Though they have a natural tolerance - and even enjoyment in some cases - for the species that they herd with, Iguanodon will push themselves and their young to the front of food resources, often cutting across the Parasaurolophus or Lambeosaurus they herd with to ensure they get to eat first. They never necessarily guard resources from these two species, but selfishly try to ensure they are the first ones to get access to them. Iguanodon do not defend any of the species they group with. If those species are attacked, Iguanodon will move away from the engagement, preferring not to get involved. Iguanodon are stubborn, and rarely back down from a fight. Solitary Iguanodon opt to flee rather than fight when in the presence of 3+ Large carnivores or 1+ Apex carnivores. Solitary Iguanodon of Adolescent or below flee from 1+ Large or higher tier carnivores. Otherwise, Iguanodon do not flee when grouped with their own kind, unless success appears unlikely. This judgement may vary across individuals. When fleeing, all Iguanodon may still defend themselves, but must focus on escape. When a predator is spotted, all Iguanodon begin to broadcast loudly, alerting the rest of the herd to the potential threat. It is at this time that the 3 potential defenders will form, rushing to the front of the group and joining the chorus with angry growls. Standing to their full height, these defenders begin their threatening pacing, bellowing loudly and stabbing at the air in an intimidating display. Iguanodon are intimidated by 5+ Small, or 2+ Medium carnivores, but if they experience these carnivores in lower numbers they ignore their presence. Bulls (or cows, if there are not enough bulls) act as the primary defenders, with up to 3 Sub/Adults pacing in a defensive position at the front of the herd. All other members bunch together tightly behind these bulls and retreat slowly from the fight, keeping their bodies close to minimise exposing each other to the predators. The 3 defenders remain in a bipedal stance, pacing back and forth in front of the herd and growling at the predators, as well as stabbing at the air in an attempt to warn them off. The defenders use a combination of front and rear attacks once the engagement begins, but most favour a bipedal stance to slam their powerful arms against threats, and pierce through hides with their thumb spikes. Kicks and tail attacks are more commonly used as a defensive gesture when being pursued, but are still used offensively if the predator attacks from behind. These defenders can chase and charge at the attackers, so long as the attackers do not leave line of sight of the herd. Iguanodons in groups of 3 or fewer individuals do not chase and charge at the attackers - as there is no herd to defend. If the attackers forfeit or reach deathscars, defenders can chase down and continue attacking until the attackers leave line of sight of the herd. When there is ample warning of a predator approaching, mothers prioritise stashing their offspring in nearby bushes and undergrowth; concealing them and keeping them out of harm's way, before joining the rest of the herd in bunching together defensively. If a predator discovers the location of the stashed young, they call out in alarm and flee to her in an attempt to join the rest of the defensive group. If the predator is attempting to attack a calf within one body length of the mother, she can step forward to defend them, but will not pursue or chase down the predators once her calf has moved to the safety of the group.
