# Iguanodon – Offspring Behavior (Nested)

Iguanodon offspring, known as calves, are born dazed and confused, tumbling into the world and greeted soothingly by their mother's soft, encouraging calls. Taking a moment to steady themselves, they soon return her affectionate calls, and work quickly to stagger to their feet. Although wobbly at first, Iguanodon calves quickly adapt to their newfound mobility and stay as close as possible to their mother and siblings. Some herds stop and rest during nesting, but others quickly resume their nomadic lifestyle, so calves must be capable of following along with the adults if the need arises. Iguanodon calves prioritise one thing above all others in their lives - their mother. Calves follow behind their mother at all times, acting as a form of shadow, and remain careful not to stray too far. Iguanodon offspring that find themselves too far from the comfort of their mother will call out in alarm, and desperately search until they can find her again. Once they reach juvenile and the herd approaches a rest stop, calves begin to play with one another. Young chase after each other, vocalise, show affection and engage in varying different games. Overly boisterous calves may get scolded by another member of the herd if their play is deemed to be too rough - or perhaps even annoying. Calves that are rebuked always rush sheepishly back to their mother. Calves also spend a lot of time gathering gifts for their mother, further cementing the bond between mother and calf. Different foods and objects get presented to her with calves eagerly awaiting their mother's approval - and sadness if greeted with her disapproval. Calves seem to have limitless energy at juvenile stage, and can become restless when the herd settles down to sleep due to their cathemeral body clocks. Other members of the herd - especially night patrolling bulls - encourage calves to calm down and rest beside their mothers and siblings - but some calves might require a firmer suggestion if they stubbornly refuse sleep. Calves usually show affection and enjoy socializing with any member of the herd that will accept them. Juvenile is a prime age for fathers to start bonding with their offspring, and calves happily accept and greet their fathers if they pay them attention. If the herd is in a mixed species grouping, Iguanodon calves happily investigate other species with curiosity. Play is especially initiated with Parasaurolophus and Lambeosaurus young due to their similar sizes. At adolescent, calves begin developing more independence from their mothers, but still seek her affection and comfort out at all times. During migrations, adolescents continue walking beside their mothers, but young bulls in particular pay special attention to the adult bulls in the herd - especially their father - and are keen to copy any calls and behaviors they display. Cow calves, meanwhile, prefer to remain with their mothers, and mimic her behaviors. Bull calves of both juvenile and adolescent age may act out bull fights if they witness them. This usually happens directly after watching a bullfight between two adults, but ends much quicker, with no more than 2 stabs each that land on their opponent. Both calves rush back to their mothers afterwards to soothe their wounds. Upon reaching Sub-Adult, calves are considered fully fledged members of the herd. Bulls, eager to prove themselves, are quick to rush forward as defenders of the herd. Both sexes typically stay with the herd for their whole lives, forging strong bonds and friendships.
