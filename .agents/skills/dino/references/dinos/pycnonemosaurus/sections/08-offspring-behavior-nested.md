# Pycnonemosaurus – Offspring Behavior (Nested)

Pycnonemosaurus offspring, known as shoats, are bold and tenacious from the moment they hatch out of the egg. Their first instinct is to get up onto their feet, and immediately begin exploring their surroundings - which is usually in a nest surrounded by dense coverage - or sometimes a cave if their parents have found a large enough one to nest in. Pycnonemosaurus are demanding from the start: as they grow quickly, their demand for food is near constant and they make this known by noisily calling for it from their bewildered mother. Their boisterous nature drives them to explore anything and everything around them. They frequently pick up and bite at whatever objects are nearby - anything from their parent's feet or tail, to siblings, to branches and foliage. Shoats are loud and rambunctious; their mother doesn’t interfere with small scraps and playful roughhousing, but if more than a couple of bites land on a sibling, the mother steps in to provide a stern telling off. Once the little shoats reach Juvenile, they are brought out from the safety of their nest and introduced to the rest of the drove. This brings great excitement to the youngsters, and they greet all new experiences with boundless energy. When the Juvenile shoats are on their feet and exploring freely, the drove resumes its nomadic lifestyle and nests are deconstructed so that the group can move on. On the move, shoats keep up with their adult drove members - usually sticking close behind their mother but often getting distracted and following any adult sized Pycnonemosaurus that they have been introduced to. Shoats are difficult to teach, as they get so distracted by anything that draws their attention. When parents attempt to teach their young to hunt, it often ends up with startled prey and excited shoats calling out with joy. By adolescence, Pycnonemosaurus offspring are expected to begin hunting critters and start being able to find their own food instead of being provided for by the rest of the drove. Shoats that try chasing down their own critters may accidentally draw themselves far from their drove members, and if they realize this, they call out fearfully and charge back in the direction their family was last seen. Mothers may not even realize their shoats had gone, especially if they themselves were distracted by something else. At Sub-Adult, offspring are welcomed to stay if there is enough room. Otherwise, the mature offspring will drift away from the drove, exploring further away and seeking out a smaller drove to join, or starting one of their own.
