# Deinosuchus – Courtship & Parenthood

Only fully grown adults are able to court and nest. Deinosuchus males call nearby females into their territory on most nights during the rainy season if they are unpaired. They broadcast loudly, snap their jaws, splash the water and let out long purring noises in a bid to attract a mate. A female that hears the male's calls and is receptive to courting will follow his broadcasts, and call out to him as she approaches, letting out low growl and purring of her own to signal her presence. The male continues his display and splashing, thrashing his jaws on the surface to create sharp slapping sounds, and lifting his head up high to expose his throat. Females will observe the performing male for a while, swimming very slowly and carefully as she investigates. Especially scarred males can prove extra impressive, and help sway her decision. Should the female show no interest, she simply moves on from the territory, leaving the still-performing male behind to seek out a different mate. However, if she deems him an impressive specimen, she rises to the surface, mirroring his body language by lifting her head to expose her own throat, and letting out low, deep rumbles. Once noticing her interest, the male stops his performance, and moves closer, bumping his snout against hers and returning affectionate calls. Should both parties be content with their choice, the pair will then swim together briefly, keeping themselves close, with the male leading the female around the borders of his territory to search for a suitable nesting location. They group together in game, forming a temporary pairbond. When a suitable nesting location is found, the male will shower the female with affection, and assist her in collecting the necessary resources to build the nest, which they construct together. After its construction, the female lays her eggs and remains close to the nest at all times, only breaking away to eat - but quickly returning afterwards. The male resumes the patrol of the territory, and begins driving out both males and females that dare to enter. Once hatchlings begin to wiggle out of their eggs, the female no longer leaves the nest to hunt or fish, and resolves herself to guarding her brood fiercely, knowing that she will not eat again until her young are mobile. She lays beside the nest, casting her eyes over them protectively, and carefully nudging any hatchlings back inside that get a little too close to the edge of the nest. Should a baby actually tumble outside of the nest at this stage, the mother will gently clasp them in her jaws and gingerly place them back into the safety of the nest. When offspring reaches the mid hatchling stage, mothers slip back into the water, calling affectionately for her babies to follow. Now with enough strength to do so, hatchlings scurry after her, chirping loudly as they do so. The female then begins to teach her young how to swim, listening carefully for any young that fall too far behind - and is quick to round them up again should they leave too much distance between her and them. The father, meanwhile, busies himself with patrolling the territory. He does not offer input on childrearing, and tolerates his young in his territory so long as they remain close to their mother. Mothers willingly provide meals for their young - ranging from well-timed ambushes that bring down struggling prey to catching fish, which are shared without question with her offspring. This continues up until adolescence - where mothers abruptly stop both providing and sharing food, and aggressing offspring away when they attempt to eat from the same corpse or fish. This behavior helps to prepare offspring for independence as they mature, and encourages them to engage in their own hunts. As young reach Sub-Adult, both parents will begin aggressively driving them out - even attacking if their young do not take the message. Mothers may opt to travel with a single daughter if not wishing to nest with the same male again, but males will not accept a son as a companion in their territory.
