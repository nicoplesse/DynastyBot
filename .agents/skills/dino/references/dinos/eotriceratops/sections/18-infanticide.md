# Eotriceratops – Infanticide

Bulls primarily attack ungrouped and unattended offspring - but, if a calf from his own group is not recognizable or continuously misbehaves despite correction, he will kill it. If there are two bulls with a herd, only one bull may attempt to commit infanticide at one time. The other bull will not take part or defend the other bull from the matriarch or the rest of the herd. These behaviors are pure instinct for a bull, and while he may gain more control over these instincts as he ages and gains more experience with females and calves, he never shows any paternal instinct, care or protection for a calf. The kindest thing he will do for a calf is shoo it back to their mother, assuming he recognizes it. Any offspring outside of the territory of the herd risk being killed. It is essential that a matriarch introduces a bull to his offspring properly – once this is done, he is less likely to commit infanticide. If a bull chases a baby and it goes from unattended to attended (2 bodylengths from its mother), a good, experienced bull will stop - while a young, inexperienced bull won't. This results in harsh corrections from the matriarch or the mother herself in some cases. Bulls have no timers in regards to killing calves, but will be driven out by the matriarchy if they successfully kill one or more calves within 30 seconds of each other. If a bull attempts to kill a calf, the matriarch will charge him, using the block ability to drive him 5 bodylengths away from the herd. She growls fiercely and forces him to back off - even if the bull headbutts her in response, she valiantly continues to push him away. All other females stay away unless the matriarch broadcasts to indicate she would like to shun the bull from the herd, rushing to circle around the herd all calves together and circle them. Females will not assist with driving the bull off from the calf, focused on defending the other calves in the herd, and trusting the matriarch to defend it. If a calf dies, its biological mother stands over the body, calling loudly in her grief. Once to the edge of the bull's territory, the matriarch stomps and aggresses him. A good bull - who will be eventually tolerated again - turns his back and walks away, shaking his frill, showing submission to the matriarch. A bad bull aggresses back - resulting in him becoming shunned by the entire matriarchy. The matriarch will only exile him from the herd if he successfully kills a calf within LOS of any females in the herd. She does not do this for showing aggression towards calves. If the bull has a pair, the other bull has a choice - either he opts to stay with the herd in order to earn their favor, or he goes along with his strong bond to seek other opportunities. Eotriceratops matriarchies enjoy and respect the presence of Psittacosaurus, a small sentry species that happily scuttles about and plays with Eotriceratops calves. In exchange for a Psittacosaurus's protection at night, Eotriceratops cows allow them to utilize them as meatshields, hiding in with the other smaller and more fragile species in order to be defended. If any species hunts a Psittacosaurus that is grouped with a matriarchy, as long as the hunt begins within LOS, 1 Eotriceratops cow may assist in defending the Psittacosaurus. Bulls, when they are present, do not defend Psittacosaurus, and will aggress them away if they attempt to use them as a meatshield. Bulls challenge any species around resources they are actively eating from, drinking from or sitting next to, excluding Argentinosaurus. The bull must issue at least 3 aggressive calls :threaten_call: to the other species before charging. If the species flees the resource, Eotriceratops bulls do not pursue them. When in ownership of a herd, a bull aggresses all species that enter within 5 bodylengths of his herd, excluding Argentinosaurus. The bull may charge immediately once the other species enters the 5 bodylength radius around the herd. Bulls aggressively call :threaten_call: other species to warn them from approaching if they are spotted before entering the radius. Eotriceratops cows do not show aggression towards other herbivore species except when nesting. They will not tolerate any species - besides Psittacosaurus - within two body lengths of their nests, and will fiercely charge and aggress these species away. Cows may do this immediately when the other species enters the 2 body length radius. If a cow spots these species before they get too close, she will aggressively call to warn them from approaching. Once the other species leaves the protective radius around the nest, cows do not pursue. This radius is extended to 10 body lengths for bull Eotriceratops. When an engagement starts, 2 Eotriceratops can take part in defence. They charge forward to the front of the group in order to defend, while the rest of the group moves behind them. Additionally, a mother may defend her calf at any time. If not acting as a defender, the attacked Eotriceratops can retreat to the safety of the herd, effectively being swapped out in the engagement with one of the 2 defenders. Once defenders are chosen, they cannot be changed. Non-defenders being attacked may land a hit to defend themselves, but should retreat to behind the defenders where possible. Eotriceratops ignore small tier and less than 3 Medium tier carnivores, except when nesting.  However, bulls within a herd will not tolerate any species within 5 body lengths of the herd. Upon seeing a carnivore approaching, Eotriceratops begin to issue loud, trumpeting bellows. This alerts the rest of a group to do the same as they swiftly form a defensive position, shaking their frills and bellowing in the same manner. If calves, the elderly, or previously injured are present, they will be pushed behind healthier targets. The two main defenders rush in front of the group, shaking their frills, roaring, and stomping in order to show their intimidating strength, and hopefully deter the predator. The defenders pace back and forth in front of the rest of the group, ready to charge and attack the aggressor once the engagement begins. The rest of the group does not have to move 10 bodylengths from the engagement, but stay behind the defenders, using their bodies and frills as an intimidating wall to deter the predators. If the active defenders move away from the circle, the defensive formation does not follow. In the case that an individual in the defensive circle is desperate hunger or thirst, or does not want to remain in the defensive formation any longer, they may fear call and flee the engagement. They may not return within 10 bodylengths of the engagement, nor may they intentionally approach the active defenders or predators. Should the time call for fleeing, the matriarch (or dominant bull) sounds the alarm, directing the most vulnerable to flee first, before the rest follows. Eotriceratops cows prioritize the health and safety of their group above all else. Bulls, however, can become enraged during a flush of testosterone, and do not flee until the very last second. Bulls may only ignore forfeits from predators if they are on deathscars. Should the carnivore hunting them enter deathscars, bulls seek to charge them down and finish them off. However, they never cross into different POIs in pursuit of the carnivore, and will not chase a predator beyond LOS of a herd he has claimed or the rest of his bull group. Cows at communal nesting grounds: While at the communal nesting grounds, matriarchies defend themselves separately and form separate defensive circles. These circles must be 10 body lengths away from the matriarchy or bull actively being hunted. Any cow can defend any calf at any time as long as it is within 3 body lengths of her -and will rush the calf to the nearest defensive circle, even if it is not her own matriarchy or the calf's.
