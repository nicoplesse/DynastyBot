# Eotriceratops – Offspring Behavior (Nested)

Eotriceratops babies, known as calves, are born in interconnected nests in Broken Tooth Canyon and Rainbow Hills during the rainy season, and Green Valley and Rockfall Hills during the dry season.  They hatch quickly, using little headbutts to break out of their shells and tumble into the world. Calves hatch fragile and weak, surrounded by the sounds of their siblings, aunts, sisters, and cousins from other matriarchies. All new babies are examined with gentle nuzzles by the adult females in the group while they rest to recover from hatching. Once Eotriceratops calves have recovered, they hop up onto their feet. This is when they become properly acquainted with their mother, who will become the best friend for the rest of their life if female, and who they will eventually have to leave if a bull. As they grow further into the hatchling stages, they quickly learn to walk, run, and trot alongside their mothers and throughout the matriarchy, doing so happily. Matriarchies allow intermingling of calves and females, so mothers may take their calves to meet up with friends, sisters and mothers that are in other matriarchies. Calves are often seen playing together, picking up items, running, and charging around together. Calves being hurt send any female nearby (within 3 bodylengths) into alert. Up to 2 females, whether or not their mother, bellow as they rush over in defense of the calf. Nuzzles and soft affectionate calls are quick to comfort it if accidental, but otherwise any calf can be fiercely defended by any female present. Calves will always flee to females first when predators are around, knowing that bulls will not protect them from threats. Bulls are not present in the rearing of offspring whatsoever, and more often than not, offspring are driven away if they approach a bull, or quickly gathered by the matriarch or their mothers. Matriarchies stay at the nesting grounds until all calves have reached Juvenile. The matriarch gathers all offspring in a clump and presents them before her to the bull in order for him to investigate them and recognize them. These initial bonds and inspections are vital to ensure that the bulls do not mis-recognize the calf and end up killing it when it strays too far from its mother. Bull calves are most likely to act out, showing off their frills and mock headbutting in the direction of the bull. Cow calves are less likely to do so, affectionately calling and curiously inspecting their father. Solitary bulls who meet their offspring often look for another bull - preferably a more submissive one -  that they can pair and bond with, in order to better defend themselves and their herds. In the case that Eotriceratops do get to meet their father, they stay very close to the matriarch, watching as he approaches and inspects them. Their behavior during this stage strongly influences whether or not the bull reacts with aggression and refuses to tolerate the offspring. Following the bull’s acceptance, the herd forms, and the entire group begins to migrate across Gondwa again. If two migrating matriarchies leave at the same time, they go opposite directions, not wanting to incidentally share resources with the other. Juvenile calves by this stage have steadily increased in confidence, forming tight bonds with their mothers, other adult Eotriceratops within their group, and their siblings. Calves happily play with one another along migration routes: chasing, calling happily, and performing ‘charging’ contests - where calves line up and fiercely charge into rocks, trees, and stumps to determine who is the strongest. Calves also love to jump, kick their feet, and chase one another. Bull calves are more boisterous than their female counterparts, showing a lack of fear and curiously approaching anything they are unfamiliar with in order to investigate. More often than not, their method of investigation is headbutting repeatedly, to see if the thing (or other dinosaur) will buckle or flee from their strength. Female calves, however, are much more timid, preferring the proximity of their mother or another adult female in the matriarchy rather than rushing out to meet anything. They enjoy picking up items and carrying them around as a source of comfort, and can be seen picking up these items even into adulthood. As they age into adolescence, bulls begin to show a bit more real aggression. They begin practising bull fights with brothers and half brothers in the matriarchy, and fiercely protect their sisters from perceived threats and otherwise. Any aggression shown towards any cows in the matriarchy is very quickly corrected by the matriarch – and repeated aggression may result in a bull being driven from the herd prematurely. Adolescent bulls also seek out attention from and approach their fathers, who are now permitted around offspring. While initial exploratory reactions may be tense; as young bulls develop, fathers teach their sons to bullfight - carefully observed by the mother and matriarch. Sons are also taught proper patrolling behavior, and how to defer to the matriarch. If the father is not interested in taking part in parenthood whatsoever - or is otherwise absent - sons learn these behaviors from the matriarch instead. Adolescent bulls begin to form small dominance hierarchies amongst themselves at this time, through sparring and posturing. Adolescent bulls also begin to travel slightly separately from the herd, with the most dominant of the group following closely behind their father, with the others budging and shoving one another behind him. Adolescent females engage in mutual grooming, and learn the signals and behaviors necessary for survival. They additionally tend to copy or mirror the behaviors of the females around them, especially adult females or the matriarch, whom they love and respect dearly. At times, if younger calves are present in the herd, adolescent females will take part in allomothering - learning to protect and care for other calves in the herd. They tend to them gently and even help bring the calves food, and use their bodies to shelter them in the middle of an engagement. Sub-Adult Eotriceratops are inspected by the matriarch as they reach adulthood. She considers each calf closely in order to determine if they will stay within her matriarchy, or if they must leave in order to find their own groupings. If a female calf is selected to stay with the matriarchy, she will affectionately call and shake her frill. The cow then has the option to join with the rest of the matriarchy if she would like to stay – otherwise, she returns the affectionate call but then turns her back, moving on and away from the others. If a female calf is not selected to stay with the matriarchy, or the calf is a bull, the matriarch will broadcast with the rest of the matriarchy echoing her call. This is treated as a final goodbye, and with a final bellow to their birth group, the calf turns their back and moves on. Male calves are immediately sent to disperse, and typically form bull groups - small social groups of males who continue to develop their bullfighting skills and establish strong bonds that help them in courting and impressing other females as they get older. At times, their father may opt to go with them, as fathers disperse at the same time as offspring. These departures never escalate into a full conflict, and bonds remain between females even if separated, resulting in them affectionately greeting one another when the cycles repeat at the communal nesting grounds.
