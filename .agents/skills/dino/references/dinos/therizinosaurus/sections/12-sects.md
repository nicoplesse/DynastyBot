# Therizinosaurus – Sects

Sects, on the other hand, only move until they can form an established territory. In migrations and patrols a sect is always led by the most dominant or eldest Female Therizinosaurus in the group, called a Dame. This female is often the most sought after by males, and tends to have brighter colors or more intense patterning on her feathers. She is the first to respond to territory challenges and to defend the group from predators. The Dame is also responsible for establishing patrols of the Sect’s territory, which all Therizinosaurus will join on a nightly basis. These patrols are to maintain their territory’s boundary line, eat, and drink. It is very rare, if ever, that a Therizinosaurus that is a part of its sect will stray far from the patrol established by the Dame, unless nesting with chicks who are unable to move. A Therizinosaurus sect is made up of the Dame, her daughters, sisters of the Dame, and unrelated males. Granddaughters of the Dame are made to leave when they are a subadult. A Therizinosaurus sect will defend its territory fiercely, clawing trees and stones in order to mark its territory around the border, and patrolling it nightly. They will always return to the same spot to sleep and nest throughout the daytime. The Dame within a sect maintains a high social standing within a group of Therizinosaurus, and is most frequently the target of the most affections. The Dame will more often than not entirely reject affectionate advances from her daughters in the group, preparing for the day where she may challenge her for the position of Dame. Roosters within a sect will always provide the most attention and affection towards the Dame, unless driven off or away by her for any reason. This can be because he already has chicks by another female, or if she does not like the gifts he is bringing her. Other females within the sect, or unrelated females in search of a sect, may choose to challenge the Dame for ownership of the sect. This will be detailed in **Aggressive Interaction.
