# Quetzalcoatlus – Orphaned Behavior (Wild Spawn)

When orphaned Quetzalcoatlus hatch, they patiently and instinctively wait for a parent to eventually return. When they never do, they begin to call and clack their beaks in desperation. These chicks often are seen running around on the ground, ducking into bushes and pecking at corpses to survive. Orphaned Quetzalcoatlus are typically not strong enough to fly until they reach Juvenile. At that point, they will instinctively find a cliff to throw themselves off of, doing so again and again until their wings catch the air and they fly for the first time. Orphaned Quetzalcoatlus will mainly scavenge and hunt critters, until they are large enough and bold enough to attempt to push another creature off of its prey. Orphaned Quetzalcoatlus are often sharper, more cunning, and independent than their nested counterparts. They will often be slightly more possessive of their resources (resting places, favored perches, food, etc) and will peck at other Quetzalcoatlus that attempt to intrude on their space. Orphaned Quetzalcoatlus chicks will avoid all larger Quetzalcoatlus to avoid being eaten. However, broody same sex female pairs may chase down unwilling orphans to forcibly adopt them. This is quite frightening for the orphan, but eventually they learn to accept their new parents. Orphaned Quetzalcoatlus chicks will often mainly scavenge, but will not engage in bullying individuals off of their corpses until they are full grown. Quetzalcoatlus can often be seen perched on tall rocks or trees, observing other creatures from a distance and sunning their wings to cleanse themselves of parasites. These massive flying creatures move quite quickly on the ground and when choosing to engage with prey, will land in order to pursue and impale them on its large beak. Quetzalcoatlus are almost always on the move, looking for prey to follow. Their travels often follow migrations or carnivores, looking for the opportunity where a body could drop to feed from, or a weakened carnivore they could challenge for the prey they’ve hunted. Quetzalcoatlus will frequently choose the path of least resistance, and will avoid conflict with other, larger carnivores when possible. If they can easily find a hunt or food opportunity that takes no real effort on their part (bullying small carnivores or a free dead body), they will do so. Quetzalcoatlus are not driven in any form of the word. They let their wings guide them and the wind lift them to where they need to be. They are very passive and casual, and are more willing to just let things happen to them. Males are typically more of a show-off than their female counterparts. They are louder and flashier, and will proudly broadcast after bullying someone off their corpse or hunting prey. They take pride in their bullying and may briefly form groups with other males to stalk large prey, or to bully larger carnivores off of their hunts. They are always looking for females to impress and often will abandon current mates and nests to seek other opportunities. Males often die young due to their impulsiveness and ego. Females are quieter and more cunning, but can be impressed by the males’ gaudy displays of confidence and ego. They are strategic and watchful, and will prefer to wait out carnivores and herbivores until they are completely exhausted. All decisions made in a female’s life are with specific reason, from hunts, to mates, to how she raises her children. Females who have made it far in life are often approached by young, excitable males. Albino and melanistic individuals will only be accepted in their adulthood, on the basis of strength or intelligence. Quetzalcoatlus chicks who are melanistic or albino are always at risk of being tossed from their nests. Quetzalcoatlus are opportunistic creatures, and will often take advantage of any weak points in a herd or in another Quetzalcoatlus that they can identify.
