# Dryosaurus – Offspring Behavior (Nested)

Baby Dryosaurus, known as kits, hatch blind, deaf and entirely dependent on their parents. During their earliest days, they remain nestled inside the safety of the warren's nesting cave, fed on fresh berries brought by their parents. These berries are available to all kits, even if their parents hold a submissive position in the warren. Too weak to stand, kits spend their time sitting or lying in the nest, trilling softly to attract their parents' attention and request food or comfort. Once their eyes open and their hearing develops at the end of the hatchling phase, Dryosaurus imprint on the first adult they see - which is almost always one of their parents. As kits grow into juveniles, they develop the strength to stand and take their first, tentative steps. Short walks help build their muscles and prepare them for active lives ahead. Quickly, kits become more agile at this stage - with their swift legs often outpacing their developing instincts. Kits eagerly venture beyond the nest, following the dominant members of the warren and their parents. They observe and mimic these adults, helping to learn essential survival skills and social behaviors. Kits are notoriously badly behaved and can develop selfish and spoiled attitudes. Adults within a warren typically expect this behavior, but new parents may be stressed if their kits continually misbehave. More experienced Dryosaurus from within the warren do not hesitate to step in and correct particularly unruly young with an aggressive call or firm bite. Young Dryosaurus rapidly grow into their species' feisty attitude, and quickly learn from the adults to aggress most other dinosaurs around them, even when the likelihood of success seems slim. Kits frequently engage in mock battles, chasing, nipping, and roughhousing with one another. These games are loud and chaotic, but they serve to establish pecking orders and develop coordination for future conflicts. Especially bold kits may even attempt this with adults, but are usually swiftly corrected! When stalked or hunted by a predator, kits below Sub-Adult scatter in all directions, only regrouping in a safe spot once the perceived threat has passed. This chaotic escape strategy serves as a form of self-preservation, lessening the chances they will get singled out by a predator. As Dryosaurus reach adolescence, their diet shifts, and their once protected access to berries no longer applies. At this stage, adolescents must forage independently, often testing their learned behaviors. Even children of the dominant pair no longer have access to berries at this stage, unless gifted by the dominant pair. Attempts to assert dominance - usually through copying dominant members of the warren - are quickly suppressed by the dominant pair, wary of potential challenges to their leadership. Adolescent kits form tight-knit groups with others, working together to chase, harass and even kill nearby critters in attempts to prove themselves as valuable members of the warren, as well as hone their own skills. By Sub-Adult, Dryosaurus kits are considered fully fledged members of the warren, but do not fully mature or show any interest in courtship until they reach full Adulthood. Dryosaurus almost always stay with the warren they were born into, and very rarely disperse.
