# Dryosaurus – Orphaned Behavior (Wild Spawn)

Orphaned Dryosaurus kits are at high risk of starvation or predation due to their inability to forage or defend themselves effectively. Without a caregiver, their chances of survival dramatically decrease. They vocalize frequently, desperate to attract the attention of a parent or caregiver. Sadly, this usually goes unanswered, and after some time trilling and cooing for help without response, they fall silent. Assuming they manage to make it through the hatchling phase without succumbing to predation or starvation, orphaned Dryosaurus have slightly better chances of survival - particularly if they manage to find a warren. Luckily for them, orphaned Dryosaurus are always adopted by other Dryosaurus, of either sex. However, as Dryosaurus open their eyes and have their hearing mature at the end of the hatchling phase, they end up imprinting on the first larger dinosaur than themselves that they see. When they are born safely inside a warren, this is always one of their parents and helps bond them deeply to that parent. Orphans, sadly, imprint on whatever species they come across first - as long as it is larger than them. Imprinting Orphaned Dryosaurus imprint on the first larger species they see, with the imprinting window closing sharply by adolescence. If a Dryosaurus imprints on a predator, the outcome is almost always fatal. If they imprint on a species that tolerates them, Dryosaurus kits become bold, possessive, and essentially a relentless nuisance - a true challenge for their unwitting "parent." Dryosaurus kits are incredibly needy and possessive. They do not tolerate any other species - even the adopted parent's own biological children - and frequently aggress them away when they come close to their parent. Without the structure of a warren and normal social interactions between other Dryosaurus (including reprimands for bad behavior), kits sadly grow into unruly, defiant troublemakers: spoiled, headstrong, and completely unmanageable for their adopted parent. They may attempt to copy their adopted parent's behaviors, but with a heightened level of aggression and possessiveness that surpasses the species they imprint on. Possessive behavior can even extend to chasing away potential courting partners from their adopted parent, or even attempting to destroy any nests they build. Only when they come across other Dryosaurus does this behavior cease. Imprinted Dryosaurus can be prized away from their adopted parent by the approach of others of their kind - kicking in an instinct for the orphan to follow these familiar figures instead. Dryosaurus are highly territorial and possessive over whatever they deem to be their property at the time. This can consist of: rocks, various objects, partners, caves, and food resources. This does not include water sources. They are prone to stealing shiny objects, plants, or even food from other species. They often hoard these items in hidden "treasure" piles. Dryosaurus spend their time migrating across various POIs in search of excitement, feeding opportunities and mates to court. They lay claim to caves to use as a home, and defend them fiercely from other species. They move as a clique, often massing together in great numbers and choosing to travel with each other, lead by a dominant individual or pair. At least one individual - usually the most submissive of the group - is relegated to act as sentry as the group move and feed. They perch on top of a high object near the group and look around for danger. When danger is spotted, the sentry lets out a loud screech, prompting a chain reaction across the rest of the group to call out in response, and become alert. Dryosaurus patrol the edges of the warren's claimed cave, chasing away intruders with exaggerated postures. They spread their arms, employ vicious hisses and stand in wide stances designed to make them appear larger and more threatening - a comical sight for most species that tower over them in size. In the dry season, Dryosaurus adults spend the hottest parts of the day resting in shaded areas or near water sources, becoming more active during cooler mornings and evenings. In the rainy season, during particularly cold evenings, Dryosaurus individuals can be observed huddling close together for warmth. During heavy rains and storms, adults lead the warren to sheltered areas like rocky outcrops, burrows, or dense thickets to avoid prolonged exposure. When solitary, Dryosaurus tend to be less bold than when they are in a group. They trail after herds in search of berries and muscle their way in to claim prime spots. This tolerance for other herbivores is only ever temporary - and to suit the Dryosaurus' own benefits - as it quickly disappears when they find others of their kind. As Dryosaurus grow in numbers, this meekness sheds almost entirely - with individuals displaying bold and fearless personalities, seemingly heightening in confidence the more of them grouped together. This bravado - including picking fights and aggressing away dinosaurs much bigger than them - sometimes puts them in dangerous situations. Dryosaurus are very vocal, and use loud, high-pitched vocalizations to intimidate rivals or signal their presence. Once in a pair or more, Dryosaurus fiercely defend their share of berries, plants, or scavenged items from other species, often hissing and screeching at any dinosaurs that approach them, and even going so far as to nip them should they not take the message. Dryosaurus are highly inquisitive, and are interested in any new object or species that they come across. If the item is potentially claimable, they will move to collect it and hoard it from other species almost immediately. Dryosaurus take short naps in frequent bursts throughout day and night. They prefer to do this in shifts - with at least one other standing watch over them while they sleep, and repaying the favor in kind as the responsibility moves. These species can become somewhat of a homebody if a particular POI is especially favorable to them. Low threats and competition are desirable for Dryosaurus, and they may temporarily remain sedentary in a POI if signs look good.
