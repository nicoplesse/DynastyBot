# Tenontosaurus – Offspring Behavior

Baby Tenontosaurus - known as fawns - spend their first hours in tight-knit sleeping bunches, eagerly cleaned and kept warm by excited males. One by one, once they have gained enough strength, fawns stand up and slowly begin exploring the nesting side, often stumbling as they learn to walk. Despite tumbling often, fawns are completely silent until Juvenile. Fawns exhibit a natural curiosity; juveniles explore their surroundings in tight and clingy groups, supervised under the vigilant watch of their fathers, who they form deep bonds with. Should a fawn find itself alone or stray too far from the rest of the group, it begins to cry out in distress and settles down on the ground, hoping to gain the attention of a nearby guardian to return it to its bundle of siblings. Behavioral dimorphism does not begin to kick in with fawns until adolescence. Before then, both bucks and does treat each other similarly - usually cuddling for warmth and comfort at sleep periods. All fawns enjoy short games of chase - a game that in turn helps to refine and strengthen their leg muscles and improve agility - and siblings take great joy in racing around with one another and any adults that might indulge them. As they approach adolescence, brothers and sisters begin to create distance with one another. This separation is natural as temperaments differ with hormonal changes, and instinctively siblings start to find more enjoyment socializing with others of the same sex. Brothers form closer and more enduring bonds, with bucks engaging in cooperative play, and mutual grooming, reminiscent of their more submissive temperaments. Sisters, on the other hand, demonstrate looser and more competitive bonds, engaging in playful but more challenging activities, such as wrestling and practice battles -  games that enable females to test their strength and hone their skills with one another. Towards the middle of adolescence during a peak of flooded hormones, does become restless. They kick and bite at nearby objects, bellowing loudly as their tempers fray. Fathers step in to correct these rowdy daughters with nips whenever they bellow, while the matriarch pushes them into joining patrols and challenges. Bucks develop quite differently, and instead begin to show a much more nurturing and playful side. They loosely follow different adults in the glade, mimicking their actions and inviting them to play. In addition, they take great interest in presenting their fathers with gifts and dances. Upon reaching Sub-Adult, does are permitted to remain in the glade if the matriarch deems them as submissive enough - especially unruly females are always pushed out quickly. Bucks always stay in their glade - at least at first - and learn to care for new young. Eventually, they disperse to find a glade that they can breed in. The matriarch sometimes becomes more irritated when she feels her sons have stayed for too long, and will chase them out if she decides they have overstayed their welcome.
