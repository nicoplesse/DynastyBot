# Suchomimus – Offspring Behavior (Nested)

Baby Suchomimus, known as snappers, hatch out of their eggs ready to take on the world with gusto! From the moment they tear free from their shells, snappers are noisy and mobile, clambering onto their feet to begin their first, initial dash towards water. With nests cleverly positioned at the water's edge, fresh hatchlings have little to no distance to cover in order to slip beneath the surface. Snappers are incredibly self-sufficient from the second they hatch, and are fully capable of survival with or without a caregiver. Some Suchomimus navigate the world entirely alone, doing so with an almost alarming competence. However - many Suchomimus are lucky enough to have attentive parents. Those that do keenly observe their adult caregivers, absorbing all learning and survival experiences hungrily. They trail after their parents, screeching and pleading for food - despite being more than capable of catching fish themselves. Snappers loosely stay together with their siblings and may share fish and kills with each other. More often than not, the bond remains a fairly shallow one that decreases as they age. Sometimes, certain siblings may grow closer and choose to stick together even as they enter adulthood. Snappers are highly opportunistic, and think nothing of helping themselves to food from other species. Especially bold young may even attempt to snatch fish or meat scraps directly out of their parent's mouths. As they age, Suchomimus rapidly learn that adults show concern when they let out fear calls. Some individuals take this further than others - and may even end up drawing out the opposite effect from their caregivers, who might opt to tune out snappers that complain in excessive doses. Suchomimus young adore mud. When not swimming, fishing or otherwise causing chaos, they can often be found wallowing, sliding and playing in muddy banks - lathering their scales in a thick coating of it. In unique circumstances, sometimes Suchomimus parents position their nests as close by to nesting Spinosaurus as possible. When snappers are born into nests in close proximity to Spinosaurus, they follow their strong smell and rush over to the Spinosaurus nest, hoping that the placid Spinosaurus will take them in and offer them an easier life. Due to this, all snappers naturally gravitate towards Spinosaurus. They do not imprint on them specifically, but they do instinctively recognise the benefit that comes from being near Spinosaurus and are more than happy to play their part in deceiving the Spinosaurus into caring for them.
