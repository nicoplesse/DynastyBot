# Albertaceratops – Offspring Behavior

Young Albertaceratops, referred to as cubs, are born weak and vulnerable. They spend their first moments lying in the nest, waiting for their clutchmates to hatch. The chirping sounds of nearby cubs pique their curiosity, prompting them to stand on shaky legs and explore. Excitedly, they venture out of their nest, greeted warmly by nearby sows resting near their own nests, celebrating the arrival of the new generation. The dominant female, perched on her elaborately decorated nest, is often the first to receive visits from the newborns. Cubs eagerly investigate her trinkets and play with other hatched young nearby. Cubs exhibit different behaviors based on gender. Young sows engage in playful chases and friendly calls, often gravitating toward the dominant female and other nearby sows. Young boars, on the other hand, are boisterous and engage in mock fights, instinctively butting their crests together. If a young sow is accidentally included in these rough games, her surprised cries prompt a swift response from the dominant sow. With a firm snort, she reprimands the offending boar, teaching him his place within the group. The chastised boar, undeterred, redirects his energy to playing with other boars while carefully avoiding sows who might land him in further trouble. Adolescence is marked by the shedding of juvenile skin. Young sows often mirror their mother’s coloration, becoming near replicas. The dominant female shows particular affection toward these daughters, allowing them to rest close to her during naps and forming strong, lasting bonds. These look-alike daughters are more likely to step into leadership roles in her absence, earning the respect of the herd due to their shared appearance with the dominant female. Even as offspring reach early adulthood, their mothers continue to watch over them. Mothers guide them to food and water and provide support, especially as young females prepare for their very first clutch. Related Albertaceratops females maintain the strongest bonds, creating a tightly knit social structure within the herd.
