# Albertaceratops – Social Interaction

Hierarchy within the sett: Albertaceratops groups are called a sett and are led by a dominant sow and a dominant boar. The dominant boar is always a fit breeding partner chosen by the dominant sow. Rivalry between boars earns the dominant boar rights to the dominant female, and her choice in him is his greatest accomplishment. The Dominant Sow The dominant sow is the driving support of the sett. Her age and experience guide the group within their complex social hierarchy. Adorned with scars, she is well respected by sows and seen as the matriarch of Albertaceratops groups. She chooses where the nesting cove will be placed and teaches other sows and young how to forage, where to migrate, and which males are best suited for breeding. How a dominant sow is chosen In a new sett, where the sows' social hierarchy is not yet established: The dominant sow is decided by whoever has the most allies and is the strongest in the sett. This female is naturally confident, pushing other sows off food and showing clear leadership skills during migration. In a perfect grouping environment, her pushy nature is supported by her close allies and look-alike offspring who help reinforce her position in the pecking order by submitting to her more dominant behavior. They show submissive headshakes and heed her decisions to eat first, migrate now, sleep here, or nest in this spot. The reinforcement from sows helps the dominant sow claim her position in a new herd. Similar sows with the same appearance as the dominant sow can lead other herd members to mistake them for her, which helps establish respect within the group. Challenges for the dominant sow position Sows refusing to submit to the dominant sow will stand their ground and fight back. With a single smack on their rival, the challenging dominants will square up and roar in defiance at each other. Both sides are supported by allies who join the intimidation displays of the sow they respect most. Even boars will pick a female, as this show of support increases their chances of being chosen as the dominant boar - should their chosen female win. The winner of this confrontation is whichever sow has the most allies. Once sides have been chosen, the loser is forced to turn away, signaling defeat. If both sides have equal allies or a losing dominant sow refuses to remain with the winner, the dominant sows will disband their group and start a new sett with their allies, often bringing other groupmates fond of their company. With two separate sett groups, each considers the other a rival and will push them off food bushes. Sows Within the sett, sows may bicker over food and resting spots, but once a dominant female emerges, she enforces group order. In small setts with only one or two sows, a dominant sow often does not exist, as both females rely heavily on each other for companionship and protection. Bickering is only common in larger setts, where multiple females feel they deserve respect. New sows joining the group New sows joining the group and offspring joining the sett immediately rank lowest in the social hierarchy. They earn favor by befriending other sows and staying on the good side of the dominant sow. The Dominant boar The dominant boar earns his rank by competing with other boars in mock fights and dominance displays to prove his skill and earn respect. Only the choice of a dominant female solidifies the dominant boar's rank. The dominant sow chooses the dominant boar based on his prowess - boars that win a lot of engagements are highly attractive. Once chosen, she affectionately calls him to her side to lead the sett on their migrations. The dominant boar uniquely aids the rest of the sett in adoption of orphaned offspring. If the sett encounters an orphan - or worse, loses a member that has offspring - the dominant boar steps forward to welcome the young, and aids to pair them with a suitable sow as a surrogate mother. Boars All other boars - alongside the other sows - walk behind the dominant pair during migrations. Though they don't contribute significantly to the group's leadership, boars play key roles in guarding the herd during migrations and rest hours. Boars eager to rise in the sett will compete with those not currently on a courting timer. Boars often wait until a nesting ground is reached to challenge for courtship rights or when rival dominant females contest the top position, as new female leadership can increase their chances. Solitary behavior: Solitary Albertaceratops behave similarly whether grouped in a sett, among other ceratopsians, or alone. Albertaceratops with mutations often face solitude from an early age, either hatching from abandoned nests or being shunned from their herds. When alone, their aggression toward threats remains the same, with individuals weighing the risks of standing their ground or chasing predators. Orphaned offspring from other herds are often accepted by solitary Albertaceratops, who value the company and responsibility. If other species nest nearby, a solitary Albertaceratops sow may instinctively create a cove to shelter hatchlings.
