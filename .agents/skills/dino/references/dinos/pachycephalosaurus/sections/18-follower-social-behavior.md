# Pachycephalosaurus – Follower Social Behavior

Comfortable in their lower-ranking role, followers avoid confrontation and are quick to yield when approached by a more dominant individual. If another draws near, the follower will quietly rise, offer a soft chirp in acknowledgment, and move aside. During migrations, followers tend to trail closely behind a confident individual. They don't take the lead, but their quiet loyalty is unmistakable. They rely on their chosen leader to decide when and where to move. When under stress, whether from predators or aggressive herbivores, more reserved Pachycephalosaurus usually hold back, waiting to see how their leader reacts before responding themselves. The constant need to relocate and avoid conflict can be stressful, so shy individuals often seek comfort by resting alongside other quiet companions. If their approach is rejected and the other moves away, followers respect the boundary and soothe themselves by shaking their bodies, a calming gesture. Patience often wins out, and followers tend to warm up over time, especially once the band has settled into a new safe spot. They seek out familiar faces to share warmth during rest. As yearlings mature, many maintain their follower tendencies, though some gradually become more assertive and take on leadership roles. It's common for this transition to occur sometime after reaching adulthood, while others retain a quieter nature well into their elder years. Disputes between females, typically between those with the "leader" personality,  often escalate into physical scuffles. A well-aimed kick may send a rival stumbling, while a headbutt punishes those who linger too close. Often, being knocked from a ledge is enough humiliation to make a challenger retreat. However, some are too proud to accept defeat and will return to fight again. Fights between females end once one reaches death scars. At this point, a 30-minute cooldown is applied to both individuals. No other females may issue a challenge to the winner while this timer is active. The Residing Buck Just as females can have personalities, so too does the buck within the band. Some bucks prefer to avoid conflict and follow the same personality type as followers. Non-dimorphic males always avoid conflict and never partake in challenges. Others burst with the same fiery confidence of leaders and even stand up to females who bully them. A buck who initiates and defends themselves from spot challenges by leader-type females engage in 1 on 1 fights. A 30-minute cooldown is applied to both individuals who took part. Male Challenges Rival males  wait for the right opportunity to challenge the current buck for his position in the Band. When the Band has found a suitable rock or cliff to rest on - the challenger will make his move (caves are never suitable locations for buck challenges, and the challenger will wait for the right opportunity before approaching). When ready, the challenger approaches with purpose, bellowing loudly three or more times to announce his intent. The current buck will never leave his spot on the cliff or rock to approach the challenger, and always stands his ground until his rival is close enough to hit. Nearby females move away to avoid being caught in the challenge. The duel is physical and intense. The goal is simple: to knock the other off the ledge, and inflict significant damage. The first male to reach death scars must yield. Victory is announced with a triumphant scream, while the loser must retreat 10 body lengths from the group. If the defeated male refuses to leave, the females themselves will step in to drive him off. (The loser must be removed from the in-game group, and the victor added immediately.) Females do not form lasting bonds with adult males and never intervene in male challenges. However, if a defeated buck remains too close after a contest (within 10 body lengths), they will not hesitate to chase him off and enforce group boundaries. They show interest in movement and activity, but never attempt to join or bond with other species. Their involvement is passive - observing from afar without seeking contact or offering assistance. If confronted or attacked, they retreat without resistance -  unless claiming a territory. Even when alone, Pachycephalosaurus may follow other herbivores purely out of boredom or curiosity. But despite their presence, they remain outsiders - never integrating into the social structure of other herds. Only at night, when isolation feels most vulnerable, will a solitary Pachycephalosaurus break this pattern. Drawn to the safety of a sleeping herd, it may quietly settle near a resting herbivore, using their bulk as a shield against the dark. If met with aggression or rejection, the Pachycephalosaurus does not retaliate. Instead, it moves off respectfully, continuing its search for warmth and safety elsewhere. When confronted by a predator, Pachycephalosaurus insinctively respond with aggresive displays if given enough time to react. As a species well adapted to steep cliffs and towering rocks, being caught on flat ground can sometimes be a death sentance. When bitten, Pachycephalosaurus react by kicking or charging to force the predator away, and open up an opportunity for them to flee to a more defendable position. A raised rock, cliff, or the safety of a cave is ideal. Once secure, their posture shifts from prey to aggressor. With renewed confidence, Pachycephalosaurus bellow loudly, smacking their skulls against stone in a noisy show of force, aimed to ward off further pursuit. Should the predator approach the chosen spot, Pachycephalosaurus leap from their perch to drive the predator away, using the raised terrain to their advantage. Knowing their skill on this type of challenging terrain, Pachycephalosaurus have adapted to battering attacks aimed to knock the predator off their perch. When ambushed by predators, yearlings often lag behind. Naive and prone to wandering, they rarely grasp the danger until it's too close. They are the last to scramble to safety - only fleeing once attacked or bitten by the predator - and rush to nearby cliffs or rocks for protection. Offspring and yearlings are the most vocal when in danger, and squeal and call out in distress. They do not partake in physical attacks -  and never defend themselves when targeted and bitten/attacked. Instead, they flee for cover or hide behind older group members - freezing or fleeing depending on the space available.
