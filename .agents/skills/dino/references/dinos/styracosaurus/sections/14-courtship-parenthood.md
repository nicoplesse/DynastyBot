# Styracosaurus – Courtship & Parenthood

When the herd is stopped in one area for some time, male Styracosaurus begin to initiate courtship. They do this by finding an open, visible space and making loud calls, while scratching their horns at the surroundings, trying to attract the attention of females. Stags begin their typical sparring matches to try and drive some interest from the does in their harem. When a stag has no potential sparring partners, he may turn this display of strength towards trees or rocks - charging and headbutting them to show off his prowess. Even stags that lose sparring matches can draw out attention from their does - the excitement of seeing fighting can cause females to want to nest. Unclaimed does with no harem approach a stag and call affectionately to him after observing the bullfights, hoping to be accepted into his harem. Stags usually always accept - but sometimes, if a harem has at least 5 does already, the dominant doe can become selective, and showcase her displeasure by aggressive calling at the potential new addition. Stags may opt to ignore this behavior from the dominant doe and still accept the new doe - but such a decision prompts the dominant doe and all other does supporting her, to shun their stag for a while, in what can only be described as sulking. Does always settle down after some time, but only when the dominant doe decides she is ready to accept the new member. Some stags may pay heed to the dominant doe's displeasure, and reject the new doe, forcing her to approach another stag in hopes for acceptance. Once a doe is suitably wooed or impressed, she approaches the stag in her harem to affectionate call, shaking her head and walking her body closely alongside his to signify her interest. Stags then respond affectionately in kind. The dominant doe  leads the harem to a suitable place to nest - this can be alongside other harems, or other species in the group if she so decides. Mothers are very protective of their fawns while they are young, but tolerate the presence of fellow does and their young from the same harem. This tolerance is not always extended to the stag leading the harem, however, if he is deemed to be inexperienced or too aggressive for fragile fawns. Fathers enjoy interacting with their young - when they are permitted to - and forge bonds with them. Their influence on childrearing is slim in the early stages, but holds more weight as fawns age - especially with their sons. Fathers tend to go more gently on their young sons when they begin practising for bullfights, but are happy to offer them the challenge. Once fawns reach maturity, they become fully part of the herd. Mothers willingly expel their fawns outside of the harem, but happily socialize with them at other times. Daughters are expected to join other harems within the wider herd, while sons are expected to form their own. Most does happily accept orphans and adopt them like one of their own, regardless of the age of their current young. After watching their does' affectionate behavior to the new baby, stags willingly accept these orphans as part of their own family.
