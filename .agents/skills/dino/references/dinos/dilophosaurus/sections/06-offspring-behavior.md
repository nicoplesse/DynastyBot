# Dilophosaurus – Offspring Behavior

Dilophosaurus hatchlings, known as pups, hatch into the world in a warm, safe place, tucked into the safe crook of their mother alongside their siblings. Shaking the remnants of the egg from their scales, they quickly move closer to their mother, cooing and squeaking softly. Dilophosaurus pups tend to remain immobile for a while, only sleepily wobbling up when they realise the absence of their mother or the movement of one of their siblings. They begin to move around more, meeting their half-siblings (if applicable) and other mothers shortly after mid hatchling. Dilophosaurus pups quickly learn that crying out gets them food brought to them, and often over-do it with this behavior, overwhelming first time mothers. More experienced mothers easily tell which noise is to make noise, and which ones are genuine cries for food. A mother will feed her pup by regurgitating what she has recently eaten - done by depositing food into the nest - when the babies are young, as they do not quite have their sharp teeth meant to easily shred through prey. As they grow, she may bring them small critters or chunks of meat to gnaw on, until they are able to eat it fully. At Juvenile they begin to properly socialize and play with one another, quickly learning the meaning of each call as established by their parents and by the rest of the pack. They practice each behavior each call directs, especially when commanded by their mothers, and learn quickly. Deaf pups will struggle with this, but may gradually pick up on body language. This is also when Dilophosaurus pups begin teething. While the process is painful, they are easily soothed by chewing on things - sticks, bones, meat, or their siblings. Dilophosaurus pups remain under the watchful gaze of their mothers at all time, or otherwise risk being nipped at and scolded as they are ushered back to the group. While the pups are curious and eager, when corrected they learn and respond quickly. At Adolescent, Dilophosaurus pups are introduced to the dominant male of their leash - their father. While he expresses very little interest in them, they circle him eagerly, jumping over his tail and attempting to initiate play. The kind of response the pups receive depends on the male's personality - but most of the time, they are met with calm indifference. At this time is when they meet the rest of their leash: typically older adult siblings who greet them with fondness and excitement. They are shown how to hunt with practice on critters and other siblings, and may even as they grow older be permitted to join in on an actual hunt. However, the adults in the group quickly usher them away if they become too severely injured, even to the point that they will forfeit the hunt to protect the pup’s life. Adolescent Dilophosaurus pups are nearly entirely self-sufficient and can provide for themselves, and are encouraged to stray from their mothers to socialize with other older siblings, the core aspects of their social groupings. From this point on, they are raised communally, with all members of the leash working together to feed, raise, and take care of them. When Dilophosaurus pups reach sub-adult, they have two options: to remain with their leash, or to disperse in order to form their own leash and head off to the courtship grounds. This typically depends on the size of the existing Dilophosaurus group and how much interest the individual has in courtship or nesting.
