# Maip – Offspring Maturity

Once pups come of age at Sub-Adult, Maip parents will have stopped providing food for them entirely, and begin aggressing them away from their kills - and themselves. Before the final dispersal, parents take their "pick of the litter". They assess all of their freshly grown offspring, measuring them and determining whether or not they want them to remain in the group. Health, ambitiousness, and curiosity are factors that are analyzed in choosing a favoured pup, and are qualities that are chosen at the discretion of the parents on what they favor the most. Parents only rarely choose a new favored offspring to remain if they already have one with them. Often times, that offspring disperses on their own to find a mate and to form their own skulk, and rarely are they pushed out in favor of a younger sibling. If they do get pushed out, they are body blocked by their parents, who turn their backs on them, lavishing affection on the new chosen offspring. This can be somewhat hurtful for the former older offspring, but they leave willingly, understanding that their place has been lost. Like many other nomadic species, Maip are particularly prone to arthritis due to their life on the move. Difficulty with moving long distances, and requiring more frequent rests is commonplace with elderly Maip. Lifelong pairs of Maip frequently spend the end of their days together, spending more time curled up with one another in a den rather than focusing on rearing pups. Male Maip become more docile and focused, with their shy and timid nature reducing to a content, yet attentive wariness. Females become much less aggressive, looking to ignore or avoid combat with other females if they can handle it. Older Maip often struggle with issues with their teeth, due to their love of gnawing bones. Cracked, broken, or even missing teeth result in reduced bite force. These Maip often refuse to use their teeth in combat or hunts, opting for use of their ever-strong claws. Maip in a mated pair often rely more and more on their partner - though pairs are usually formed by individuals close in age. With both members of a pair aging, they continue to hunt and provide for one another, although their older years prompts them to focus more and more on easier prey.
