# Rhamphorhynchus – Orphaned Behavior (Wild Spawn)

Rhamphorhynchus chicks who hatch alone face high predation rates, unable to easily flee from their natural predators unless they are near a water source. They are typically only capable of survival if a sauropod or large herbivore herd come across them. They quickly learn to be self-sufficient by the time they are juvenile, and are able to swim and to fish quickly. They do not begin to fly until they are juvenile, and begin to do so by instinctively seeking cliffs to throw themselves off of, and having the air catch their wings. These Rhamphorhynchus typically form close bonds with sauropods, especially if growing up with them - viewing them as pseudo-parental figures. By the time they are adolescent, they are adept flyers and fishers, and are capable of aerial displays. However, they do not court or nest until they reach Sub-Adult in age. Rhamphorhynchus are cathemeral in order to best fit the needs of their high metabolism, but enjoy traveling and exploring best at night. They have well-developed night vision and enjoy resting in completely dark areas, such as caves and small holes in the ground. Rhamphorhynchus saliva has anticoagulant compounds that prolong bleeding and inhibit blood clotting, as well as compounds that prevent the re-construction of damaged blood vessels. In order to combat their constant hunger, some Rhamphorhynchus may take part in hematophagy - the consumption of the blood of other animals. Sounds are unique from individual to individual, making them easily identifiable within their colony. Mothers quickly recognize, and gravitate to, the sounds of their offspring. Rhamphorhynchus are exclusively fishers and scavengers. They dive both within salt and freshwater sources, hunting for insects, worms, crustaceans, salamanders, small fish, frogs, and other small reptiles. Rhamphorhynchus develop mutualistic relationships with large herbivores across Gondwa, particularly sauropods such as Amargasaurus, Ampelosaurus, Apatosaurus and Argentinosaurus. These unique relationships are seen most commonly amongst Rhamphorhynchus when it comes time to breed and nest, as Rhamphorhynchus will choose to nest nearby sauropods for the benefits it poses to their offspring. In return, Rhamphorhynchus clean their herbivore companion's nests and bodies, ensuring they remain free of parasites and other mess. Rhamphorhynchus can be observed attempting to copy and mimic all sounds that they hear, whether they come from other animals or foliage. While this mimicry may not always be accurate, some older Rhamphorhynchus are able to mimic the sounds of young dinosaurs crying out. Rhamphorhynchus do not exhibit much emotional or actual intelligence, but instinctively go completely silent at the sight of a predator, quickly dispersing with the rest of their colony to make room for any hunt that may occur. Only if another Rhamphorhynchus is attacked will they shriek in fear or fury. Rhamph Sting Rhamphorhynchus often carry fungal spores in their feathers and on their talons. While most Rhamphorhynchus keep themselves clean and well maintained in this aspect, others may opt to use it as a method of attack or defense for their allies. Rhamphorhynchus may latch to another species with Plague Carrier and/or Infectious Touch active. They will remain latched to their target for as long as possible, unless bitten, out of stamina, or shaken off. Once shaken off, a 30 minute timer begins. Rhamphorhynchus begin to die at this stage, not immune to their own fungal spores. They are unable to cure this and are unable to “sting” again. See Death under Elderly Behavior for more. For other species, being stung by a Rhamphorhynchus means itchy scales, skin, or feathers, and at times a more irritable manner. Continuous scratching of itchy places may result in raw skin and turn to infection. These stung creatures also grow hungry and thirsty faster. Depending on how long the Rhamphorhynchus was latched, the effects of the sting fade after the next in game day cycle.
