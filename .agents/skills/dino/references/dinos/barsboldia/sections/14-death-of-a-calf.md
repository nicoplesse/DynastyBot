# Barsboldia – Death of a Calf

Should the worst occur, the entire herd calls out in sadness and grief at the loss of an offspring. This mournful song goes on for some time, but the most mournful song of all comes from the mother. Mothers fall into very deep despair at the loss of a calf. They stop eating, and walk very slowly with their heads down, crying softly. Herd members try their best to console the grieving female, but little can replace a lost calf. Driven by pure maternal hormones, grieving Barsboldia mothers go on to adopt the next orphaned offspring under Adolescent they encounter. This does not have to be a Barsboldia, and can even be a carnivorous species. This still occurs even when the mother has other young still alive. This adoptee is treated just as lovingly as if it were a Barsboldia calf, and is fiercely defended by the mother. Most of the herd overlook this intrusion but keep their distance, though some bulls - particularly the lead bull, if there is one present - will try to drive them out. The entire herd will assist in driving out this adopted offspring once it reaches Sub-Adult. If the species is not a threat to Barsboldia, it may be permitted to trail behind the herd for a while, but herd members use their bodies to block access to its adoptive mother, breaking the unusual bond. Mothers fight to defend this "calf" at first, but always relent, calling out with soft cries as she is separated from her adoptee. This process is always easier for the mother when the offspring is Sub-Adult, but herdmates soothe the female with affectionate nuzzles and calls, and she eventually overcomes the separation. Barsboldia nesting ground. A male can be seen patrolling around the nest sites for threats. Barsboldia bulls tend to deteriorate in age quicker than Barsboldia cows -  especially solitary bulls, who have no others to take care of them. During the rainy season, females start to prefer grouping with their children or sisters when tolerated, or those that they have formed strong bonds with. They show less interest in nesting, and more interest in nurturing offspring of their sisters and family members. At times, groups of elderly females may band together to lead multiple separate herds, helping new parents with their young and establishing migration paths. Barsboldia females prefer to lead their groups along rivers and water sources during the rainy season. When a Barsboldia ages, saturated colors on their skin tend to desaturate. Barsboldia will stay close to elderly members of their closely bonded groups, and will often times stay by their bodies when one passes, unless aggressed away by a predator. They nuzzle their fallen group member’s body lovingly and will cry sadly to it. When the body disappears they will depart, mournfully bugling up to the sky as they move again.
