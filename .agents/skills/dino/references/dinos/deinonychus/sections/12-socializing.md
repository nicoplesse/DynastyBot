# Deinonychus – Socializing

Socialization within Deinonychus groups are complex and heavily influenced by sex and status. Males form deep, lifelong friendships with other males. It's not uncommon to see pairs resting with their necks draped over one another, or grooming for extended periods. Same sex bonds between males frequently occur due to this closeness. These males spend nearly all their time together, and express deep sorrow at the loss of a partner. Despite this, partnered males may still accept courtship advances from a female within a dynasty, but quickly go back to their male partner afterwards. Male relationships are generally peaceful, with disagreements resolved very differently to female disputes. Rather than fighting, males tend to express their frustration through avoidance, sulking, and withdrawing affection. A male who feels neglected may refuse to groom his companion, deliberately sit apart from them, or ignore attempts at social interaction until the relationship - or issue - is repaired. Female social dynamics are far more volatile. While some friendships between females form, competition is a constant undercurrent. Jealousy between females is common. Females often dislike sharing a male, particularly with sisters or daughters, and fights over courtship, grooming attention, and even sleeping spots are frequent. Likewise, jealousy is equally common in males, particularly when a close companion begins to spend more time with another individual. Unlike females, this never escalates into violence. However, males do not interfere with female choices - though they may reject courtship - and do not compete for status among themselves. Males may frequently feel jealousy among each other if a female picks another male over them. Some males may resort to sneaky tactics - such as covertly stealing a gift out from under another male - to present to a female. Lower-ranking females, especially those overlooked by the mother, often band together. These small alliances serve as both emotional support and a strategic effort. They groom each other, share scraps, and collectively work together to isolate a rival in an attempt to climb the social ladder. Alliances among females are fragile, however. A female who begins to gain the mother's favor may be turned on by her former companions in a scramble for status. Female friendships are often tested by courtship conflicts. If a male gifts a female or overly grooms her - and the other female had grown possessive over that male - that female may respond jealously with tail-fanning, biting, or shunning both the male and female temporarily. Social grooming is frequent and reinforces bonds between individuals. While most feather preening is self-directed, close individuals (especially males to other males, or males to females they are trying to win favor with) groom one another regularly. Grooming is also used to calm group tensions after disputes.
