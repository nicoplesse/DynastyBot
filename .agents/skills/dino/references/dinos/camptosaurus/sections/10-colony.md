# Camptosaurus – Colony

Camptosaurus live in large groups known as colonies, occupying 1 POI known as the colony ground. These colonies are loosely maintained, and Camptosaurus are free to come and go as they please. The area regarded as the primary food and water spot within the POI is known as the community ground. Community grounds act as a social, neutral space for all residing Camptosaurus, where Camptosaurus come to sunbathe and loosely interact with each other. It also acts as the courting ground and an area where offspring are brought to be first introduced to the colony. Within the colony ground, each Camptosaurus claims a small territory, known as a home range. This claim is often a desirable cave, clearing, foliage or area around one’s nest, which they defend aggressively from other intruding Camptosaurus. These territories are typically close to the community ground. Territories are maintained and decorated to signify its claim. Camptosaurus regularly patrol the edges of their home-range, rubbing against trees, rocks, or foliage to mark its borders, and are known for plucking overgrown vegetation, or shoving undesirable items out of their territory. Camptosaurus take great care in ensuring their home-range, and their nest in particular, are tidy and beautifully decorated, as these efforts signify a keen eye and a healthy Camptosaurus that is able to maintain constant upkeep. These claims never include large water sources or berry bushes, and any Camptosaurus found attempting to claim either will be shunned, with repeat offenders getting chased out by the whole colony. A Camptosaurus preference for the size of the claim will vary between individuals, depending on their nature and tolerance of others. Regardless, these territories never exceed 5bdl of distance, which can cause large caves and clearing to be claimed by multiple Camptosaurus. Home ranges can be challenged by other Camptosaurus, though these challenges are far more frequent between males than females. Male Camptosaurus within a colony live under a hierarchy, built up of dominant and subordinate figures. This rank determines their rights to the most desirable home ranges, to females, and security to food and water during harsher periods. Dominant males are aggressive to other males, centering their lives around capturing the interest of females within the colony and maintaining their place in the hierarchy. They earn their position through frequent challenging and bullying of other males. These challenges are most often for desirable home ranges owned by other males, but challenges may arise out of bullying behaviour too. A male does not necessarily have to come out as victorious to earn or maintain the dominant status, simply portraying these aggressive behaviours are enough to indicate it. Frequent losses will place the male lower on the hierarchy, but they still rank above subordinate males. Subordinate males are tolerant towards other males, valuing the safety of residing in a colony as opposed to placing focus on courtship and nesting. These males still defend themselves against dominant males, but do so infrequently, so as to keep attention away from themselves. They may still court and nest, but their meek nature means they often get overlooked in the process, acting as stepping stones for a dominant male’s display instead. Female Camptosaurus live largely in peace with everyone. They are not affected by male hierarchies, except when it comes to courting, and have no hierarchy between themselves and other females. All Camptosaurus may challenge each other for a home range, but males often avoid challenging females to avoid losing favour during courtship. Males that continuously act aggressive towards females may be noticed by the dominant males, who will in turn bully the offending male in hopes of earning favour next time the females seek a nesting partner.
