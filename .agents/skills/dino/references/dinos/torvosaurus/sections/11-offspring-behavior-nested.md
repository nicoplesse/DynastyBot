# Torvosaurus – Offspring Behavior (Nested)

Torvosaurus young, known as cubs, are bold and energetic. Cubs can move and navigate their surroundings soon after hatching, where they begin to explore anything and everything. They are noisy young, and spend much of their time squabbling with siblings, picking up random objects, and biting at anything that moves. Playfighting begins practically as soon as cubs hatch. This behavior requires frequent intervention from adults at first, as cubs lack the understanding of boundaries and when to stop - sometimes resulting in an accidental fatality if the adult caregivers are not quick enough to intervene. With repeated corrections, cubs quickly learn to limit their playfighting to safer levels, avoiding excessive harm (stopping before 50% Health). Female cubs tend to grasp this rule more quickly, while males often require more frequent reminders to adhere to the boundaries. Torvosaurus cubs exhibit relentless curiosity, frequently venturing beyond the immediate safety of the nest. Their boldness can attract unwanted attention from predators, requiring vigilant protection (and rounding up!) by the adult caregivers in the group. Female offspring form strong bonds with their sisters and other female cubs. This behavior can be observed from early on, where biting and squabbling gradually phases out to affectionate gestures and coordination with one another. They begin to band together to confront small threats - ranging from critters to unusual objects - showcasing their developing teamwork and communication skills. Males remain quite independent from other cubs and notoriously nippy. They are often the loudest and most confrontational in the nest, constantly brawling and pushing other cubs around. They enjoy tussling with their father, and hassle all other cubs within the pack to play fight at all times of day. Despite their boldness, males are also highly attached to the adult females in the group, particularly their mother. The females indulge the young males’ clingy nature with tenderness, though this dependence gradually diminishes as the males near adolescence. In contrast, female cubs show a greater tendency toward independence from adults from a younger age. A nest comprised of only male offspring may be especially loud, causing the adult females to feel quite strained under the pressure of providing for such demanding young. Female siblings strengthen their bonds through cooperative play, including mock hunts that mimic real predation strategies. These mock hunts may involve ambushing their brothers, testing their reaction times, or "capturing" inanimate objects as prey. Male siblings often respond to these games with defiance, defending themselves loudly or attempting to dominate the play, reinforcing their independent streak. Cubs quickly learn the body language adults give off and when to fall in line - even males. When adult females begin crouching and staring in a particular direction - signifying they may hunt soon - cubs of all ages and genders mimic this action and quieten down their play, excited at the prospect of a potential meal. As the females move off to hunt, cubs instinctively head towards their father, who guides them to a hiding place. They huddle close together, with males nipping at each other and females watching quietly, and wait for further instruction. Their father standing guard over them is a sense of protection for female cubs who purr and cuddle against him and each other, but can prove irresistible to male cubs who frequently get scolded for their mouthy behavior - especially when they nip at their father's legs or tail at such serious moments! Once they reach adolescence, the behavioral dimorphism between Torvosaurus widens, with females working together and attempting to hunt independently, targeting small creatures such as those from burrows or scavengers. Males are much more lazy in this respect, and tend to come over to their sisters and steal their kills, or simply wait for food to be provided for them. Males are much more likely to loudly complain when hungry - or scavenge at best - whereas females prefer to find a meal for themselves. Adolescent males become quite prone to wandering at this age, and frequently have to be rounded up by the adults - usually the father - to prevent them from straying too far from the rest of the pack. Males continue to squabble and roughhouse with each other, but gradually stop playfighting with their sisters at adolescence. They take instead to challenging their own father more frequently - especially if they lack brothers - which can sometimes result in an angry outburst if their father is not in the mood. By maturity at Sub-Adult, male offspring become too much of a challenge to their father's authority to remain, and are aggressively driven out by him. At this stage, these sons have grown to be wildly independent, and leave alone or in pairs with another brother, keen to find their own way in the world.  Their leaving can draw out some sadness in mothers, but is more often than not a relief, due to their demanding natures. Females are allowed to remain, and sometimes take turns filling a hunting place with one of the other adult females to allow them to rest. Their presence is only tolerated temporarily, however - as once the adult females desire to nest again, or these females have the opportunity to find a male of their own, they get driven out of the pack by the adult females. Females rarely leave alone and usually take sisters with them to form their own sisterhood until they can secure a male. In exceptional cases, sometimes a mother may leave with up to 2 of her daughters, provoking a deep sense of sadness in the adult male of the pack. Mothers leaving with daughters is very rare, as they usually prefer to remain with the other adult females of the group, whom they have normally forged very strong bonds with.
