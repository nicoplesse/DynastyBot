# Tropeognathus – Cross-Species Interaction

Tropeognathus are largely curious of any terrestrial species that cross their path, and are prone to harassment of these species when they discover them. Herbivorous species are pursued and harassed - with air snaps common as they fly around them, hoping to provoke a chase for their own amusement. This does not always signify a hunt - and often herbivores much larger than they can comfortably hunt are tormented in this way. Herbivores that retaliate with aggression - or worse, ignore them - are quickly left alone, with Tropeognathus moving on to other species of interest. Tropeognathus has no interest in annoying sleeping dinosaurs, and ignores them unless actively hunting. Carnivores, meanwhile, tend to be followed with less aggression, with Tropeognathus wary of the potential danger behind a sharp set of teeth or claws. Often, carnivores can quickly tire of Tropeognathus following them overhead, due to the potential of the pterosaurs spoiling hunting opportunities for them by revealing their location. If met with aggression by these carnivores, Tropeognathus is quick to leave. Tropeognathus are wary especially of many semi-aquatic species, but especially Deinosuchus & Sarcosuchus. They avoid these species as much as possible, recognizing the potential threat they pose to fishing Tropeognathus. Tropeognathus do not routinely attempt to hunt Hatzegopteryx or Quetzalcoatlus unless they are in a group of 3 or more, but are particularly fond of feasting on their young. They use their wings to try and toss these offspring out of their nests - hopefully to their deaths below. Tropeognathus, however, quickly back down if met with aggression by the parents of these larger species. Unguarded chicks are always the priority target. Tropeognathus - when hunting healthy adults of these species - prefer to target lone individuals. They do not attempt these risky hunts unless they are in a group of 3 or 4 adults. Tropeognathus readily hunt Thalassodromeus whenever the opportunity arises - but only within the Tropeognathus’ preferred POI. Though these species rarely cross paths - with Thalassodromeus preferring to remain inland, and Tropeognathus traveling the coastal regions - Tropeognathus seem to relish in attacking these other pterosaurs, and will do so regardless of hunger requirements or time of day. Outside of a preferred POI, Thalassodromeus is sometimes harassed but never hunted. Tropeognathus especially enjoy hunting Lurdusaurus. These semi-aquatic herbivores are a reasonable size for Tropeognathus to attempt to hunt, and usually frequent similar environments to Tropeognathus. The Lurdusaurus' usual defence of diving into the water does little to protect against Tropeognathus, who can effectively swim after them to continue their hunts. Rhamphorhynchus are surprisingly one of the few smaller creatures not hunted by Tropeognathus. Instead, Tropeognathus seem to actually tolerate these species - so long as they do not attempt to scavenge from their meals - and may sometimes engage in playful behaviors with them. Rhamphorhynchus that attempt to scavenge from Tropeognathus' meals get pecked at and chased away. Though Tropeognathus will not hunt Rhamphorhynchus, they may kill them (even accidentally) if this smaller pterosaur does not respect their boundaries with food.
