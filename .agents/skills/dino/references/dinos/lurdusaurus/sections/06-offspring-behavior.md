# Lurdusaurus – Offspring Behavior

Lurdusaurus hatchlings, known as calves, are born weary and tired. The first thing they hear is their mother's gentle snorts and rumbles, encouraging them towards her, where she lays only a short distance away. Despite being tired, hatchlings eagerly attempt to stumble towards their mother, often tripping over their own legs - or in extreme cases, outright refusing to move until she nudges them up. They frequently snort at her for aid and attention during these first moments as they learn to wobble about on unsteady footing. As soon as they begin to walk, their mother aims to introduce them to the water - swimming is as crucial to a Lurdusaurus' survival as walking, and calves are encouraged to take their first paddles quickly so they can start to develop vital motor skills. Luckily for calves, swimming seems to come far more naturally to them than standing and running does, and their transition from nervous paddlers to adept swimmers is a fast one. By juvenile, Lurdusaurus calves become much more energetic and playful. Now fully in control of their limbs, they rush into shallow water pools and splash and kick around - much to the annoyance of some of the adults in the float. Strengthening their legs and tails is the most important step for a young Lurdusaurus, as slow swimmers may be left behind. The offspring however are completely oblivious to this fact, rather they believe it is a game. Chasing one another through the water, playing hide and seek behind kelp or rocks, and even trying to drag their mother into their games. Calves learn and practice diving at juvenile, enjoying sinking to the bottom of a lake or water source and walking across the floor to conserve energy. Though it may seem like a simple play activity - this behavior helps build their lung strength. When it rains young Lurdusaurus enjoy mud races with each other. They  compete for whoever can go the farthest and fastest. Calves broadcast in excitement, jumping out of the water and onto the shore, especially enjoying sliding about in mud. As they mature into Adolescence, calves lose some of their outgoing cheeky behaviour and start to settle into a more serious personality. They spend less time playing, and more time copying adults in the float. Males become louder and more dominant than their female siblings, engaging in mock fights when on the shore. They occasionally stand on their hind legs to display themselves, before running about shoving other young males about. The play that they still engage in becomes rougher and sometimes includes nipping at one another. If they do scrap, whoever reaches half health forfeits first - unless a mother tells them off first. Females gain more independence as they grow, and prefer to keep their personal space clear of others. They may snort at unrelated females who get too close, but tolerate their female relatives. Unknown females may receive nips if they come too close to a maturing young female. It’s at Adolescence where their fathers will finally show an interest in raising offspring. When the (highly protective) mothers aren’t looking, fathers will try to play and teach their children practical skills, such as how to dart between cover, navigate currents, and hide amongst the plants when under the water. At Sub-Adult, a calves personality matches its adult self. Females bond to their female relatives, while avoiding socialising with strangers. Males strut about with self importance, carefully looking for bullfighting opportunities to show off their mature strength and impress others in the float. Males start to flood with testosterone as they go through their final growth spurt. They experience bursts of aggression, challenging one another and engaging in short bullfights. Very rarely, a young male may attempt to challenge a female by mistake. If this happens, the float will shun him and push him away. Other young males may choose to follow him and form their own dispersal group. This dispersal group of young bulls holds a charged atmosphere, with bullfights breaking out frequently while they find their footing and test their strength. As bulls hit Adult stage, they feel more secure in their position and only bullfight when they feel there is a need to impress a female or dominate another male. At Sub-Adult, females try to remain in the group if there is enough room. If the float becomes over-full the group may choose to split apart into family groups and start new floats - giving opportunity for males to claim a dominant position in a new float. Sub-Adult males will stay if there is space for them, but will equally wish to form their own floats and claim dominant positions. If there are multiple young males in a float maturing at the same time, they almost always break away and form a bachelor dispersal group to practice their strength and dominance displays ready for Adulthood.
