# Lurdusaurus – Courtship & Parenthood

Lurdusaurus courtship begins with the dominant bull bellowing loudly from the shore, and gaining the attention of nearby cows. When he has their attention, he re-enters the water to perform an incredible acrobatic display. Leaping as high as possible from the water and over the heads of cows in the water as he bellows and displays himself, showing off his physical prowess and attractive colors. This will incite the action of the other males, who quickly seek to join in with the displays, all seeking to prove themselves to nearby cows. They splash their large bodies on the surface of the water, bellowing, leaping and twisting through the air. Dominant bulls are accepted by the float as the strongest and most attractive of all the bulls present, and they always have first choice in females. Other bulls will always back down if the dominant bull is interested in the same female - unless they wish to challenge for the position of dominant bull. Bulls become more aggressive than usual during this time, and any bull that gets too close to another will initiate aggressive calls. If neither bull backs down, they begin a vicious bullfight which ends when either bull reaches half health, or one bull chooses to forfeit. Cows will watch this display with great interest. They keep out of the water and rest on the shoreline, keeping out of any bullfights, and gauging the skills of the bulls on display. Cows that are interested in a particular bull will approach him in the water, calling lovingly and nuzzling against him to show her approval. Multiple females can choose one male, and neither will get jealous over this - their bonds are loose and never cemented. Once all the interested females have courted, the float will be led by the older cows to a suitable nearby area that can host their nests. The bulls stay near the back of the float, resting and recovering their strength and allowing cows to demonstrate their wisdom in choosing the right nesting ground. Nests are built communally, females show reduced aggression when they are nest building and do not start fights with one another - all working with the single purpose of building a safe environment to welcome the new offspring into. Cows and bulls will work together to dig up mud, branches, and reeds tirelessly to form the perfect nest for their clutches. Females may even share resources at this time, bonded individuals will place their nests close together so that the offspring can mix scents and familiarise themselves with close relatives. In contrast, when the nests are all built, females become highly territorial towards males and will chase them away from the nest area. They remain tolerant of other females, but males have no part in rearing young calves, and therefore must remain a suitable distance from the nesting site once the construction is finished. Cows lay close to their nests and curl around their eggs, keeping close watch and refusing to leave unless desperate for food or water, until their eggs have hatched. Males detach from the females, and keep well clear while the females enter their territorial state. They fall back into their normal routine of patrolling the territory, and de-stressing after courtship by rolling about in mud and swimming in the nearby water. Bulls will keep a fierce guard over their float while cows are nesting, particularly when calves are present. They watch the surrounding area closely for any potential threats and are quicker than normal to react aggressively if threatened. Bulls who have recently integrated into a float, as part of a territory dispute that they have won, will be aggressive to any existing calves. Since the calves were born to the bulls that have just been chased out, they will be swiftly killed to ensure that the cows can take part in courting the new bulls. Cows are doting on their new calves, and spend much of their time grooming and checking over their young offspring, often calling lovingly. Soon after hatching, they are gently nudged to their feet so they can be introduced to the water. She will let them rest and regain their energy upon her back, before encouraging them lovingly to learn to swim. She will proudly encourage them to play, and may even play with them. Teaching them everything she knows about survival, how to forage for water-plants, and how to dive and leap. Mothers are incredibly possessive and defensive of her young when they are below Adolescent. She will not allow anything near her offspring, not even other Lurdusaurus, and will break away from them entirely until her young are old enough to not be at immediate risk. Until then, she will spoil them. Often playing, basking, resting and showing great affection to her children. Once the offspring reach Adolescent, she will tentatively introduce them to the float. Letting them begin to play and interact with other children, never losing sight of her offspring. Should she be unable to locate them, she will begin to bellow loudly in fear, causing a mass panic as she searches for her missing children. Females who have just lost a clutch can be prone to this behaviour, crying out and hoping for them to return to her as she grieves. During this time she will be less prone to aggressive other individuals, and will allow them to approach and comfort her. Young Lurdusaurus often try to tempt their mothers into playing games with them, but mothers take a more serious role and rarely engage in play. Instead encouraging the calves to play amongst themselves. Play-fights that occur between calves are quickly halted by nearby mothers as soon as they are noticed, they are far too protective of their respective calves to allow much damage to either one. Fathers will show no interest in raising their offspring, but will try to sneak in games with their children at Adolescence when their mothers aren’t watching. However - should they be caught, the female will rush towards him with angry bellows. Giving him a firm nip or wack on the face or side to remind him to stay away. As the offspring age, females will ease on their protective behaviours and return to normal. It isn’t uncommon for the dominant male to begin to lead the herd away from the nesting territory in search of someplace more rich in resources once the offspring are old enough to move. Young bulls that prove too rowdy with their bullfighting are sometimes kicked out of the float, and encouraged to form their own dispersal group. This happens when youngsters reach Sub-Adult, when the float may have to push out members due to their grouping limit and resource availability.
