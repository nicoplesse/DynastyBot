# Giganotosaurus – Offspring Behavior (Nested)

Giganotosaurus hatchlings, known as kits, emerge quietly from their shells, chirping softly as they blink against the light. Their parents often watch in silence, offering occasional low calls to reassure their young of their presence. Kits squeak and quickly stumble towards their parents if nearby, nuzzling up to their warmth in order to regain the strength taken by hatching. If their parents are absent, they attempt to do this with their siblings. Kits rest and stay very close to each other until halfway through hatchling, where they tentatively begin to take their first steps. If the mother has to leave the nesting area for any reason, kits will quickly scuttle back to their nest to rest silently until her return, only responding when they hear her calling for them. At juvenile, kits have established strong bonds with the other kits in their litter. They are fully capable of walking, running, and keeping up with their parents on the move to food or water, and have developed enough muscle tone to land rather substantial bites on their siblings regardless of their size. Their mother takes them from the nest to find their father, who will greet them by carefully sniffing each one. Nervous kits flee back to their mothers for protection from the unfamiliar sight and individual, while fiercer ones posture towards their father, defiantly standing their ground. Once the anxieties of the introduction have settled, kits have no issue on attempting to initiate play with their father, even if he does not respond or behaves grumpily towards them. A favored pastime of many kits is pouncing on their father's tail. At this point the family begins to migrate, following the male's lead with the female keeping track of the rowdy kits. Fathers have no issue leaving their kits behind, while mothers hesitate when her kits are out of sight. Kits quickly learn to stay close to their mother - especially if they ever come across any threats. Kits are expected to keep up. If they are unable to, or they lag behind, the parents will leave them behind, and keep moving along with the kits who are the strongest. Kits eagerly swarm to feed from any kills made, but are nipped at by their fathers, which efficiently drives them off. Their mothers will take chunks of the kill and drop them in front of them, which the kits excitedly gnaw on and feed from. Continued travel and their parent’s teachings has kits hunting their own critters as they hit adolescence. They begin to grow increasingly independent from that moment forward, even drifting away from their parents and bonded siblings for solace and solitude. By the time they are Sub-Adult, they have a full set of skills ready enough for them to split from their parents and siblings, and head out on their own independently. They do so on their own without issue, shaking as a way to say goodbye to their parents, who by this time are indifferent to their presence. Some siblings opt to stay with another sibling or parent temporarily if they are solitary out of convenience, but split away from the group if any courtship opportunities arise.
