**Balz unverändert:** Sie wird wörtlich übernommen, nur ein Tippfehler ist korrigiert. Sie passt gut zur Leeds-Balz: Bei beiden werben die Männchen mit einer Vorführung, die Weibchen wählen, und beide balzen an den Flüssen zwischen Big Tree Overlook und Cedrus Forest.

**Vorbilder:**
- **Fächerfisch:** Fächerfische greifen einen Fischball abwechselnd an, einer nach dem anderen, und schlagen mit dem Schnabel hinein. Beim Jagen wechseln sie blitzschnell die Farbe. Das passt zum Schnabel und zu den Farbwechseln des Eurhino.
- **Delfine** (schon im Profil): laut, verspielt, mit engen Freundschaften unter Gleichgeschlechtlichen. Sie schlafen mit einer Gehirnhälfte und halten dabei ein Auge offen. Darum ist der Eurhino die geborene Nachtwache.
- **Lachs** (schon im Profil): Er kehrt zum Nisten in den Fluss zurück, in dem er geschlüpft ist. Auf dem Lauf flussaufwärts frisst er nicht. Der Eurhino zieht an Spino und Deino vorbei wie der Lachs an den Grizzlys.
- **Herings- und Sardinenschwärme:** Greift ein Jäger an, teilt sich der Schwarm um ihn herum und schließt sich hinter ihm wieder („Fontäne“). Stößt er hinein, spritzt der Schwarm in alle Richtungen auseinander.
- **Elritzen und Guppys:** Kleine Gruppen schwimmen nah an einen Räuber heran, um ihn zu begutachten. Daraus wird das „Angeben“ des Eurhino.
- **Thunfisch bei Fleckendelfinen:** Gelbflossen-Thunfische ziehen mit Delfinschulen und haben wohl mehr davon als die Delfine. So steht der Eurhino zur Kai-Bale.
- **Balz:** Die Männchen stranden am Ufer wie Grunions und Schlammspringer, die an Land werben.

**Neue Systeme in diesem Entwurf:**
- **Schwarm ohne Leitung:**
  - Wer Fisch findet, ruft, und der Schwarm folgt. Bei Streit teilt er sich und findet später wieder zusammen.
  - Innerhalb des Schwarms gibt es Freundeskreise aus 2 bis 4 Tieren meist gleichen Geschlechts.
- **Späher:** Bis zu 2 Eurhinos schwimmen bis zu 10 Körperlängen voraus. Wer Fisch findet, ruft den Schwarm mit einem :broadcast_call:. In einer Kai-Bale entscheidet die Leitkuh, ob die Bale folgt.
- **Fischen als Sport:**
  - Sie greifen abwechselnd in den Fischball wie Fächerfische.
  - Freundeskreise treten gegeneinander an. Die Sieger geben mit Sprüngen und Rufen an, die Verlierer bespritzen sie.
- **Schlaf mit einem offenen Auge:** In einem Schwarm ab 3 Tieren bleibt immer einer wach. Ruhen Kai auf Felsen, halten die Eurhinos unten im Wasser Wache.
- **Der Lauf zum Nistfluss:**
  - Die Schwärme sammeln sich an der Flussmündung zur Schule. Gemeinsam ziehen sie flussaufwärts durch Big Tree Overlook nach Cedrus Forest.
  - Auf dem Lauf fischen sie nicht und halten in Big Tree Overlook nicht an.
- **Flucht als Schwarm:**
  - Gegen einen einzelnen Jäger bilden sie die Fontäne, bei einem Stoß in den Schwarm spritzen sie auseinander.
  - Gegen Xiph-Rudel bleiben sie eng zusammen und halten den Sprint länger durch.
- **Angeben:** Höchstens 2 gesunde Adulte dürfen einmal an einem Apex vorbeischwimmen, mit mindestens 3 Körperlängen Abstand und nie in Richtung einer Gruppe.
- **Die Linie:** Bei knappen Fischen stellt sich der ansässige Schwarm nach festem Ablauf gegen einen neuen.
- **Körpersprache:**
  - Sprünge zeigen Freude.
  - Ein gehobener Schnabel mit Kopfschütteln zeigt Ärger.
  - Ein Schwanzschlag neben dem anderen ist eine Warnung.
  - Plötzliche Stille ist Alarm.

**Neu und zu entscheiden:**
1. **Späher:** bis zu 2, höchstens 10 Körperlängen voraus.
2. **Wache:** In einem Schwarm ab 3 Tieren bleibt immer einer wach.
3. **Der Lauf:**
   - Die Schwärme schwimmen ihn gemeinsam als Schule.
   - Auf dem Weg flussaufwärts wird nicht gefischt, und in Big Tree Overlook wird nicht angehalten.
4. **Angeben:**
   - Höchstens 2 gesunde Sub/Adults im offenen Wasser.
   - Einmal je Begegnung, mit mindestens 3 Körperlängen Abstand.
   - Nicht mit Jungen in der Nähe, nicht am Nest und nie in der Nähe von Heilwasser (Regel 4.8.4.2).
   - Nie in Richtung einer Gruppe.
   - Die Regeln des Jägers, wann er jagen darf, ändern sich dadurch nicht.
5. **Die Linie:**
   - Der ansässige Schwarm stellt sich in einer Reihe auf und ruft.
   - Ziehen die Neuen nach 30 s nicht ab, dürfen bis zu 3 von jeder Seite kämpfen.
   - Der Kampf endet, wenn ein Schwarm abzieht.
6. **Nur zur Balz an Land:** Außerhalb der Balz bleiben Eurhinos im Wasser, Sprünge ausgenommen.
7. **Optional:** Die Kai-Balz weckt bei den Eurhinos die Lust aufs Nisten. Soll sie den Lauf zum Nistfluss auslösen dürfen?

Eurhinosaurus was a sleek, fast-swimming marine reptile that lived during the Early Jurassic period, around 180 million years ago, in what is now Europe. This remarkable ichthyosaur was known for its extremely elongated upper jaw, which extended far beyond its lower jaw like a swordfish's bill. Reaching lengths of up to 6–7 meters, Eurhinosaurus was a powerful predator, using its long snout and numerous sharp teeth to catch small fish and squid. Its name, meaning "well-nosed lizard," highlights its distinctive jaw structure, and its streamlined body and large flippers made it an efficient, agile swimmer in the ancient seas.

## Overview

**Medium Cathemeral Aquatic Piscivore**

- Eurhinosaurus form promiscuous bonds, and close friendships with others of the same sex.
- Eurhinosaurus are nomadic and follow the Leedsichthys migration route. To nest, they return to their nesting river.
- Eurhinosaurus have a passive nature towards other species. Shoals may become defensive towards other Eurhinosaurus shoals when resources are low.
- Eurhinosaurus feed on fish and crustaceans. They do not hunt other players and do not claim or feed from bodies.
- Eurhinosaurus may group with Kaiwhekea and with one Leedsichthys. Eurhinosaurus do not defend the species they group with, and those species do not defend them.
- Eurhinosaurus tolerate albino and melanistic individuals.

## Genetics

Skins that are considered mutations for Eurhinosaurus include: Albino, Melanistic.
Players must roll in genetics-rolls to use these skins in game. This is one roll per species, per character. Only when the character dies can you roll again if using the same character name.

## Group Limits (In-Game Group)

- Unlimited Sub/Adults
- Unlimited Offspring
- Unlimited Adoptees

This group is known as a shoal.

- **Kaiwhekea:** When grouped with a Kaiwhekea bale, Eurhinosaurus count towards the bale's limit of 16 Sub/Adults.
- **Leedsichthys:** One Leedsichthys may join in addition. A group with a Leedsichthys is known as a flotilla.
- **School:** During courtship, shoals mix into one large breeding group, known as a school (see Courtship).

## Engagement Limits (Defence & Hunts)

**Predator Reaction: Flighty**

- Up to 3 Sub/Adult Eurhinosaurus may defend if attacked.
- Eurhinosaurus rely on their speed. They flee from Apex and Giant carnivores and from Xiphactinus packs of 3 or more, and only turn to fight when cornered or defending their nest.
- Adolescent Eurhinosaurus never take part in a defence.
- When grouped with Kaiwhekea, Eurhinosaurus do not join the protective ring or act as active defenders. They raise the alarm and flee with the bale.
- Eurhinosaurus never lead a predator towards another group on purpose.

## Resource Challenges

- Eurhinosaurus only challenge other Eurhinosaurus shoals, and only when fish are scarce (see Other Shoals).
- They do not accept territory challenges from other species, and simply move on.
- Eurhinosaurus never show aggression to other adults within their nesting grounds.

## Preferred Diet

- Eurhinosaurus live on fish and various crustaceans. They check rocks and underwater caves for crustaceans.
- They hunt fish as a sport, not out of need.
- They do not fish on the run to their nesting river (see Migration).
- Eurhinosaurus do not hunt Kaiwhekea or Leedsichthys offspring.

## Nesting Limits

- 🌧️ Rainy Season: 4 🥚 per 3 hours.
- ☀️ Dry Season: 2 🥚 per 3 hours.

Eurhinosaurus nest in the saltwater river south of Grand Plains river. Their nests are built out of kelp, branches and rocks.
On Riparia, Eurhinosaurus nest in the Cedrus Forest rivers, ideally as far upstream as possible.

## Physical Dimorphism

- ♀️ Females have shorter noses and a heavier build than males. Suitable subspecies include: communis.
- ♂️ Males have longer noses and slimmer builds than females. Suitable subspecies include: longirostris and pelagicus.

Subspecies dimorphism for Eurhinosaurus is not mandatory, but individuals differing from the typical dimorphism may face difficulties during courtship.

## Color Dimorphism

- Outside of the breeding season, they shed their skin regularly depending on their environment, changing their color, but not patterns, to better fit into their surroundings and hide from predators.
- Both males and females shed into bright colors during nesting season, sometimes also changing their skin patterns.
- Females prioritise blue skins, while males prioritise green skins.

Color dimorphism for Eurhinosaurus is mandatory, the changing of environmental camouflage is optional, however courting color changes are mandatory.

## Growing Up

### Offspring Behavior

- **Fry:** Eurhinosaurus young, known as fry, hatch from their egg completely mobile and talkative. Often locking sights with their mother, they stay particularly close to their mother's tail, feeding from shells and small fish within their nesting areas.
  - When shore hunters such as Austroraptor, Halszkaraptor or Sarcosuchus come near, fry fall silent and hide in the shallows, among roots and reeds.
- **Juveniles:** Approaching juvenile, young fry adjust to the chaos of other young Eurhinosaurus within the nesting areas.
  - Juveniles from all nests often take up games of tag together, with slight nips and pushing into each other's sides. These games promote key survival instincts, and the friendship bonds they need for their survival in the future.
  - Mothers monitor these games of tag to make sure they never cause too much injury.
  - Juveniles practise the fishing styles of the adults on small fish, one loud, one silent, and race each other along the river.
- **The creche:**
  - When the oldest fry in a mother's clutch becomes adolescent, the mother abandons her offspring and leaves the nesting grounds to return to the ocean.
  - She seeks out her previous shoal members, and groups of adult females leave together. Closely bonded females sometimes wait at the river's mouth for a companion to leave her nest.
  - Mothers do not wait for their youngest offspring to mature. They leave all the offspring in their nesting ground, which turns into a creche of youngsters looking out for one another while they continue to grow.
- **Adolescents:**
  - At adolescent, siblings or other similarly aged young start to form their own groups, known as shoals, and form close friendship bonds with those of the same sex. The first friend groups form here (see Shoal Life).
  - They swim downstream with their newly formed shoal, or sometimes choose to branch out on their own.
  - Young shoals often join their first flotilla or Kaiwhekea bale to learn the migration route.
  - These youngsters only return to the river nesting ground when wishing to nest themselves.

### Orphaned Behavior

- Orphaned Eurhinosaurus are born into the waters alone and defenceless. With natural survival instincts, these orphans tend to hide within overgrown kelp and coral rocks. They remain hidden, feeding off small fish and crustaceans.
- As they mature into sub-adult, these orphans explore more bravely, venturing out into open waters in hopes they may find a shoal to join.
- Kaiwhekea bales welcome orphaned Eurhinosaurus kindly. An orphan may travel with a bale like any Eurhinosaurus, but it is not adopted, and not protected.

## General Behavior

- **Voice:** Eurhinosaurus are usually loud and rambunctious by nature, and are normally heard long before they are seen. While both sexes enjoy vocalising, males call more loudly and more often, projecting their voices through the water and singing out to their group mates.
- **Routine:** Shoals often follow a familiar route around their current POI, travelling between popular fish locations and checking rocks and underwater caves for crustaceans.
- **Speed:** Eurhinosaurus are among the fastest swimmers in the ocean, and can hold a sprint longer than almost any other aquatic. Only Xiphactinus sprint faster.
- **Play:** They race each other, leap high out of the water with Hydrofoil, and ride the bow wave of a swimming Leedsichthys. Friend groups often leap in sync, like spinner dolphins.
- **Curiosity:** Despite being confined to the waters, Eurhinosaurus are playful and endlessly curious about land animals. They follow them along the shore, take great pleasure in calling to them, and poke their heads above the water to peer into nearby forests. For an audience on land they leap high with Hydrofoil, like dolphins leaping beside a boat.
- **Arrogance:** They enjoy their speed and their perceived dominance over the ocean, and like to show off in front of hunters (see Showing Off).

### Fishing

Fishing is seen as a sport. Shoals train constantly to refine their coordination.

- **Taking turns:** Like sailfish, the shoal herds a fish school into a tight ball. Members dart into it one at a time with a quick sweep of the bill, then pull away to make room for the next.
- **Loud style:** Some members circle a fish school with loud calls and fast turns until it bunches into a tight ball. The others then dart in from below and the sides to snatch fish.
- **Silent style:** Some shoals approach in silence, dive deep and strike the fish school from below.
- **Mixed style:** Shoals switch between loud chaos and silent strikes to keep the fish off balance.
- **Contests:** Friend groups compete for the biggest catch. The winners boast with high leaps and loud calls, and the losers splash them. Contests never turn into fights.
- **With Kaiwhekea:** In a bale, Eurhinosaurus join the Kaiwhekea's herding with their speed, and drive fish into the bale's circle.
- **Sharing:** A successful shoal often leaves part of the catch to young, or to bonded Kaiwhekea nearby.

### Day and Night

- **Rhythm:** Eurhinosaurus are cathemeral. They are active by day and night in short bursts, and rest in short naps whenever the shoal feels safe.
- **One eye open:** Like dolphins, Eurhinosaurus sleep lightly with one eye open, and wake at the slightest disturbance.
- **Watch:**
  - In a shoal of 3 or more, at least one member always stays awake while the others rest. Members take turns.
  - In a Kaiwhekea bale, Eurhinosaurus often take on part of the night watch, resting on the bale's outer edge.
  - When Kaiwhekea nap on high rocks, Eurhinosaurus stay in the water below and keep watch.
- **Dawn and dusk:** Xiphactinus hunt the saltwater channels at dawn and dusk. Shoals cross the channels by day where they can, and stay close together in the half light.
- **Night:** Sachicasaurus hunt sleeping flotillas at night. Eurhinosaurus resting at a flotilla's edge are among the first they reach, and among the first to wake.

### Time Out of Water

- Eurhinosaurus leave the water only to display in courtship (see Courtship). Outside courtship, they stay in the water, apart from leaps.
- Their leaps carry them high above the surface in play, in contests, and in their joy at the courtship of others.

### Migration

- **The route:** Eurhinosaurus follow the Leedsichthys migration route marked on the profile map: a loop through the warm saltwater channels between the islands, with regular trips out into the open oceans of Kelp Vale, the Abyssal Depths, Palm Islands and Stonebed Shoal.
- **The oceans** hold the fish shoals Eurhinosaurus value most. Shoals often travel out together with a Kaiwhekea bale or a Leedsichthys.
- **Fresh water:** Shoals may leave the route for detours into the lakes and fresh waters connected to the channels, where land animals come to drink. Spinosaurus live there, and land carnivores wait on the banks.
- **The run:** When driven to nest, Eurhinosaurus leave the route and return to their nesting river, like salmon.
  - Shoals gather at the river mouth, and swim upstream together as one school.
  - On Riparia, the run leads from the saltwater channels up through Big Tree Overlook into the Cedrus Forest rivers.
  - On the run, they do not fish, and do not stop in Big Tree Overlook. Spinosaurus wait there like bears at a salmon run.
  - The school swims fast and close together. Loud calls keep stragglers with the school. At the sight of a hunter, the school falls silent and speeds up.
  - The run passes the courting grounds of the Leedsichthys. Eurhinosaurus swim past the males' nests without disturbing them.
- **The way back:** Once they have finished courting, males swim back downstream to the route. Mothers follow once their oldest offspring become adolescent.

### Albino & Melanistic Behavior

- Both albino and melanistic Eurhinosaurus are accepted by their shoals.
- **Albino** Eurhinosaurus cannot hide with their colors, and stand out to predators. They stay in the middle of the shoal, and rarely scout.
- **Melanistic** individuals are hard to see at night, and are often trusted with the watch.

## Communication

- **Chatter:** A shoal is rarely quiet. Constant calls tell everyone where the others are. Sudden silence means danger, or a silent hunt.
- **Songs:** Males sing longer sequences of :broadcast_call: to their group mates.
- **Joy and play:** high leaps, racing circles and tail slaps in play.
- **Looking around:** They poke their heads above the water to look at the shore, often with a :question_emote:, and call out to land species.
- **Annoyance:** A raised bill and a :shake_emote:. If this is ignored, a hard tail slap beside the other follows, without landing the hit. Both are respected at once: the other moves away by at least 1 body length.
- **Alarm:** The chatter stops, and a :danger_call: follows. The shoal either bunches together or bursts apart (see Predator Reaction). The call carries far, which is why Kaiwhekea value Eurhinosaurus as sentries.

## Social Interaction

Shoals form strong, lasting bonds with their members, often spending the majority of their lives together. These connections are vital due to their cooperative fishing and their mutual reliance for comfort, protection and survival.

### Shoal Life

Shoals have no leader. Like a real fish school, a shoal follows whoever has found the fish.

- **Following the finder:**
  - A member that spots fish or crustaceans gives a :broadcast_call: and swims towards them, and the shoal follows.
  - Disagreements are settled by splitting. Those who want to go elsewhere may leave, and the shoal often meets again later.
- **Friend groups:**
  - Within a shoal, 2 to 4 close friends, usually of the same sex, form a friend group. They swim in sync, leap together, share fish and rest side by side.
  - Friend groups often stay together for life, even when the shoal around them changes.
- **Scouts:**
  - Up to 2 members may swim ahead of the shoal as scouts, no further than 10 body lengths.
  - A scout that finds fish calls the shoal over. A scout that spots a predator falls silent and turns back at once.
  - In a Kaiwhekea bale, the lead cow decides whether the bale follows a scout's call.
- **Merging:** When a shoal meets a smaller shoal, they often merge, favouring safety in numbers.

### Other Shoals

- When two separate shoals encounter each other, they may briefly interact in a friendly manner before moving on.
- Shoals rarely spend more than a few minutes within the same POI as one another, so as not to empty its fish.
- **The line:** If a shoal occupies a POI with few resources, it may challenge a larger shoal that enters:
  1. The resident shoal lines up side by side, facing the newcomers. It calls out with :threaten_call:, and does not move from its line.
  2. The newcomers have 30 seconds to turn away.
  3. If they stay, up to 3 members of each shoal may fight. The fight ends when one shoal leaves.
- Eurhinosaurus never show aggression to other adults within their nesting grounds.

### Correcting Over-Aggression

Eurhinosaurus are rarely aggressive towards their own kind. Over-aggression is corrected in steps:

1. A raised bill and a :shake_emote:.
2. A hard tail slap beside the aggressor, while its friend group closes in around it.
3. Firm nips from the shoal.
4. If it continues, the shoal leaves it behind.

## Aggressive Interaction

- Eurhinosaurus are generally not aggressive towards members of their own species. Typically, shoals strive to maintain harmony among their own kind.
- Aggressive encounters are rare, and usually occur only when resources are scarce (see Other Shoals). In most cases, they prefer to relocate to another point of interest where resources are more abundant.

## Cross-Species Interaction

- **Kaiwhekea:**
  - Eurhinosaurus and Kaiwhekea are frequent companions. Eurhinosaurus stay with a bale because they gain more from it than the Kaiwhekea do: safety in numbers, and the fish the bale drives together. Yellowfin tuna travel with spotted dolphins in the same way.
  - In return they act as the bale's sentries. Their loud voices carry far through the water and warn the bale long before a predator arrives.
  - Shoals often swim on the outer edge of a migrating bale, and dart ahead to scout for fish.
  - Eurhinosaurus tease Kaiwhekea with races and close passes, but never bite them.
  - **Kaiwhekea courtship:** Eurhinosaurus watch the bulls' dance closely. It excites them, puts them in a happy mood and stirs their own wish to court, and they often breach along with the bulls. Kaiwhekea do not let this distract them.
  - Neither species defends the other.
- **Leedsichthys:**
  - Eurhinosaurus may travel and group with one Leedsichthys, like pilot fish around a whale shark. Such a group is known as a flotilla.
  - Neither side defends the other.
  - Eurhinosaurus may use the giant's body as cover. The Leedsichthys never moves to block a predator for them.
  - Shoals love to race circles around the slow giant. Its pups soon learn to school alongside Eurhinosaurus young.
- **Tropeognathus:** Tropeognathus follow flotillas from the air and dive in when the flotilla reaches a gathering of fish. Eurhinosaurus take this as an insult to their sport, and drive them off. They give a :threaten_call: first, then charge and snap at it until it leaves the water. This is not a hunt: once the Tropeognathus is airborne, they let it go.
- **Xiphactinus:** The only fish that sprint faster than Eurhinosaurus, and their most feared hunters. Xiphactinus live in the saltwater channels, so shoals cross them quickly.
- **Apex carnivores:** Eurhinosaurus flee from adult Tylosaurus, Sachicasaurus, Livyatan, Spinosaurus and Deinosuchus (see Predator Reaction).
- **Sarcosuchus:** As a Large carnivore, Sarcosuchus may be defended against by up to 3 Eurhinosaurus. Most often, the shoal escapes by speed. Sarcosuchus lurk along the Cedrus Forest rivers, near the nests.
- **Shore hunters:** Austroraptor and Halszkaraptor are a danger to fry in the shallows of the nesting river.
- **Land animals:** Eurhinosaurus are fascinated by every land animal they meet at the water (see General Behavior). Herbivores get calls and leaps up close. Carnivores get the same show, but only from a safe distance (see Showing Off).
- **Deinocheirus:** Eurhinosaurus display a strong fascination and affection for Deinocheirus, admiring these docile semi-aquatics for their ability to spurt water, which creates playful environments the Eurhinosaurus eagerly enjoy. They often attempt to trade fish or decorative shells with Deinocheirus in exchange for water spouts, turning the encounter into a joyful game.
- **Pycnonemosaurus:** Terrestrial species they encounter are sometimes teased. Eurhinosaurus especially enjoy screaming matches with Pycnonemosaurus, who rarely back down from the noisy challenge.
- Both Deinocheirus and Pycnonemosaurus live along the Cedrus Forest rivers, so most of these meetings happen during nesting.

## Predator Reaction

At the first sign of danger the chatter stops. A sudden silence is the shoal's first warning, followed by a :danger_call:.

### Solo

- A lone Eurhinosaurus flees from every hunter it can outrun, and relies on its speed. It only fights when cornered.

### The Shoal

- **Fountain:** Against a single hunter, the shoal splits around it, and closes again behind it, like a herring school.
- **Burst:** If a hunter strikes into the shoal, members dart away in all directions, then regroup out of reach, calling softly.
- **Packs:** Against a Xiphactinus battery, the shoal bunches together and sprints. Batteries try to split it, so it bursts apart only once the battery strikes.
- **Defenders:** Up to 3 Sub/Adults may turn to fight when cornered, at their nest, or against non-Apex hunters such as Sarcosuchus. They strike with Joust and pull away again, rather than holding a drawn-out fight.
- **In a bale:** Eurhinosaurus do not join the protective ring or act as defenders. They raise the alarm and flee with the bale.

### Where to Flee

- **Tylosaurus:** along the surface at full speed. Eurhinosaurus are faster, but a Tylosaurus does not tire sooner.
- **Livyatan:** into the shallows, where Livyatan do not follow.
- **Sachicasaurus:** out of the kelp and the reef, into open water. A seized Eurhinosaurus is lost; its shoal does not help it.
- **Deinosuchus and Spinosaurus:** away from banks and shores, into deeper, open water. On the run, the school passes their waters without stopping.
- **Xiphactinus:** as one tight group, holding the sprint for as long as it takes. A battery that is spotted early tires first.

### Showing Off

Eurhinosaurus enjoy their speed, and sometimes come across as arrogant. Like minnows that swim up to a pike to inspect it, they show off by swimming past hunters they know they can outrun.

- Only healthy Sub/Adults in open water may show off, and no more than 2 at a time.
- They pass a hunter once, no closer than 3 body lengths, then swim away, and do not return in the same encounter.
- They never show off near fry, at a nest, or near healing water (4.8.4.2), and never lead the hunter towards a group.
- A hunter may chase them, and showing off is a risk the Eurhinosaurus takes. It does not change the hunter's own rules for when it may hunt.

## Courtship & Parenthood

Once ready to court, shoals from all over the world travel to the nesting river between the POIs of Dark Woods and Young Grove, or Cedrus Forest as shown in the map below.
Shoals mix together and group into one larger breeding group, this group is called a school.
Eurhinosaurus are passive to all members of their species within these POIs and will travel further away to seek food if resources are scarce, before returning to their chosen nesting area.
Both males and females shed into bright courting skins which signifies their desire to court and nest.
Females prioritise blue skins, while males prioritise green skins.

Males of this species begin the courtship process by beaching themselves on the river edge, vocalising and flapping their fins on the sand or mud. They move up and down the small section of beach and show off their strength and ability to stay out of water for a short period of time.
Females who are interested will watch from the water's edge, approaching, splashing, and calling out softly with approval.
When the courtship display ends, the male returns to the water and approaches a female nearby that has been calling to him. They circle together and brush their bodies against one another to form a close bond.
The female leads him to a section of the riverbed, and builds her nest to lay eggs and allow the male to fertilise them.

Females stay near their nest to protect their eggs once laid, but males are promiscuous and will return to the beach to display themselves once more, hoping to attract another female.
Once males have finished courting and fertilising eggs, they leave the nesting grounds. A small group of bonded males will usually leave together. Males take no responsibility in child rearing, and return to the ocean long before any offspring hatch.
Mothers stay within the nesting grounds with their young until their oldest offspring becomes adolescent. Then, the mothers leave the rivers and swim back to the open ocean with other adult females.

## Elderly & End of Life (Optional)

- As Eurhinosaurus age, they spend less time in the open ocean and stay close to rivers and coast lines.
- They lose the ability to shed their skin to camouflage themselves, and show more white or pale colors in their skin as their patterns and original colors fade.
- The pale colors often make elderly Eurhinosaurus stick out and become a target for predators. They no longer scout, and swim in the middle of the shoal.
- When the shoal migrates to breeding territory, elderly males will beach themselves to display, but sometimes struggle to return to the water quickly and become a bigger target for predators.
- Companions of elderly individuals will sometimes stick close by their side, guiding them to feeding areas and encouraging them to keep up with the shoal. Friend groups often stay with an elderly friend to the end.
- Eventually, elderly Eurhinosaurus lose their swimming strength and float gently to the bottom of the sea or river bed, in their final resting place.
- Closely bonded individuals will mourn the loss of their friends for a short period of time, before moving on with their shoal and forming new bonds.

## Rule Exemptions

- **Grouping:** As piscivores, Eurhinosaurus may group with Kaiwhekea and one Leedsichthys (exemption from 8.1, under the proposed piscivore rule). There is no defence between the species.
- The Metriacanthosaurus exemption is no longer needed, because Eurhinosaurus do not feed from bodies.

## Profile – Quick View

- Eurhinosaurus are Medium Cathemeral Aquatic Piscivores.
- They group in unlimited numbers, offspring and adoptees in a group known as a shoal. They may join a Kaiwhekea bale, where they count towards its 16 Sub/Adults, and one Leedsichthys, without any defence between the species.
- Shoals have no leader. They follow whoever finds the fish, send up to 2 scouts ahead, and hold close friend groups of the same sex.
- They are passive, feed only on fish and crustaceans, and fish as a sport: in turns, loud or silent.
- They sleep lightly with one eye open. In a shoal of 3 or more, one always stays awake, and they act as sentries for Kaiwhekea bales.
- They follow the Leedsichthys route, with detours into the fresh waters connected to the channels. To nest, they make the run upstream to their nesting river as one school, without fishing or stopping.
- They are endlessly curious about land animals. They follow them along the shore, call to them and leap for them.
- They flee from Apex and Giant carnivores and Xiphactinus packs of 3 or more, and up to 3 may defend. Within limits, they show off by swimming past hunters.
- They court at the nesting river: males beach themselves to display, and females choose. Mothers stay with their young until the oldest reach adolescent, and leave the rest in a creche.
- They are friendly towards Deinocheirus, and enjoy screaming matches with Pycnonemosaurus.
