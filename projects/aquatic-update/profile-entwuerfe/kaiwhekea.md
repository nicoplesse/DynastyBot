**Neue Systeme in diesem Entwurf:**
- **Der eigene Gruß** ist der Kern des Kai:
  - Jeder Kai hat einen kurzen Gruß aus höchstens 2 Emotes und 2 Fähigkeiten.
  - Erlaubt sind als Fähigkeiten nur Bite und Neck Swipe, ins leere Wasser. Als Ruf ist nur der :friendly_call: erlaubt. Große Bewegungen gehören nicht hinein.
  - Der Spieler muss sich den Gruß merken, er wird nirgends notiert.
  - Wählt eine Kuh bei der Balz einen Bullen, übernimmt sie seinen Gruß für eine Saison, bei einer Lebensbindung für immer. Nur die Leitkuh behält immer ihren eigenen.
  - Dominante Bullen tragen einen festen Gruß, der für die ganze Art gilt. Ihre Kühe übernehmen ihn.
  - Vorbilder: Verbündete Tümmler-Männchen gleichen ihre Pfiffe einander an, Wellensittich-Paare ihre Rufe.
- **Dominante Bullen:** bis zu 2 je Bale. Sie begleiten die Bale und tanzen bei der Balz zuerst. Herausgefordert werden dürfen sie nur nach einem sichtbaren Fehler im Gruß, jeder höchstens einmal je Sitzung.
- **Balz als Ritual:**
  1. Die Kühe springen und rufen.
  2. Die Dominanten tanzen.
  3. Wer einen Fehler im Gruß macht, darf herausgefordert werden.
  4. Die Kühe wählen.
  5. Die übrigen Bullen tanzen in einer zweiten Runde.

  Vorbild sind See-Elefanten: Die Kühe sammeln sich dort, wo der Dominante seinen Platz hält.
- **Heimatstrand:** Die Bale nistet am Geburtsstrand der Leitkuh. Nach einem Erfolg bleibt sie dort, nach einem Misserfolg wechselt sie den Strand, wie Dreizehenmöwen.
- **Land und Felsen:**
  - Kai bleiben an Land, bis ihr Durst auf 75 % fällt (rund 10 min). In großer Gefahr dürfen sie bis 50 % bleiben (rund 20 min).
  - Wer darunter fällt, bekommt Hautschäden und wird dauerhaft unfruchtbar.
  - Sie schlafen auch auf hohen Felsen, so wie Seelöwen und Pinguine vor Orcas auf Felsen und Eis springen.
- **Struktur der Bale:**
  - Die **Leitkuh** wählt Route, Fanggründe und Ruheplatz. Bei Streit entscheidet die Mehrheit der Kühe.
  - Die **dominanten Bullen** begleiten die Bale. Fremde Bullen dürfen sie um die Begleitung herausfordern.
  - **Tanten** (Kühe ohne eigene Kälber) hüten fremde Kälber.
- **Körpersprache:**
  - Kopfschütteln, ins Wasser schnappen und Flossenschlag aufs Wasser. Das ersetzt das Niesen des Para.
  - Frustsignale beginnen immer mit Kopfschütteln. Ein Gruß enthält nie eins, deshalb kann man beides nicht verwechseln.
- **Fischen** als eigenes System:
  - **Reihenfolge am Schwarm:** Kälber zuerst, dann wird abgewechselt.
  - **Schwarm-Treiben** mit den Eurhinos.
  - **Kelp aufscheuchen** in Kelp Vale.
  - **Strand-Fischen:** In Mudflats und Coastland Swamp treiben sie Fische auf Schlammbänke und gleiten hinterher.
- **Ruheplatz-Wahl und Nachtwache** mit erlaubten Nachtaktivitäten.
- **Fluchtwege je Jäger:**
  - Vor dem Livy geht es in die Untiefen.
  - Vor Krokodilen geht es weg von Ufer und Schlammbänken, ins tiefere Wasser.
  - Vor dem Sachi geht es raus aus dem Kelp oder auf einen hohen Felsen.
- **Drehkampf** mit festem Ablauf für jeden Kampf zwischen Bullen:
  1. Kreisen mit 3 :threaten_call:.
  2. Beide grüßen.
  3. Ein letzter :threaten_call:.
  4. Höchstens 2 Bisse je Durchgang, Ende unter 50 % Health.
- **Kälber:** Sie schwimmen im Sog der Mutter und übernehmen anfangs ihren Gruß. Ab Adolescent hat jeder Kai einen eigenen.
- **Alter und Tod:** Die Älteste führt die Bale an ihren letzten Ort. Sterbende werden an der Oberfläche gestützt. Über der Stelle, an der ein Toter versunken ist, zeigen seine Freunde noch eine Weile seinen Gruß.

**Entschieden am 04.11.:**
1. Ein gepackter Kai ist verloren. Die Bale hilft ihm nicht. Kommt er frei, darf er fliehen.
2. Es gibt keine Obergrenze für Bullen. Bullen sind dominant oder nicht-dominant, und dominante Bullen haben einen eigenen Gruß.
3. Fremde Bullen dürfen die Dominanten um die Begleitung herausfordern. Steht es 1 zu 1, entscheiden die Kühe.
4. Statt 10 s auf der Schlammbank gilt die Durst-Regel: zurück ins Wasser bei 75 %, in großer Gefahr bei 50 %. Wer darunter fällt, wird unfruchtbar. Kai schlafen auch auf hohen Felsen.
5. Eurhinos werden nicht adoptiert. Ein verwaistes Eurhino-Kalb wird nur freundlich aufgenommen.
6. Jeder Kai hat einen Gruß, den der Spieler sich merken muss. Große Familienverbände gibt es nicht.
7. Nach dem Tanz geben die Dominanten 2 :friendly_call:.
8. Es gibt einen dominanten Gruß für die ganze Art, und die Kühe der Dominanten übernehmen ihn.
9. Im Gruß ist als Ruf nur der :friendly_call: erlaubt, als Fähigkeiten nur Bite und Neck Swipe, ohne große Bewegungen.
10. Ein übernommener Gruß gilt eine Saison, bei einer Lebensbindung für immer.
11. Junge Bullen ziehen oft aus, müssen es aber nicht.
12. Das Geschlecht zeigt künftig die Unterart.

**Von mir ergänzt, bitte prüfen:**
1. **Dimorphismus wird Pflicht,** wie beim Tylo. Der Kai hat schon passende Unterarten: Kühe tenui, Bullen katiki und grandis. Dafür fallen zwei Regeln weg: die halbe Eierzahl für nicht-dimorphe Kühe und die stille Werbung nicht-dimorpher Bullen.
2. **Fehler im Gruß:**
   - Ein Fehler zählt nur, wenn ihn jeder sieht: ein falsches oder fehlendes Element, eine falsche Reihenfolge, mehr als 3 s Pause oder ein Neuanfang. Im Zweifel ist es kein Fehler.
   - Herausgefordert wird nur nach einem Fehler, und zwar direkt nach dem Tanz, vor den 2 Calls.
3. **Rückzieher:**
   - Gibt der Herausforderer den letzten :threaten_call: nicht, ist seine Herausforderung verbraucht.
   - Gibt ihn der Dominante nicht, verliert er den Platz.
   - Wer mit :heart_call: aufgibt, hat verloren.
4. **Jeder dominante Bulle** wird höchstens einmal je Sitzung herausgefordert, egal ob beim Ritual oder um die Begleitung.
5. **Freie Plätze:**
   - Ein Platz wird frei durch Tod, Weggang oder eine Bindung. Auch eine neue Bale hat freie Plätze.
   - Die ungebundenen Bullen kämpfen tagsüber nach demselben Ablauf darum. Ist nur einer da, bekommt er den Platz ohne Kampf.
   - Bindet sich ein Dominanter, wird sein Platz zum nächsten Ritual frei.
6. **Herausforderung um die Begleitung:**
   - Kühe eines geschlagenen Dominanten wählen selbst. Folgen sie ihm, nehmen sie seinen eigenen Gruß an. Bleiben sie beim Platz, behalten sie den dominanten Gruß.
   - Geschlagene Herausforderer verlassen die Bale.
7. **Felsen:**
   - Erlaubt sind nur Felsen, die ein Kai mit einem normalen Sprung aus dem Wasser erreicht.
   - Ein gesunder Kai darf auf einen Felsen fliehen. Mit schweren Deathscars oder beim Verbluten darf er es nicht (Regel 4.6).
8. **Unfruchtbarkeit:**
   - Sie ist dauerhaft und wird im Bloodline-Log vermerkt.
   - Wer ohne Gefahr unter 75 % an Land bleibt, begeht einen normalen Regelverstoß.
9. **Heimatstrand:**
   - Erfolg heißt: Mindestens die Hälfte eines Geleges erreicht Juvenile, aufgerundet. Das sind 2 von 4 Eiern oder 1 von 2.
   - Der Strand bleibt, solange irgendein Gelege der Bale dort erfolgreich ist.
   - Nach einem Scheitern wählt die Mehrheit der Kühe den neuen Strand, so wie bei einer verwaisten Leitkuh.
10. **Lebensbindungen:**
    - Sie sind monogam, weil gebundene Bullen nur ihre Kuh nehmen. Die drei Formen aus dem letzten Entwurf fallen weg.
    - Eine verwitwete Kuh darf den Gruß ihres Partners behalten oder zu ihrem eigenen zurückkehren.
    - Gleichgeschlechtliche Paare einigen sich, wessen Gruß sie teilen.

**Noch offen:**
- **Der dominante Gruß** selbst ist noch nicht festgelegt.
- **Ausdauer des Sachi beim Halten:** Er macht dabei rund 19 Schaden/s und braucht für einen vollen Kai etwa eine Minute. Ob seine Ausdauer so lange reicht, zeigt erst der Mod.
- **Durst an Land im Spiel bestätigen:** Laut den Werten hat der Kai 400 Durst, der an Land nach etwa 40 min leer ist. Daraus kommen die 10 und 20 min.

**Warum tagaktiv statt cathemeral:** Wie beim Para wird die Nacht zur verwundbaren Zeit. Das gibt dem nächtlichen Sachicasaurus seine Nische. Kai fischen am Tag, Sachi in der Nacht, so kommen sie sich beim Fisch nicht in die Quere.

**Warum nur Health und nicht auch CW:** Mehr Health lässt Kai gegen Apexe länger durchhalten. Die CW bleibt, damit Xiphactinus-Rudel einen vereinzelten Kai weiterhin mit 4 Tieren erlegen können, genau wie Achillobator einen Para. Und damit bleibt der Sachi (Tragkraft 4001) der einzige, der einen adulten Kai packen kann.

**Warum ein gepackter Kai verloren ist:** Er ist es wie eine Robbe im Maul eines Orcas. Der Schutz liegt vor dem Griff:
- die Nachtwache,
- Ruhe weg von Kelp und Riffen,
- hohe Felsen, auf die der Sachi nicht kommt.

Mit Beachcomber ist der Sachi an Land schnell, ein flacher Strand schützt also nicht.

**Warum der Gruß das Geschlecht nicht mehr zeigt:** Kühe übernehmen den Gruß ihres Bullen, also grüßen beide gleich. Deshalb übernimmt die Unterart diese Aufgabe, wie beim Tylo.

**Leedsichthys in der Gruppe (bis 06.11.):** Ein Leeds durfte mit der Bale gruppieren, ohne Schutz in beide Richtungen. Seit dem 06.11. gruppieren Kai und Leeds nicht mehr (siehe unten).

**Entschieden am 06.11. (vom Nutzer): Kolonie statt Flotilla.**
- **Getrennt:** Kai und Leeds gruppieren nicht mehr. Die Flotilla ist jetzt der Leeds mit seinen Eurhinos.
- **Der Eurhino gehört zu beidem:** Als Schwarm zieht er mit dem Leeds. Bis zu 4 Eurhinos dürfen stattdessen bei einer Kai-Bale leben.
- **Die Balz bleibt genau so,** wie sie ist.
- **Kolonien:** Kai gehen wie Seelöwen sehr oft an Land, und mehrere Bales liegen dort zusammen.
- **Nicht sesshaft:** Eine große Kolonie frisst den Fisch um sich herum schnell leer. Deshalb schwimmen die Bales ständig hin und her, zwischen Fischgründen und Kolonien.
- **Der Leeds ist eigensinnig:** Er folgt zu 100 % seiner eigenen Route.
  - Einer Kai-Balz schaut er gern zu und hält dafür an, auch ohne Gruppe. Das Geplänkel gefällt ihm, und seine Eurhinos springen mit.
  - Sucht die Bale danach ihren Geburtsstrand auf, zieht er mit seinen Eurhinos seinen eigenen Weg. Nur wenn der Strand auf seiner Route liegt, schwimmen sie ein Stück zusammen.
- **Warum:** Jede Art funktioniert allein, das Treffen ist der Bonus. Kai nisten im Osten (Palm Islands, Castaway Isle, Volcano Islands), Leeds und Eurhino balzen im Westen an den Flüssen. Eine feste Kolonie ist außerdem leichter zu finden als eine wandernde Flotilla.
- **Echte Vorbilder:**
  - Seelöwen pendeln zwischen Kolonie und Fischgrund. Draußen schließen sie sich an, wo gefressen wird, zum Beispiel Delfinen und Buckelwalen an einem Fischschwarm.
  - Um große Seevogel-Kolonien bleibt ein Ring, in dem kaum noch Fisch ist. Genau deshalb müssen große Kolonien weit fahren oder weiterziehen.
  - Thunfische ziehen so zuverlässig mit Walhaien, dass Thunfischfänger ihre Netze gezielt um Walhaie legen. Das ist das Vorbild für den Eurhino beim Leeds.

**Selbst ergänzt, bitte prüfen:**
1. **Eurhinos in der Bale** zählen zusätzlich zu den 16 Kai, meist eine Freundesgruppe (2 bis 4). Das sind höchstens 20, genau wie die Mischherde des Para.
2. **In einer Kolonie verteidigt jede Bale nur sich selbst.** Sonst hätten 3 Bales zusammen 6 Verteidiger und 3 Schutzringe und wären nicht mehr zu jagen. In echten Kolonien verteidigt auch jede Mutter nur ihr eigenes Junges.
3. **Eine Kolonie hält höchstens einen Tag-Nacht-Zyklus,** dann ist der Fisch knapp, und jede Bale zieht ihren Weg. Folgen darf keine der anderen. Das ist die alte Regel „Bales teilen einen POI höchstens einen Tag“, nur dass sie jetzt zusammen an Land liegen dürfen.
4. **Treffen mit einer Flotilla:** Eine Bale darf eine Weile neben einer Flotilla schwimmen, auf der Bugwelle reiten und die Fische um den Riesen fangen. Das geht ohne Gruppe und ohne Verteidigung, höchstens einen Tag-Nacht-Zyklus wie bei anderen Bales. Der Leeds ändert seinen Kurs dafür nicht.
5. **Rückkehr in die Kolonie:** Wer vom Fischen zurückkommt, grüßt. Pelzrobben-Mütter finden ihr Junges in der Kolonie unter Hunderten an der Stimme. Dafür ist der eigene Gruß wie gemacht.
6. **Abstand zum Wasser:** Kai liegen gut oberhalb der Wasserkante, außerhalb der Reichweite des Tylo-Schubs (siehe Land-Sprint).

**Warum ohne feste Route:** Kai ziehen in die Ozeane, weil es dort die wertvollsten Fischarten gibt, nicht wegen des Leeds. Ihr Weg wechselt zwischen zwei Gefahren. In den Flussengen warten Xiphactinus-Schwärme, Deino und Spino. In den Ozeanen jagen Tylo, nachts Sachi, und außer bei Palm Islands Livy. Einen sicheren Ort gibt es nicht, nur bessere und schlechtere Zeiten.

**Entschieden am 10.11. (vom Nutzer): Großfisch und Rhythmus.** Kai ziehen in die Ozeane, weil es dort die Nahrung gibt, die sie brauchen. Daraus wird jetzt ein Rhythmus, aber keine strenge Pflicht, sondern eine Leitlinie im Profil wie die Salt Rocks beim Barsboldia: keine feste Zeit, aber irgendwann braucht er sie.
- **Großfisch:** Kai müssen regelmäßig große Fisch-AIs fressen: Kelp Shark, Manta Ray, Tuna und River Stingray. Kleiner Fisch hält sie am Leben, aber nicht gesund.
- **Sonst Probleme:** Ohne Großfisch bekommen sie Nutritional Stress, eine Rollenspiel-Krankheit wie die Hyponatremia beim Barsboldia. Sie werden mager, frieren, liegen länger in der Sonne, ermüden schnell, fallen hinter die Bale zurück und haben keine Lust mehr auf Spiel und Balz. Heilen kann nur Großfisch.
- **Echtes Vorbild:** die „Junk-Food-Hypothese“ bei Stellerschen Seelöwen in Alaska. Als fetter Hering knapp wurde und sie vor allem mageren Pollack fraßen, wurden sie trotz vollem Magen dünn, und es gab weniger und schwächere Junge.

**Selbst ergänzt, bitte prüfen:**
1. **Ab Sub-Adult,** wie beim Barsboldia. Kälber brauchen noch keinen Großfisch, Mütter und Tanten teilen ihn aber oft.
2. **Teilen zählt:** Ein Großfisch, den Mutter, Tante oder ein enger Freund teilt, zählt für den, der ihn frisst. Das passt zum Teilen, das schon im Profil steht.
3. **Keine Menge, keine Frist:** Wie beim Barsboldia steht keine Zahl drin, nur „at regular intervals“ und „semi-regular“. Wer eine feste Grenze will, könnte zum Beispiel einen Großfisch pro Tag-Nacht-Zyklus nehmen.
4. **Folgen bei der Balz:** Magere Kühe werden selten gewählt und nehmen selten einen Bullen an. Das ist der stärkste Teil und bleibt Rollenspiel, keine Sperre.

**Bitte prüfen, wo die Fische leben:** Wo die vier im Spiel spawnen, steht nicht in den Kartendaten. Der River Stingray lebt dem Namen nach eher in Flüssen. Gibt es ihn in den Flussengen, kann eine Bale ihren Großfisch dort holen und muss nie in die Ozeane, dann fehlt der Rhythmus. Dann sollte er raus oder nur halb zählen.

**Entschieden am 05.11. (vom Nutzer): Landtiere und Süßwasser.**
- **Neugier auf Landtiere:** Kai, Eurhino und Leeds sind unglaublich neugierig auf Landtiere. Kai kommen bei Pflanzenfressern auch aus dem Wasser und gehen an Land, um Hallo zu sagen. Laufen können sie gut. Die Flotilla springt dabei fröhlich in die Luft.
- **Warum:** Das verbindet die zwei Welten. Der Spieler sieht mehr von der Welt und kann mit ihr interagieren, statt nur im Wasser zu bleiben.
- **Süßwasser:** Die Flotilla darf Abstecher in die Seen und das Süßwasser machen, das mit den Flussengen verbunden ist. Krill gibt es im Spiel auch dort. Welche POIs das sind, steht nicht in den Kartendaten und muss im Spiel geklärt werden.

**Selbst ergänzt, bitte prüfen:** Besuche an Land zählen zur Landzeit, also zurück ins Wasser bei 75 % Durst. Zu Fleischfressern geht ein Kai nicht an Land, sie werden nur aus dem Wasser beobachtet. So steht es schon im heutigen Profil („Experienced adults do this only with herbivores“).

**Entschieden am 06.11. (vom Nutzer): Land-Sprint 300 mit Beachcomber.**
- Beachcomber bleibt ein wichtiger Teil des Kai: im Hide-Slot, +80 % Landtempo, +50 % Ausdauer-Erholung an Land, kein Austrocknen an Land, solange er nass ist. Erst danach sinkt sein Durst, die Durst-Regel bleibt also auch mit Beachcomber, wie sie ist (vom Nutzer geklärt am 06.11.).
- Mit Beachcomber sprintet der Kai an Land höchstens 300. Daraus folgt der Grundwert: 300 ÷ 1,8 ≈ **167** (Sprint-Multiplikator 1,67 auf die bisherigen 100). Gehen (30) und Traben (58) bleiben.
- *(Zuerst stand hier am selben Tag ein Grundwert von 250. Mit Beachcomber wären das 450 gewesen.)*
- **Warum:** An Land soll ein Kai etwas schneller sein als ein sprintender Tylo (160), aber langsamer als dessen Beachcomber-Schub. Den Schub bekommt der Tylo beim Verlassen des Wassers: 10 s lang bis zu +250 %, also von 560 abklingend auf 160, und nur alle 120 s.
- **Was das bedeutet:**
  - **Mit Beachcomber (300):** Der Tylo ist 6,5 s lang schneller und holt dabei rund 850 Einheiten auf. Liegt ein Kai näher an der Wasserkante, wird er erwischt. Liegt er weiter oben, entkommt er, und danach ist er um 140 pro Sekunde schneller.
  - **Ohne Beachcomber (167):** Der Tylo ist fast die ganzen 10 s schneller und holt rund 1900 Einheiten auf. Danach sind beide fast gleich schnell (167 gegen 160).
  - Vorbild sind die Orcas in Patagonien, die sich auf den Strand werfen, um Seelöwen zu packen.
- **Landjäger bleiben schneller:** Deino sprintet 550, Spino 700, Sarco 900. An Land rettet den Kai also nur der Weg ins Wasser.
- **Im Spiel prüfen:** Ob Beachcomber die 167 wirklich auf 300 bringt, hängt davon ab, wie das Spiel die Dynasty-Anpassung und den Bonus verrechnet (multipliziert oder addiert).

**Noch offen:**
- **Sachi an Land:** Sein Grundtempo ist unbekannt, weil seine Mod nicht heruntergeladen ist. Beachcomber gibt ihm dauerhaft +10 % und denselben Schub wie dem Tylo.

**Entschieden am 06.11. (vom Nutzer, aus dem Spieltest): Leitkuh und dominante Bullen.**
- **Die Leitkuh ist vor allem erfahren.** Bei Engagements macht sie nur ganz selten mit.
- **Ihr Wort beendet jeden Streit.** Bricht in der Bale Streit aus, egal worüber, kommt sie dazu. Alle hören sofort auf und gehorchen ihr. Auch ein Bulle, der doppelt so groß ist wie sie, gibt sofort nach.
- **Ihr gehört der beste Platz.** Die Bale überlässt ihr von selbst den höchsten Platz und den besten Sonnenplatz.
- **Die dominanten Bullen bleiben im Wasser,** wenn oben kein Platz mehr ist. Kommen die Kälber noch nicht auf die Felsen, bleiben die dominanten Bullen meist ebenfalls im Wasser und halten Wache.
- **Die dominanten Bullen machen fast alles:** Engagements, Wache, Verteidigung.
- **Vorbild:** große Hunderudel, in denen eine ältere Hündin dazukommt und selbst die großen Rüden sofort einlenken. Im Meer passen Orcas: Dort führt oft eine alte Kuh die Gruppe, gerade wenn der Fisch knapp ist, und erwachsene Söhne, deren alte Mutter noch lebt, tragen weniger Bissspuren aus Streit.
- **Der Gruß** wird noch einmal überdacht, weil das Abbrechen eines Emotes im Spiel nicht so gut funktioniert wie gedacht. Bis dahin bleibt er, wie er ist.

**Selbst ergänzt, bitte prüfen:**
1. **Das Zeichen:** Sie schwimmt zwischen die beiden und schüttelt einmal den Kopf (:shake_emote:). Beide hören sofort auf, schwimmen mindestens 2 Körperlängen auseinander und nehmen den Streit an diesem Tag nicht wieder auf. Der Kopfschüttler ist schon das Frust-Zeichen der Art und steckt nie in einem Gruß. Bei ihr heißt er: Schluss. Wer sie übergeht, gilt als übergriffig (Correcting Over-Aggression).
2. **Ritualkämpfe sind kein Streit.** Balz-Herausforderung, Kampf um freie Plätze und Herausforderung um die Begleitung haben eigene Regeln und ein eigenes Ende (unter 50 % oder :heart_call:). Die Leitkuh unterbricht sie nicht. Bricht einer die Regeln, zum Beispiel mit mehr als 2 Bissen pro Anlauf oder einem Biss nach dem :heart_call:, beendet sie den Kampf. So bleibt sie Schiedsrichterin und entscheidet nicht selbst über Plätze.
3. **„Ganz selten“ konkret:** Aktive Verteidigerin wird sie nur, wenn kein dominanter Bulle da ist. Als Mutter verteidigt sie ihr Kalb und ihr Nest wie jede Mutter. Ressourcen verteidigen die dominanten Bullen, sie nur ohne Bullen. *(Bisher stand bei den Ressourcen „meist die dominanten Bullen und die Leitkuh“.)*
4. **Platzordnung:** Die dominanten Bullen gehen als Letzte hoch. Ist oben kein Platz mehr, bleiben sie im Wasser.

---

Kaiwhekea is an extinct genus of plesiosaur from the Late Cretaceous (Maastrichtian age) of what is now New Zealand. Kaiwhekea belongs to the clade Aristonectinae inside the family Elasmosauridae. With their long necks, small heads and fine interlocking teeth, they fed on small, soft-bodied prey such as fish and squid. Like other plesiosaurs, they swam with four large flippers, and swallowed stones that are still found in their stomachs today.

Profile inspired by sea lions, elephant seals, sea turtles and bottlenose dolphins.

## Overview

**Medium Diurnal Aquatic Piscivore**

- Every Kaiwhekea has its own greeting. Cows take on the greeting of the bull they choose, for one season, or for life in a lifelong bond.
- Kaiwhekea form seasonal harems. After 5 successful clutches together, or by mutual agreement, a bull and a cow may become a lifelong pair.
- Kaiwhekea are nomadic. They roam back and forth between fishing grounds and haul-outs, and like sea lions they spend much of the day on land, often in colonies of several bales. They become territorial only around their nesting pools.
- Kaiwhekea have a passive nature.
- Kaiwhekea feed on fish, shellfish and squid. They do not hunt other players and do not claim or feed from bodies.
- Sub-Adult and older Kaiwhekea need large fish at regular intervals, which draws them out into the oceans. Without them they suffer nutritional stress (see Large Fish).
- Kaiwhekea may group with up to 4 Eurhinosaurus. They do not group with Leedsichthys. Kaiwhekea do not defend grouped Eurhinosaurus, and Eurhinosaurus do not defend them.
- Kaiwhekea tolerate albino and melanistic individuals.

## Genetics

Skins that are considered mutations for Kaiwhekea include: Albino, Melanistic.
Players must roll in genetics-rolls to use these skins in game. This is one roll per species, per character. Only when the character dies can you roll again if using the same character name.

## Group Limits (In-Game Group)

- Up to 16 Sub/Adults
- Unlimited Offspring
- Unlimited Adoptees

This group is known as a bale.

- **Bulls:** There is no limit on bulls. A bale has up to 2 dominant bulls, and any number of non-dominant bulls (see Bale Structure).
- **Eurhinosaurus:** Up to 4 Eurhinosaurus, usually one friend group, may join a bale in addition to the 16 Sub/Adults. An orphaned Eurhinosaurus calf may travel with the bale like any Eurhinosaurus, but it is not an adoptee.
- **Leedsichthys:** Kaiwhekea do not group with Leedsichthys. A Leedsichthys travels with its own flotilla of Eurhinosaurus (see Cross-Species Interaction).
- **Colonies:** Several bales may rest together at the same haul-out as a colony. They do not group beyond these limits, and each bale defends only itself (see Colonies).
- **Splitting:** Once offspring reach Sub-Adult, the bale must remain at 16 Sub/Adults or below. Lifelong pairs and mothers with their daughters stay together when a bale splits. Young bulls often leave at Sub-Adult (see Growing Up).
- **Bale size in courtship:** Bales may court at full size, but often split into smaller nursery groups to nest, so as not to empty the fishing grounds near the pools.

## Engagement Limits (Defence & Hunts)

**Predator Reaction: Flighty / Defensive**

- Up to 2 individuals may act as active defenders. In addition, up to 6 may form 1 protective ring around calves.
- The active defenders are usually the dominant bulls. The lead cow only becomes an active defender if no dominant bull is present (see Bale Structure).
- Active defenders move freely, but stay within 3 body lengths of the bale. See Predator Reaction for exceptions.
- Kaiwhekea never chase predators that flee or have forfeited.
- Kaiwhekea flee from Apex and Giant carnivores on sight, unless defending a nest with hatchlings.
- The protective ring does not accept other species or other adults. Once formed, the ring cannot swap out individuals.

**Abilities in an engagement:**
- Only the active defenders and the protective ring may use AoE Barrel Roll. It counts as an attack, not a buff.
- Only the active defenders may use Call to Arms.
- Every member may use Hustle and Rejuvenating Roar.
- The protective ring may use Rallying Horn.

## Resource Challenges

- Kaiwhekea only challenge or accept challenges for resources while nesting, or when offspring below Sub-Adult are present.
- At all other times they move away from a contested resource.
- Kaiwhekea do not accept territory challenges and simply move on.
- A maximum of 3 adults may defend resources such as a fishing ground or a nesting pool. These are usually the dominant bulls. The lead cow only joins if no dominant bull is present.

## Preferred Diet

- Kaiwhekea live on fish, shellfish and squid. Fish are their staple, and the richest ocean fishing grounds draw them out to sea again and again.
- **Large fish:** Sub-Adult and older Kaiwhekea need large fish at regular intervals: kelp sharks, manta rays, tuna and river stingrays. Without them they suffer nutritional stress (see Large Fish).
- Calves catch small fish on their own from the moment they can swim. Mothers and aunts often share their catch.
- Kaiwhekea swallow pebbles and shells from beaches and riverbeds. The stones help them grind their food, and to balance in the water.
- Kaiwhekea fish by day. At night they rest and do not fish.

## Nesting Limits

- 🌧️ Rainy Season: 4 🥚 per 4 hours.
- ☀️ Dry Season: 2 🥚 per 4 hours.

Kaiwhekea nest along the coastline or in shallow pools on coastal islands in their preferred POIs (Palm Islands, Castaway Isle, Volcano Islands). Their nests are built from kelp, branches and rocks. Each bale returns to the birth beach of its lead cow (see Nesting).
Kaiwhekea may form same-sex pairs and adopt and raise offspring together.

## Physical Dimorphism

- ♀️ Females, known as cows, are slimmer than males and have duller colors. Suitable subspecies include: tenui.
- ♂️ Males, known as bulls, are bulkier, with contrasting coloration. Suitable subspecies include: katiki, grandis.

Physical dimorphism for Kaiwhekea is mandatory. Cows take on the greetings of their bulls, so a greeting does not show sex. The subspecies does.

## Greetings

Every Kaiwhekea has its own greeting. It tells others who it is, and, for a cow, which bull she has chosen. Greetings are inspired by bottlenose dolphins, whose allied males match their signature whistles to each other, and by budgerigars, whose mated pairs match their calls.

- **Making a greeting:**
  - A greeting holds no more than 2 emotes and 2 abilities.
  - The only call allowed is :friendly_call:, which counts as an emote.
  - The only abilities allowed are Bite and Neck Swipe, aimed into open water. A bite or swipe that lands counts as an attack.
  - If a greeting holds 2 emotes, one of them must be cut short by a Bite or a Neck Swipe.
  - Greetings are quick, and made almost on the spot. A turn in a circle is the most movement allowed. There are no lunges or sprints, and no Barrel Roll, Fluke Slap or area abilities.
  - Greetings never contain a :shake_emote:, :threaten_call:, :danger_call: or :fear_call:. These remain warnings.
  - A greeting can be as simple as a nod. Every greeting is individual.
- **Remembering:**
  - Players choose their greeting freely, and must remember it. It is not written down.
  - A Kaiwhekea that carries another greeting for a season must still remember its own, and returns to it afterwards.
- **Using it:**
  - Both greet when two Kaiwhekea meet, or when bale mates meet again after time apart. When initiated, the greeting must be returned.
  - Kaiwhekea recognise friends by their greeting, and remember the greetings of bale mates for life.
  - Similar greetings attract each other. Kaiwhekea often feel drawn to those whose greeting resembles their own.
- **Taking on a greeting:**
  - A cow who chooses a bull in courtship takes on his greeting until the end of the season. Then she returns to her own.
  - In a lifelong bond, the pair matches their greetings to each other, and the cow takes on the bull's greeting for life. Same-sex pairs agree on whose greeting they share.
  - The lead cow always keeps her own greeting, even when she courts or bonds.
  - A cow whose lifelong partner has died may keep his greeting for the rest of her life, or return to her own.
- **The dominant greeting:**
  - Dominant bulls share one fixed greeting, the same for the whole species. Only dominant bulls, and the cows who chose them this season, may use it. *(The exact greeting is still to be decided.)*
  - A bull who wins a dominant place takes on the dominant greeting. A bull who loses his place returns to his own.
  - Every Kaiwhekea knows the dominant greeting, so dominant bulls are recognised in any bale. Male humpback whales all sing the same song, and in the courting season, half a bale may carry the dominant greeting in the same way.
- **Mistakes:**
  - Only clear mistakes count: a wrong or missing element, the wrong order, a pause of more than 3 seconds, or starting over. When in doubt, it was not a mistake.
  - Outside courtship, a mistake means nothing. In courtship, it is a sign of weakness (see Courtship).
- **Young Kaiwhekea:**
  - Calves copy their mother's greeting at first. From Adolescent on, every Kaiwhekea has its own.
  - Some keep a greeting close to a parent's, but they do not have to, and siblings sometimes share the same greeting.
  - Kaiwhekea do not form large family groups, and greetings are not passed down a bloodline.

## Growing Up

### Offspring Behavior

- **Hatchlings:** Kaiwhekea hatchlings, known as calves, hatch fully developed and able to swim, but they stay in the shallow pools where they were born until they reach Juvenile.
  - From birth they watch their mother leave the pool to rejoin her bale in deeper water, and wait behind in the shallows for safety.
  - Calves stay quiet in the pools, and only come out of hiding for their mother's greeting.
  - One or two aunts often stay near the pools while the mothers fish (see Bale Structure).
- **The run:** After reaching Juvenile, calves make the stretch into deeper water to meet their maternal bale.
  - The run happens in full daylight. Dawn and dusk belong to Xiphactinus, and night to Sachicasaurus.
  - The bale waits just offshore, giving soft :friendly_call:, and gathers the calves in as they arrive.
- **Juveniles:**
  - Juveniles swim in the middle of the bale, tucked against their mother's flank, where her wake carries them along with little effort. A calf that drifts out of this position calls out in alarm.
  - Mothers turn back at once for a calf that falls behind, and nudge it forward with soft calls.
  - Juveniles copy their mother's greeting, and greet every bale mate they meet with it.
  - They chase small fish and practise catching them, and learn the bale's fishing techniques by copying their mother.
- **Play:**
  - Play-fighting, leaping, chasing games and tossing kelp or shells to each other are common between calves.
  - Rough play near younger calves is stopped by a watching mother: a head shake first, then a gentle nip if it is ignored.
  - Friendships formed in play often last into adulthood.
- **Adolescents:**
  - Every Adolescent develops its own greeting (see Greetings).
  - **Young bulls** begin to spar, swim in sync with a best friend, and shadow the dominant bulls to learn their dance. Many practise the courtship dance with their best friend.
  - **Young cows** stay close to their mothers, help the aunts with younger calves, and learn the fishing grounds and haul-outs from the lead cow.
- **Sub-Adult:**
  - At Sub-Adult, offspring are adults of the bale and are expected to behave as such. This includes night watch, the protective ring and active defence.
  - Young cows usually stay in their natal bale for life.
  - Young bulls often leave their natal bale, but do not have to.
    - Those who leave often go together with their best friend, and may challenge the dominant bulls of another bale together (see Escort challenge).
    - Those who stay live on as non-dominant bulls.

### Orphaned Behavior

- Calves can survive on their own from the moment they hatch.
- Orphans without visible adults become skittish, and hide in shallow water, near the surface or among rocks, away from deep-water predators. They leave the pools later than calves with parents.
- Orphans seek out other Kaiwhekea, and also follow Eurhinosaurus shoals or an adult Leedsichthys. They call constantly, hoping for an answer.
- A bale readily adopts orphaned calves. An aunt is usually the first to take one in.
- Orphans that never find a bale grow up quieter and more independent than most of their kind, and often struggle to read social cues. Over time, this can improve.
- An orphan has no birth beach to remember. If an orphan becomes a lead cow, her bale chooses a beach for her (see Nesting).

## General Behavior

- **Swimming:** Kaiwhekea are fast and agile swimmers. They swim near the surface, rising to breathe in smooth rolls, and enjoy leaping out of the water as a form of play.
- **Scanning:** They are watchful, and often raise their head above the surface to look around, even while fishing.
- **Basking:** They bask in shallow water, on beaches and on high rocks (see Time on Land and Colonies).
- **Open water:** Kaiwhekea prefer clear water where they can see far. They become uneasy in murky channels and dense kelp, where predators can hide.
- **Voice:** Kaiwhekea are vocal by day. A steady chatter of calls tells the bale that all is well. The chatter falls silent at times of stress, and at night the bale stays quiet.
- **Curiosity:** Kaiwhekea are fascinated by land animals. When they meet terrestrial species near the water, they swim over to watch them, follow them along the shore, and often leap out of the water in excitement. Some attempt to get their attention by fetching objects from the seafloor and presenting them as trinkets. Experienced adults do this only with herbivores, and may go ashore to greet them (see Time on Land).

### Time on Land

Kaiwhekea are at home in the water, but like sea lions they haul out on land many times a day, to rest, bask and socialise. They may stay for as long as their skin allows. Like sea lions and penguins, which leap onto rocks and ice to escape orcas, they rest and sleep on high rocks where marine hunters cannot reach them.

- **Drying out:**
  - Kaiwhekea dislike drying out. Once their thirst drops to 75%, they return to the water. This takes about 10 minutes on land.
  - In great danger, for example with a hunter waiting below their rock, they may stay until their thirst drops to 50%. This takes about 20 minutes on land. They never stay longer.
- **Skin damage:**
  - A Kaiwhekea whose thirst drops below 50% on land suffers lasting skin damage.
  - The damage is painful, and the Kaiwhekea becomes infertile for the rest of its life. It no longer courts. This is noted in the character's bloodline-log.
  - Staying on land below 75% without great danger is a rule break, even if the Kaiwhekea returns before 50%.
- **High rocks:**
  - Kaiwhekea leap from the water onto high rocks to rest or sleep, safe from Tylosaurus, Sachicasaurus and Livyatan. Only rocks a Kaiwhekea can reach with a normal leap from the water may be used.
  - A healthy Kaiwhekea may also flee onto a rock from a hunter. One with heavy deathscars, or one bleeding to death, may not (rule 4.6).
  - A rock is a place for a nap, not for the whole night. Members often take turns on a rock while the rest of the bale rests in the shallows below.
- **The best places:**
  - On a rock or a beach, the bale leaves the highest place and the best spot in the sun to the lead cow, without being asked.
  - The dominant bulls go up last. If there is no room left, they stay in the water below.
  - Calves that cannot yet reach a rock stay in the shallows below. Their mothers may stay with them, and the dominant bulls usually stay in the water and keep watch around them.
- **Visiting land animals:**
  - Kaiwhekea walk well on land. Like curious sea lions, they may leave the water to greet herbivores at the shore: they walk up to them, call softly with :friendly_call:, and present their trinkets.
  - Visits count towards their time on land, so they return to the water once their thirst drops to 75% (see Drying out).
  - Carnivores are only watched from the water, never visited.
- **Beaches:**
  - Flat beaches are not safe. A Tylosaurus that throws itself onto the beach is faster than a Kaiwhekea for a few seconds. Kaiwhekea that rest well above the waterline are out of its reach, and once its burst fades, they outrun it on land. Sachicasaurus move quickly on land too.
  - Crocodylians and shore hunters can reach beaches and low rocks, and are faster on land. Kaiwhekea on land keep watch for them, and escape into the water.

### Colonies

Kaiwhekea are colonial, like sea lions. Wherever bales haul out together, a colony forms.

- **Haul-outs:** Colonies form on beaches and rocks along the coasts of the channels and oceans, close to good fishing grounds. Any coast can hold one, and the best are known to the elders.
- **Life in the colony:** A colony is loud and lively. Kaiwhekea chatter, greet each other, bask side by side and cross necks. Calves play at the water's edge under the eyes of the aunts. Members slip back into the water whenever their skin needs it, and haul out again.
- **Coming back:** Members returning from fishing greet their bale mates on arrival. Like fur seal mothers that find their pup among hundreds by its voice, Kaiwhekea find their own by their greeting.
- **Several bales:** Bales in a colony greet each other, rest side by side and may fish the same grounds. They do not group beyond the group limits. Each bale keeps its own defenders and its own protective ring, and no bale defends another.
- **Moving on:** A colony empties the fish around it quickly. Bales stay at a haul-out for no longer than one day–night cycle, then each bale goes its own way. As after any meeting, one bale may not follow another (see Other Bales).
- **Danger:** Colonies draw hunters. Tylosaurus and Sachicasaurus wait in the water below, and land carnivores come from the shore. Kaiwhekea rest well above the waterline, and the high rocks remain the safest place.

### Fishing

Kaiwhekea are skilled and cooperative fishers. Each bale develops its own techniques over time, and calves learn them from their mothers.

- **Taking turns:** At a gathering of fish, the bale circles the shoal and takes turns diving through it, one or two at a time, while the rest keep the fish together. Calves are allowed through first.
- **Herding:** In open water, the bale drives a shoal towards the surface or into a bay. Grouped Eurhinosaurus join in with their speed, and the two species fish side by side.
- **Kelp flushing:** At the edges of Kelp Vale, Kaiwhekea slap their flukes against the kelp to flush hidden fish into open water.
- **Strand fishing:** In the Mudflats and Coastland Swamp, a bale may drive fish against a mudbank, then lunge onto the bank after them and slide back into the water. Mudbanks are also where crocodylians wait, so the bale only strand-fishes where none have been seen.
- **Sharing:** Mothers, aunts and bonded friends share fish. Fish are not shared with Tropeognathus (see Cross-Species Interaction).

### Day and Night

- **Rhythm:** Kaiwhekea are diurnal. Mornings are for travel, the middle of the day for fishing, and the afternoon for socialising and basking at a haul-out. At dusk the bale moves to its resting place.
- **Resting place:**
  - The lead cow chooses where the bale rests: calm, shallow water near an island or near high rocks, where the bale can see the surface and the shore.
  - Experienced bales avoid known Sachicasaurus territories and banks where crocodylians were seen.
  - A bale that was attacked at a resting place does not use it again for the rest of the session.
- **Rest formation:** The bale rests close together at the surface, with calves in the centre, cows around them, and the bulls and grouped Eurhinosaurus on the outer edge.
- **Night watch:**
  - Up to 2 individuals stay awake and slowly circle the resting bale. These are usually the dominant bulls, relieved by non-dominant bulls. Cows only keep watch if no bulls are present.
  - Watch duty is passed on with a gentle nudge: the watcher swims softly into the next one, who takes over.
  - Grouped Eurhinosaurus often take on part of the watch. Their loud calls give the earliest warning.
  - On a threat, the watcher gives a :danger_call: and slaps the water with its fluke. The bale wakes, pulls the calves into the centre and leaves as one group.

### Downtime

Night is a time for rest and bonding. The bale does not migrate, fish or court at night, but Kaiwhekea do not need to sleep the whole night.

- **Socialising:** Kaiwhekea that wish to socialise call a bale mate over with a soft :friendly_call:. Those who answer may rest alongside, brush along each other's bodies, or cross necks.
- **Calves:** Calves younger than Adolescent may play quietly, for example hide and seek among the sleeping adults. Calves that keep waking others are escorted back to their mothers by the watch.
- **Pairs and harems** may rest a little apart from the main bale, but always within sight of the watch.
- **Noise:** Loud calls at night are corrected with a head shake.

### Migration

Bales are nomadic, but follow no fixed route. They swim back and forth between fishing grounds and haul-outs, and are always on the move.

- **Channels:** Bales roam the warm saltwater channels between the islands: the Volcano Islands, Twisted Forest, Stillwater Bog, the Mudflats and Coastland Swamp.
- **Oceans:** From there, bales regularly head out into the open oceans of Coastal Bluffs, Kelp Vale, the Abyssal Depths, Palm Islands and Stonebed Shoal. The oceans hold the fish Kaiwhekea value most, and the richest fishing grounds of the year. Their need for large fish gives the bale its rhythm, back and forth between the channels and the oceans (see Large Fish).
- **Moving on:** A bale, and even more a colony, empties the fish around it quickly. Bales move on once the fish grows scarce, and rarely stay in one place for longer than a day–night cycle.
- **Seasons:** In the Rainy Season, bales spend longer in the channels, where the rivers bring fish. In the Dry Season, the channels grow warm, and bales spend longer in the cooler oceans.
- **Flotillas:** Bales do not follow the Leedsichthys route. When a bale meets a flotilla, it may travel alongside it for a while (see Cross-Species Interaction).
- **Fresh water:** Bales may leave the coasts for detours into the lakes and fresh waters connected to the channels, where land animals come to drink. Spinosaurus live there, and land carnivores wait on the banks.
- **Tradition:** The best fishing grounds and haul-outs are passed down between generations, mostly by the lead cow and the elders.

### Large Fish

Small fish keep a Kaiwhekea alive, but not healthy. Sub-Adult and older Kaiwhekea need large fish at regular intervals: kelp sharks, manta rays, tuna and river stingrays. There is no fixed time, but every Kaiwhekea feels when it has gone too long without them.

- **The pull of the oceans:** Large fish are what draws bales out of the channels and into the oceans again and again. Bales usually set out when several members begin to need them.
- **Sharing counts:** A large fish shared by a mother, an aunt or a bonded friend counts for the one who eats it.
- **Calves:** Below Sub-Adult, calves do not need large fish yet, but mothers and aunts often share them.

### Nutritional Stress

Sub-Adult and older Kaiwhekea that do not eat large fish on a semi-regular basis suffer from nutritional stress.

- This is a roleplay behavior, and not a built-in in-game mechanic.
- They grow lean and lose their blubber. They feel the cold, and spend longer basking in the sun at a haul-out.
- They tire quickly, swim slowly, dive for shorter times and fall behind the bale.
- They lose interest in play, greetings and courtship. Lean cows are rarely chosen, and rarely accept a bull.
- The only cure is to eat large fish again. The Kaiwhekea slowly regains its strength.
- Like Steller sea lions that lived on lean fish instead of fatty fish, a bale that never leaves the channels grows thin, even with full stomachs.

### Albino & Melanistic Behavior

- Both albino and melanistic Kaiwhekea are accepted and cared for by their bales.
- **Albino** Kaiwhekea are sensitive to bright sunlight. They bask less, rest in the shade of cliffs and kelp at midday, and find the Dry Season especially hard.
- **Melanistic** individuals suffer no health problems, but warm up faster at the surface, and cool off in deeper water more often.

## Communication

- **Greetings:** A greeting is the first thing two Kaiwhekea exchange when they meet (see Greetings).
- **Finding each other:** A member separated from the bale gives a :broadcast_call:, and the bale answers. Once they see each other, they greet.
- **Contentment and affection:** soft :friendly_call: chatter, swimming side by side, brushing along each other's bodies, and crossed necks.
- **Discomfort and unease:** a head shake (:shake_emote:), looking around above the surface (:question_emote:), or swimming restlessly at the edge of the bale. These are not signs of aggression.
- **Frustration:** A frustration signal always begins with a head shake (:shake_emote:). Greetings never contain one, so the two cannot be mistaken. After the head shake, a Kaiwhekea may also:
  - snap at the water in front of the other, without landing the bite;
  - slap the water beside it with a Fluke Slap, without landing the hit.

  These are done once, and are respected at once: the other moves away by at least 1 body length.
- **Peace:** A head shake from the lead cow ends any quarrel in the bale at once (see Bale Structure).
- **Alarm:** a :danger_call: and a hard fluke slap on the water. Every Kaiwhekea that hears it raises its head and looks for the threat.

## Social Interaction

Kaiwhekea are a highly social species. Bales migrate together in mixed-sex groups.

### Bale Structure

Bales are loosely structured. They are guided by a lead cow and escorted by up to 2 dominant bulls, and aunts help raise the young.

- **Lead cow:**
  - The lead cow is an experienced cow, often the oldest, who knows the fishing grounds and haul-outs best. She swims at the front, and decides where the bale travels, fishes and rests.
  - She leads through experience, not strength, and rarely takes part in engagements. She only becomes an active defender if no dominant bull is present, and defends her own calf and nest like any mother.
  - She is the first to welcome newcomers into the bale, and the first to correct over-aggression.
  - **Keeping the peace:** If a quarrel breaks out in the bale, about anything, the lead cow swims between the two and shakes her head once (:shake_emote:). Both stop at once, move at least 2 body lengths apart, and do not pick the quarrel up again that day. Even a bull twice her size gives way to her at once. Ignoring her counts as over-aggression (see Correcting Over-Aggression).
  - **Spin fights** in courtship, over free places and in escort challenges are not quarrels. They follow their own rules, and she does not stop them, unless a bull breaks those rules, for example by biting more than twice in a pass, or after a :heart_call:.
  - **The best place:** The bale leaves her the highest place and the best spot in the sun, without being asked (see Time on Land).
  - Her birth beach is the bale's nesting beach (see Nesting). She is the only Kaiwhekea who always keeps her own greeting, and every member of her bale knows it.
  - Other cows may disagree with her choice. A cow shows this by swimming in front of the lead cow and slapping the water with her fluke. If several cows disagree, the bale follows the choice of the majority of cows. On an even split, the lead cow decides.
  - The role is not fought over. When a lead cow dies, leaves or grows too old, the cow the others follow most often takes her place.
  - Bulls guide the bale only when no cow is willing to lead.
- **Dominant bulls:**
  - A bale has up to 2 dominant bulls. They carry the dominant greeting, escort the bale on its outer edge, and drive away rival bulls.
  - They do most of the bale's work. They are the usual active defenders, keep most of the night watch, defend resources and make the mock charges. When there is no room on a rock, or the calves cannot reach it yet, they stay in the water and keep watch.
  - They dance first in courtship, and most cows prefer them.
  - The 2 dominant bulls need not be friends, but they do not fight each other over cows. A cow chooses between them, and the other accepts it.
  - A dominant bull who forms a lifelong bond gives up his place at the next courtship ritual, and returns to his own greeting.
- **Non-dominant bulls:**
  - Every other bull of the bale is non-dominant. They live in the bale like any member, take part in the watch and the defence, and dance in the second round of courtship with their own greeting.
  - Unbonded non-dominant bulls may challenge a dominant bull who makes a mistake in courtship (see Courtship).
- **Free places:**
  - A place falls free when a dominant bull dies, leaves the bale or bonds. A new bale starts with both places free.
  - The unbonded bulls of the bale may fight for a free place at any time by day, using the procedure for spin fights (see Aggressive Interaction). If only one unbonded bull is present, he takes the place without a fight.
- **Escort challenge:**
  - Foreign bulls may challenge the dominant bulls of a bale for their places. One or two challengers swim to the front of the bale, give a :threaten_call: and slap the water with their flukes.
  - The dominant bulls must answer within 10 seconds, or step aside.
  - Each place is fought for bull against bull, one fight at a time (see Aggressive Interaction). Two challengers who win both fights take both places. If each side wins one, the cows decide which pair escorts the bale, by swimming alongside the pair they choose.
  - The winners take on the dominant greeting, and beaten challengers leave the bale.
  - Beaten dominant bulls return to their own greetings. They may stay as non-dominant bulls, or leave. Cows who chose a beaten dominant bull this season decide for themselves. If they follow him, they take on his own greeting. If they stay with the place, they keep the dominant greeting.
  - A dominant bull may be challenged no more than once per session, whether in courtship or for the escort.
- **Aunts:**
  - Cows without calves of their own often help raise the calves of others. They stay near the nesting pools while the mothers fish, and watch over juveniles during fishing.
  - Aunts may correct unruly calves when the mother is absent, and support new or overwhelmed mothers.
  - An aunt who stands guard at the pools may take part in the protective ring.

### Bale Behavior

- **Harmony:** Members share space peacefully and treat each other with respect, regardless of sex.
- **Bonding:**
  - Bonded Kaiwhekea, such as the cows of one harem, pairs, close kin or tight friends, swim side by side, breach in sync and rest with their necks gently crossed.
  - Cows who carry the same greeting in a season often swim together.
  - Gift-giving is a common sign of affection, for example a shell, a bright stone or a piece of kelp.
  - Close friends of any sex share fish and groom each other by brushing along each other's bodies.
- **Bulls and cows:** Bulls let cows and calves through to the fish first, and swim on the outer edge of the bale to shield them. Cows value this, and remember it when they choose a partner.
- **Play:** Adults play too. Leaping contests, bow-riding in the wake of a Leedsichthys and chasing games are common. Play never turns into real biting. Anyone who bites in play is corrected by the others.
- **Solitary time:** Kaiwhekea also enjoy time alone, sunbathing on beaches and rocks or swimming spread out, careful not to crowd one another.
- **Injured members:** Injured Kaiwhekea swim in the middle of the bale, flanked by bonded friends. The bale slows for them, but does not stop in a POI where an Apex carnivore was seen.

### Other Bales

- When two bales meet, their members greet each other warmly, one by one, and may exchange members.
- Dominant bulls of different bales recognise each other by the dominant greeting, and keep a respectful distance.
- At a rich fishing ground, two bales may fish together for that day, but never group beyond the group limits.
- Bales share a POI, or a haul-out as a colony, for no longer than one day–night cycle. Once one bale moves on, the other may not follow it. A bale that tries to follow is turned away with head shakes.

### Solitary Kaiwhekea

- Solitary Kaiwhekea are rare. Most are young bulls between bales, or individuals shunned from a bale.
- Solitary Kaiwhekea give a :broadcast_call: often, hoping to hear a bale answer. They only do this when they can see far around them.
- Long, unwanted solitude makes them restless and wary. They swim close to the shore, and flee earlier than Kaiwhekea in a bale.

### Correcting Over-Aggression

Over-aggression is rare and not tolerated.

1. Correction begins with a head shake and a hard slap of the flipper on the water surface.
2. If it is ignored, nearby members block the aggressor with their bodies and give :threaten_call:.
3. If it continues, they follow up with firm nips. Other members quickly rally to help push the aggressor away.
4. If the behavior continues despite this, the bale shuns the individual.

The lead cow can end it at any step with her head shake (see Bale Structure).

Over-aggression is never defended by bale mates, regardless of relations. Anyone who defends an aggressive individual is shunned along with it.

## Aggressive Interaction

- Kaiwhekea avoid conflict with their own species, except in courtship, over free places and in escort challenges.
- Ignoring is always their first response to aggression from another Kaiwhekea. Bulls that drift too close to another bull's harem quickly slip away.
- Kaiwhekea do not give :threaten_call: to bale mates, except in courtship, in challenges or when correcting over-aggression.
- **Spin fights between bulls:** Every fight between bulls follows the same procedure.
  1. The two bulls circle each other at the surface, giving 3 :threaten_call: between them.
  2. They stop face to face, and each performs his greeting. The dominant greeting can be intimidating, and a challenger may back down here.
  3. If both then give one more :threaten_call:, the fight begins. A bull who does not give it within 10 seconds backs down. If the challenger backs down, his challenge is used up. If a dominant bull backs down, he loses his place.
  4. They use only their Bite. Each makes no more than 2 bite attempts, then they swim past each other, turn, and meet again for 2 more.
  5. This repeats until one bull drops below 50% health, or gives up with a :heart_call:. Either counts as a loss. A fight usually takes 5 or 6 passes.
- Losing a fight does not lower a bull's standing in the bale. Self-preservation is valued.
- These fights rarely end in death, but leave deep scars on both.

## Cross-Species Interaction

Kaiwhekea are tolerant towards other species and avoid conflict at all times. When they meet a new species they approach it with curiosity, though experienced adults keep a safe distance from carnivores.

- **Eurhinosaurus:**
  - Kaiwhekea welcome them as bale mates. Up to 4, usually one friend group, may group with a bale. Most Eurhinosaurus travel with a Leedsichthys instead. Eurhinosaurus stay with a bale because they gain more from it than they give: safety in numbers, and the fish the bale drives together. Their speed and loud voices make them excellent sentries.
  - The two species fish together and share fishing grounds. Eurhinosaurus scouts often find the fish first, and the lead cow decides whether the bale follows their call.
  - When Kaiwhekea haul out or nap on high rocks, grouped Eurhinosaurus stay in the water below and keep watch.
  - An orphaned Eurhinosaurus calf is welcomed kindly, like any Eurhinosaurus. It is not adopted, and has no place in the protective ring.
  - Kaiwhekea do not defend grouped Eurhinosaurus, but call out in grief if a bonded one is lost.
  - **In courtship:** Eurhinosaurus watch the dance closely. It excites them and puts them in a happy mood, and they often breach along with the bulls. Kaiwhekea do not let this distract them.
- **Leedsichthys:**
  - Kaiwhekea do not group with Leedsichthys. A Leedsichthys travels with its own flotilla of Eurhinosaurus, and follows its own route without exception.
  - When a bale meets a flotilla, Kaiwhekea may swim alongside it for a while. They ride the giant's bow wave, race its Eurhinosaurus and fish the shoals that gather around it. The Leedsichthys never changes course for them, so the bale turns back once the route leads away from its own way. Like two bales, they stay together for no longer than one day–night cycle.
  - Neither side defends the other. Kaiwhekea may hide behind or beneath the giant, but the Leedsichthys never moves to block a predator on their behalf. Kaiwhekea may not lead a predator to it on purpose to save themselves.
  - **In courtship:** A passing Leedsichthys loves the splashing and banter of a courting bale, and often stops to watch, even though it is not grouped with it. Its Eurhinosaurus breach along with the bulls. When the bale leaves for its nesting beach, the Leedsichthys goes its own way with its Eurhinosaurus. Only if the beach lies on its route do they travel on together for a while.
- **Tropeognathus:** Tropeognathus follow flotillas from the air and dive in wherever fish gather, also where a bale is fishing. Fish are the one thing a bale does not share, so Kaiwhekea may drive them off. They give a :threaten_call: first, then charge and snap at it until it leaves the water. This is not a hunt: once the Tropeognathus is airborne, they let it go.
- **Lurdusaurus and other herbivores at the shore:** Kaiwhekea are fascinated by them. They follow them along the shore, go ashore to greet them, and like to present them with trinkets from the seafloor (see Time on Land).
- **Carnivores at the shore:** Austroraptor and other shore hunters are a danger to calves in the pools, and to Kaiwhekea resting on beaches. Aunts and mothers keep watch, and calves hide deeper in the pool when one wades in.
- **Apex carnivores:** Kaiwhekea flee from adult Tylosaurus, Sachicasaurus, Livyatan, Spinosaurus and Deinosuchus on sight (see Hunted by Apex).
- **Xiphactinus:** Single Xiphactinus are watched closely. Packs of 3 or more are treated as an active threat. The bale stays close together, because Xiphactinus try to cut single Kaiwhekea off from the group.
- **Sarcosuchus:** As a Large carnivore, Sarcosuchus are met with the protective ring rather than flight.

## Predator Reaction

At the first sign of danger, Kaiwhekea raise their heads above the surface to look around, and alert the bale with a :danger_call:. Nearby bale mates mirror the movement. The bale gathers, calves move into the centre, and the bale either forms a ring or flees.

- If a member gives a false alarm, the others look around, then calm it with soft calls.
- Small carnivores are ignored in small numbers, but treated as a threat at 4 or more. Xiphactinus are a threat from 3.
- If a hunt begins, defenders do not rush out to an attacked member. It must swim back to the bale within 30 seconds.

### Solo and Duo Reaction

- **Solo:** Without a bale, a Kaiwhekea is nervous, and may flee or turn defensive when a predator is spotted. If it fights, it may move to adjust its position, but does not chase its attackers. Apex carnivores are always fled from.
- **Duo:** A pair is more confident, but may still flee. Once both have turned to face the threat, they stay together until the hunt is over, and drift no more than 1 body length apart to land a hit. They do not chase. Apex carnivores are always fled from.

### Hunted by Non-Apex

- **Defenders:** Up to 2 adults, usually the dominant bulls, step forward as active defenders. The lead cow only steps forward if no dominant bull is present. Once the engagement starts, they cannot be swapped out, and cannot join the ring.
- **Reaching the bale:** An attacked individual must swim back to the bale within 30 seconds to receive defenders. Being within 2 body lengths of the bale counts as reaching it.
  - If it does not reach the bale in time, or turns to fight, it counts as a defender.
  - Any Kaiwhekea may use Fluke Slap in self-defence while being hunted without counting as a defender. Turning to bite or barrel roll makes it an active defender.
- **Mock charges:** Defenders stay within 3 body lengths of the bale. They may charge further out at a predator in a mock charge, with snapping and fluke slaps, if:
  - the predator sits or rests at the surface;
  - the predator stays still for more than 15 seconds;
  - or the predator does not come back within reach after three :threaten_call: and 10 seconds.
- **Protective ring:**
  - Up to 6 adults form a ring around the calves. Mothers join the ring if they get there in time; otherwise the nearest adults form it.
  - Members stay side by side, facing outwards. They may leave the ring by 1 body length to land a hit, then return to it.
  - An attacked member outside a full ring counts as part of the fleeing bale.
- **The fleeing bale:** Everyone else flees, using Hustle, and pushes stragglers ahead of it. The defenders block the predators' way. If the predators chase the fleeing bale, it may form a new ring, and the old ring breaks up. Defenders who lose sight of the bale may be replaced by new ones at the new ring.
- **Mothers:** A mother may always land a hit on a predator attacking her calf below Sub-Adult, if the calf is within 1 body length of her. She does not count as a defender unless she is in the ring.
- **When the hunters forfeit:** Kaiwhekea let them go and do not pursue.

### Hunted by Apex – Not Nesting

- Kaiwhekea flee from Apex and Giant carnivores on sight. While fleeing they may defend themselves, but must prioritise escape. They may not pace themselves to tire out with another member, approach the Apex, or intervene in an Apex's chase of another member.
- **Where to flee:**
  - **Livyatan:** into the shallows. Livyatan do not follow prey into shallow water.
  - **Tylosaurus:** along the surface at full speed, turning sharply. Kaiwhekea are faster than Tylosaurus, but a Tylosaurus can keep up the chase for longer. When basking or nesting on beaches, Kaiwhekea keep an eye on the water, because Tylosaurus lunge out of it.
  - **Deinosuchus and Spinosaurus:** away from banks and shores, into deeper, open water.
  - **Sachicasaurus:** out of the kelp and the reef, into open water as one tight group, or up onto a high rock. Sachicasaurus move quickly on land, so a flat beach is no refuge.
- **Seized by a Sachicasaurus:**
  - Sachicasaurus are the only hunters strong enough to seize a grown Kaiwhekea, and a seized Kaiwhekea is lost. Like a seal in the jaws of an orca, it cannot count on its bale. The bale does not attack the Sachicasaurus, and flees.
  - The seized Kaiwhekea may fight back. If the Sachicasaurus lets go, it flees at once.
  - The bale's defence lies before the grab: the night watch, resting away from kelp and reefs, and high rocks out of reach.
  - A nearby Leedsichthys can offer cover to hide behind, but no protection.
- Kaiwhekea do not flee from Leedsichthys, which do not hunt them.

### Hunted by Apex – Nesting

- Kaiwhekea only stand their ground at a nest with hatchlings, and only against a single Apex.
- Up to 2 parents may defend one nest and must stay within 1 body length of it. A bull may move between the nests of his harem to protect his calves.
- They may charge a predator that provokes them, for example by resting at the edge of the pool, but must return to the nest at once.
- If an Apex pair approaches, they flee and the calves hide in the shallows.

## Courtship & Parenthood

### Harems

- Harems form anew each season. A bull's harem is made up of the cows who chose him in courtship, and who carry his greeting until the season ends. Dominant bulls usually hold the largest harems.
- The harem bull holds the nesting rights with the cows of his harem for that season.
- A bull may accept several cows. A bonded bull accepts only his partner.
- Same-sex pairs may form. They do not nest, but may adopt.

### Courtship

Courtship is a ritual, held by day in open, clear water where the bale can watch for predators. It is inspired by elephant seals, whose cows gather where the dominant bulls hold their place.

- **Calls:** During the courting season both sexes become extremely vocal underwater. Long-distance calls (:broadcast_call:) are used to find a mate.
- **Who courts:** Infertile Kaiwhekea, such as those with lasting skin damage, do not court.

The ritual follows these steps:

1. **Cows show interest:** Cows ready to court breach the water again and again in high leaps, and give loud :broadcast_call:.
2. **The dominant dance:** The dominant bulls dance first. They breach at regular intervals, and perform the dominant greeting and :broadcast_call: in turn.
3. **Challenge:** Faltering in the greeting is a clear sign of weakness.
   - Right after the dance, an unbonded bull may challenge a dominant bull who made a mistake in his greeting during the dance (see Greetings). Without a mistake, there is no challenge.
   - Non-dominant bulls of the bale, and unbonded foreign bulls present at the ritual, may challenge. Bonded bulls may not.
   - Each dominant bull may be challenged only once per ritual.
   - The fight follows the procedure for spin fights (see Aggressive Interaction).
   - The winner is dominant. He keeps the dominant greeting, or takes it on if he was not dominant before. He does not need to dance again.
   - The loser returns to his own greeting, and may dance in the second round.
4. **The closing calls:** The dominant bulls give 2 :friendly_call:, then swim a short distance away from the cows to their places, and wait.
5. **Choosing a dominant bull:**
   - Cows who want a dominant bull swim to him, and perform the dominant greeting. They carry it until the season ends.
   - Most cows prefer the dominant greeting.
   - A cow who has gone to a dominant bull cannot change her choice in this ritual.
6. **The second round:**
   - The other bulls now perform the same dance, each with his own greeting.
   - Cows who have not yet chosen watch, and may choose a bull whose greeting they like. They swim to him and perform his greeting. Similar greetings attract each other.
   - Bulls who are not chosen do not hold it against the cow.
   - Bonded bulls dance too, but accept only their partner, who already carries their greeting.
7. **The lead cow** chooses like any cow, but always keeps her own greeting.
8. **The journey:** Once courtship is over, the lead cow leads the bale to the nesting beach (see Nesting). The harems of the dominant bulls lay their nests first.

Eurhinosaurus and passing Leedsichthys often watch the ritual (see Cross-Species Interaction).

### Lifelong Bonds

- After 5 successful clutches together, or by mutual agreement, a bull and a cow may become a lifelong pair. Lifelong pairs are monogamous: a bonded bull accepts only his partner.
- The pair matches their greetings to each other, and the cow takes on the bull's greeting for life (see Greetings).
- A lifelong pair always rejoins the same bale, and travels together even when the rest of the bale is not around.
- Bonded bulls still dance for their partner in the second round, and bring her gifts.
- Bonded bulls cannot challenge for a dominant place. A dominant bull who bonds gives up his place at the next ritual.
- **Jealousy:** If another Kaiwhekea courts a bonded partner, the partner may place itself between them and shake its head once or twice. This never escalates to biting. The courted partner then decides: stay with the bond, or open it.
- **Rejection:** A bonded Kaiwhekea who does not want a suitor turns away from it. If the suitor persists, it shakes its head and slaps the water between them. After this, the suitor stops.
- **Mourning:**
  - A cow who is abandoned or ignored by her lifelong partner may leave him, and returns to her own greeting.
  - After a lifelong bond ends, she does not court or nest for a while.
  - A widowed cow may keep her partner's greeting for the rest of her life.

### Nesting

- **The birth beach:** Like sea turtles, a bale returns to nest at the beach or pool where its lead cow hatched, if it lies in one of the preferred POIs.
  - If the lead cow does not know her birth beach, for example as an orphan, the bale chooses one peacefully, by the majority of cows.
- **Success:** A nesting is successful if at least half of one clutch, rounded up, survives to Juvenile: 2 calves from 4 eggs, or 1 from 2.
  - After a successful nesting, the beach becomes the lead cow's birth beach, even if she hatched elsewhere. It also becomes the birth beach of every cow present who may lead one day, if she has none of her own.
  - The bale keeps its beach as long as at least one clutch there is successful.
  - If no clutch at the birth beach succeeds, the lead cow gives it up. The bale chooses a new beach for the next nesting, by the majority of cows.
  - Like black-legged kittiwakes, Kaiwhekea stay where they succeed, and move on where they fail.
- **Nesting together:** The cows of a bale nest on the same day, side by side along the same shore.
- **Space:** Cows place their nests at least 1 body length apart. Mothers, sisters and daughters nest close together.
- **Splitting:** A full bale often splits into smaller nursery groups to nest, so as not to empty the fishing grounds near the pools.

### Offspring Rearing

1. When preparing to nest, cows head to coastal shallow pools and build simple nests.
2. After all young hatch, mothers return to deeper water nearby. Aunts take turns guarding the pools while the mothers fish. The bale stays close to the nesting area until the calves reach Juvenile.
3. Mothers come back to the pools often, and greet their calves with their greeting. Anxious new mothers who check the pool too often are gently calmed by the other cows.
4. At Juvenile, the calves make their run into deeper water in daylight, and the bale travels on, with the bulls on the outside.
5. Bulls protect their bale and their calves until the calves reach Adolescent. Bonded bulls stay with the bale afterwards. Unbonded bulls may leave to find another bale.

### Adoption

- Cows and bulls may adopt orphaned Kaiwhekea, and same-sex pairs often raise a calf together. An adopted calf usually copies the greeting of its new mother.
- Eurhinosaurus calves are not adopted (see Cross-Species Interaction).

## Elderly & End of Life (Optional)

- **Old age:** When Kaiwhekea reach old age, they slow down and swim at the back of the bale, with bonded friends or a lifelong partner beside them. The bale slows its pace for them.
- **Keepers of the coast:** Elders remember the oldest fishing grounds, haul-outs and resting places. Many lead cows are elders, and young cows follow them to learn the way.
- **Nesting:** Elders lose most of their wish to court. Some still nest, but often lay infertile eggs, or only one that hatches. Many take on the role of an aunt instead.
- **Colour:** As they near the end of their lives, they become less vocal, and may shed into darker or duller coloration.
- **The last journey:**
  - A dying elder swims to the front of the bale, nudges the lead cow and gives soft, tired calls. The lead cow lets it set the pace, and the bale follows it to a place of meaning: a favourite fishing ground, its own nesting beach, or where a bonded friend died.
  - There, bonded friends, offspring and partners stay close, and hold it up at the surface so it can breathe, until they can no longer do so.
  - Most Kaiwhekea die when they no longer have the strength to resurface. The bale lets the body sink. Its friends perform its greeting over the place for a while after.
- **Calves:** A mother who loses a calf may push it along at the surface for some time before she lets it go.
- Elders who have no bale left seek deeper, quieter waters, and live out their days alone.

## Rule Exemptions

- **Grouping:** As piscivores, Kaiwhekea may group with up to 4 Eurhinosaurus (exemption from 8.1, under the proposed piscivore rule). There is no defence between the species.
- **Abilities:** In a mixed group, only active defenders use Call to Arms (8.1.1).

## Profile – Quick View

- Kaiwhekea are Medium Diurnal Aquatic Piscivores.
- They group in bales of up to 16 Sub/Adults, with unlimited offspring and adoptees. Up to 4 Eurhinosaurus may join, without any defence between the species. They do not group with Leedsichthys.
- Like sea lions, they haul out on land many times a day, often in colonies of several bales. In a colony, each bale defends only itself, and after one day–night cycle each goes its own way.
- They are passive, feed only on fish, shellfish and squid, and never hunt other players. From Sub-Adult they need large fish at regular intervals, or suffer nutritional stress. They fish together by day, with techniques passed from mother to calf.
- An experienced lead cow guides the bale. She rarely fights, but ends any quarrel with a single head shake, and the bale leaves her the best place on the rock. Up to 2 dominant bulls escort the bale and do most of the work: defence, the night watch, and staying in the water when there is no room above. Aunts help raise the calves.
- Every Kaiwhekea has its own greeting, which players must remember. Cows take on the greeting of their chosen bull for one season, or for life in a lifelong bond. Dominant bulls share one fixed greeting.
- Physical dimorphism is mandatory, because greetings do not show sex.
- 2 active defenders protect the bale, and up to 6 form a ring around the calves. They flee from Apex and Giant carnivores on sight, except at a nest. A Kaiwhekea seized by a Sachicasaurus is lost.
- They may stay on land until their thirst drops to 75%, or 50% in great danger, and nap on high rocks. Below 50% they become infertile.
- Courtship is a ritual: cows breach and call, the dominant bulls dance first and may be challenged after a mistake in their greeting, and cows choose a bull by taking on his greeting.
- Bales nest at the birth beach of their lead cow, and change it after a failed nesting.
- They roam by day, without a fixed route, back and forth between fishing grounds and haul-outs in the saltwater channels and the oceans, with detours into the fresh waters connected to the channels. They move on once the fish runs low, and rest in the shallows at night under watch.
- They do not follow the Leedsichthys, but may swim alongside a passing flotilla for a while. A Leedsichthys often stops to watch their courtship.
- They are fascinated by land animals. They follow them along the shore, leap in excitement, and go ashore to greet herbivores.
