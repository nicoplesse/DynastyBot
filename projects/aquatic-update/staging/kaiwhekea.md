# Kaiwhekea
"Kai"
Kaiwhekea are long-necked plesiosaurs that live in bales along the warm coasts and channels of Riparia. Like sea lions, they haul out on land many times a day, rest together in loud colonies and fish side by side by day. Every Kaiwhekea carries its own greeting, and each bale is guided by an experienced lead cow and escorted by its dominant bulls.

%% banner Overview
**Medium Diurnal Aquatic Piscivore**
Kaiwhekea form seasonal harem bonds. After 5 successful clutches together or mutual agreement, these can develop into lifelong pairbonds.
Kaiwhekea are nomadic, but become territorial around their nesting pools.
Kaiwhekea have a passive nature.
Kaiwhekea are not scavengers. They feed only on fish, shellfish and squid, and do not hunt other players.
Kaiwhekea may group with up to 4 Eurhinosaurus. Kaiwhekea may not group with Leedsichthys.
Kaiwhekea may not defend grouped Eurhinosaurus, and Eurhinosaurus may not defend them.
Kaiwhekea tolerate albino and melanistic individuals.

## Genetics
Skins that are considered mutations for Kaiwhekea include:
Albino, Melanistic
Players must roll in #🎲・genetics-rolls to use these skins in game. This is one roll per species, per character. Only when the character dies can you roll again if using the same character name.

## Profile Specific Unique Feature
Every Kaiwhekea has its own greeting. Players choose it freely and must remember it, as it is not written down.
Cows take on the greeting of the bull they choose in courtship for one season, or for life in a lifelong bond.
Sub-Adult and older Kaiwhekea need large fish at regular intervals: kelp sharks, manta rays, tuna and river stingrays. Without them they suffer nutritional stress. See ✦ Large Fish.
Kaiwhekea dry out on land. They return to the water once their thirst drops to 75%, and a Kaiwhekea whose thirst drops below 50% on land becomes infertile.
See ✦ Greetings and ✦ Time on Land for more information.

## Group Limits (In-Game Group)
16 Sub/Adults
Unlimited Offspring
Unlimited Adoptees

This group is known as a bale.
- There is no limit on bulls. A bale has up to 2 dominant bulls and any number of non-dominant bulls. See ✦ Bale Structure.
- Up to 4 Eurhinosaurus, usually one friend group, may join a bale in addition to the 16 Sub/Adults.
  - An orphaned Eurhinosaurus calf may travel with the bale like any Eurhinosaurus, but it is not an adoptee.
- Kaiwhekea do not group with Leedsichthys. A Leedsichthys travels with its own flotilla of Eurhinosaurus. See Cross-Species Interaction.
- Several bales may rest together at the same haul-out as a colony. They do not group beyond these limits, and each bale defends only itself. See ✦ Colonies.
- Once offspring reach Sub-Adult, the bale must remain at 16 Sub/Adults or below. When a bale splits, lifelong pairs and mothers with their daughters stay together.
- Bales may court at full size, but often split into smaller nursery groups to nest, so as not to empty the fishing grounds near the pools.

## Engagement Limits (Defence & Hunts)
Predator Reaction: Flighty / Defensive
Kaiwhekea do not hunt other players.
Up to 2 individuals may act as active defenders, while an additional 6 may form 1 protective ring around calves.
The active defenders are usually the dominant bulls. The lead cow only becomes an active defender if no dominant bull is present.
Defenders move freely, but remain within 3 body lengths of the bale. See Predator Reaction for exceptions.
If the predator is actively fleeing or has forfeited, Kaiwhekea do not chase them down.
Kaiwhekea flee from Apex and Giant carnivores on sight, unless defending a nest with hatchlings.
A Kaiwhekea seized by a Sachicasaurus is lost. The bale does not come to its aid. See Predator Reaction.
The protective ring does not accept other species or other adults, and cannot swap out individuals once formed.
**Abilities in an engagement:**
- Only the active defenders and the protective ring may use AoE Barrel Roll. It counts as an attack, not a buff.
- Only the active defenders may use Call to Arms.
- Every member may use Hustle and Rejuvenating Roar.
- The protective ring may use Rallying Horn.

## Resource Challenges
Kaiwhekea only challenge or accept challenges for resources while nesting, or when offspring below Sub-Adult are present.
If contested outside of these times, Kaiwhekea move away from the resource.
Kaiwhekea do not accept territory challenges from any species and will simply move on.
A maximum of 3 adults may defend resources such as a fishing ground or a nesting pool. These are usually the dominant bulls. The lead cow only joins if no dominant bull is present.

## Preferred Diet
Kaiwhekea live on fish, shellfish and squid. Fish are their staple, and the richest ocean fishing grounds draw them out to sea again and again.
Sub-Adult and older Kaiwhekea need large fish at regular intervals: kelp sharks, manta rays, tuna and river stingrays. See ✦ Large Fish.
Kaiwhekea fish by day. At night they rest and do not fish.
Calves catch small fish on their own from the moment they can swim. Mothers and aunts often share their catch.
Kaiwhekea swallow pebbles and shells from beaches and riverbeds, which help them grind their food and balance in the water.
Kaiwhekea do not share fish with Tropeognathus, and may drive them off. See Cross-Species Interaction.

## Nesting Limits
🌧️ Rainy Season: 4 🥚 per 4 hours.
☀️ Dry Season: 2 🥚 per 4 hours.
Kaiwhekea nest along the coastline or in shallow pools on coastal islands in their preferred POIs (Palm Islands, Castaway Isle, Volcano Islands). Their nests are built from kelp, branches and rocks.
Each bale returns to the birth beach of its lead cow. See Courtship & Parenthood for more information.
Kaiwhekea may form same-sex pairs and adopt and raise offspring together.

## Physical Dimorphism
This profile does not have high and low T individuals.
♀️ Females, known as cows, are slimmer than males and have duller colors.
Suitable subspecies include: tenui
♂️ Males, known as bulls, are bulkier, with contrasting coloration.
Suitable subspecies include: katiki, grandis
Physical dimorphism for Kaiwhekea is mandatory.
Cows take on the greetings of their bulls, so a greeting does not show sex. The subspecies does.

### ✦ Offspring Behavior (Nested)
Kaiwhekea hatchlings, known as calves, hatch fully developed and able to swim, but stay in the shallow pools where they were born until they reach Juvenile.
From birth they watch their mother leave the pool to rejoin her bale in deeper water, and wait behind in the shallows for safety.
Calves stay quiet in the pools, and only come out of hiding for their mother's greeting.
One or two aunts often stay near the pools while the mothers fish.
After reaching Juvenile, calves make the stretch into deeper water to meet their maternal bale. This run happens in full daylight, as dawn and dusk are the hours of Xiphactinus, and the night belongs to Sachicasaurus.
The bale waits just offshore, giving soft (:friendly_call:), and gathers the calves in as they arrive.
Juveniles swim in the middle of the bale, tucked against their mother's flank, where her wake carries them along with little effort. A calf that drifts out of this position calls out in alarm.
Mothers turn back at once for a calf that falls behind, and nudge it forward with soft calls.
Juveniles copy their mother's greeting, and greet every bale mate they meet with it.
They chase small fish and practise catching them, and learn the bale's fishing techniques by copying their mother.
Play-fighting, leaping, chasing games and tossing kelp or shells to each other are common between calves.
Rough play near younger calves is stopped by a watching mother: a head shake first, then a gentle nip if it is ignored.
Friendships formed in play often last into adulthood.
At Adolescent, every Kaiwhekea develops its own greeting. See ✦ Greetings.
- **Young bulls** begin to spar, swim in sync with a best friend, and shadow the dominant bulls to learn their dance. Many practise the courtship dance with their best friend.
- **Young cows** stay close to their mothers, help the aunts with younger calves, and learn the fishing grounds and haul-outs from the lead cow.
Once offspring reach Sub-Adult, they are recognized as adults within the bale, and are expected to behave as such. This includes night watch, the protective ring and active defence.
Young cows usually stay in their natal bale for life.
Young bulls often leave their natal bale, but do not have to.
- Those who leave often go together with their best friend, and may challenge the dominant bulls of another bale together. See ✦ Escort Challenge.
- Those who stay live on as non-dominant bulls.

### ✦ Orphaned Behavior (Wild Spawn)
Kaiwhekea calves can survive on their own from the moment they hatch.
Orphans without visible adults become skittish, and hide in shallow water, near the surface or among rocks, away from deep-water predators. They leave the pools later than calves with parents.
Orphans seek out other Kaiwhekea, and also follow Eurhinosaurus shoals or an adult Leedsichthys. They call constantly, hoping for an answer.
A bale readily adopts orphaned calves. An aunt is usually the first to take one in.
Orphans that never find a bale grow up quieter and more independent than most of their kind, and often struggle to read social cues. Over time, this can improve.
An orphan has no birth beach to remember. If an orphan becomes a lead cow, her bale chooses a beach for her. See ✦ Nesting.
%% banner General Behavior
Kaiwhekea are fast and agile swimmers. They swim near the surface, rising to breathe in smooth rolls, and enjoy leaping out of the water as a form of play.
They are watchful, and often raise their heads above the surface to look around, even while fishing.
Kaiwhekea bask in shallow water, on beaches and on high rocks.
They prefer clear water where they can see far, and become uneasy in murky channels and dense kelp, where predators can hide.
Kaiwhekea are vocal by day. A steady chatter of calls tells the bale that all is well. The chatter falls silent at times of stress, and at night the bale stays quiet.
Kaiwhekea are fascinated by land animals. When they meet terrestrial species near the water, they swim over to watch them, follow them along the shore, and often leap out of the water in excitement.
Some try to get their attention by fetching objects from the seafloor and presenting them as trinkets. Experienced adults do this only with herbivores, and may go ashore to greet them.

### ✦ Time on Land
Kaiwhekea are at home in the water, but like sea lions they haul out on land many times a day to rest, bask and socialise. They may stay for as long as their skin allows.
Like sea lions and penguins, which leap onto rocks and ice to escape orcas, they rest and sleep on high rocks where marine hunters cannot reach them.
**Drying out:**
- Kaiwhekea dislike drying out. Once their thirst drops to 75%, they return to the water. This takes about 10 minutes on land.
- In great danger, for example with a hunter waiting below their rock, they may stay until their thirst drops to 50%. This takes about 20 minutes on land. They never stay longer.
- A Kaiwhekea whose thirst drops below 50% on land suffers lasting skin damage. The damage is painful, and the Kaiwhekea becomes infertile for the rest of its life. It no longer courts, and this is noted in the character's bloodline log.
- Staying on land below 75% without great danger is a rule break, even if the Kaiwhekea returns before 50%.
**High rocks:**
- Kaiwhekea leap from the water onto high rocks to rest or sleep, safe from Tylosaurus, Sachicasaurus and Livyatan. Only rocks a Kaiwhekea can reach with a normal leap from the water may be used.
- A healthy Kaiwhekea may also flee onto a rock from a hunter. One with heavy deathscars, or one bleeding to death, may not (4.6).
- A rock is a place for a nap, not for the whole night. Members often take turns on a rock while the rest of the bale rests in the shallows below.
**The best places:**
- On a rock or a beach, the bale leaves the highest place and the best spot in the sun to the lead cow, without being asked.
- The dominant bulls go up last. If there is no room left, they stay in the water below.
- Calves that cannot yet reach a rock stay in the shallows below. Their mothers may stay with them, and the dominant bulls usually stay in the water and keep watch around them.
**Visiting land animals:**
- Kaiwhekea walk well on land. Like curious sea lions, they may leave the water to greet herbivores at the shore: they walk up to them, call softly with (:friendly_call:), and present their trinkets.
- Visits count towards their time on land, so they return to the water once their thirst drops to 75%.
- Carnivores are only watched from the water, never visited.
**Beaches:**
- Flat beaches are not safe. A Tylosaurus that throws itself onto the beach is faster than a Kaiwhekea for a few seconds. Kaiwhekea that rest well above the waterline are out of its reach, and once its burst fades, they outrun it on land.
- Sachicasaurus move quickly on land too.
- Crocodylians and shore hunters can reach beaches and low rocks, and are faster on land. Kaiwhekea on land keep watch for them, and escape into the water.

### ✦ Fishing
Kaiwhekea are skilled and cooperative fishers. Each bale develops its own techniques over time, and calves learn them from their mothers.
- **Taking turns:** At a gathering of fish, the bale circles the shoal and takes turns diving through it, one or two at a time, while the rest keep the fish together. Calves are allowed through first.
- **Herding:** In open water, the bale drives a shoal towards the surface or into a bay. Grouped Eurhinosaurus join in with their speed, and the two species fish side by side.
- **Kelp flushing:** At the edges of Kelp Vale, Kaiwhekea slap their flukes against the kelp to flush hidden fish into open water.
- **Strand fishing:** In the Mudflats and Coastland Swamp, a bale may drive fish against a mudbank, then lunge onto the bank after them and slide back into the water. Mudbanks are also where crocodylians wait, so the bale only strand-fishes where none have been seen.
Mothers, aunts and bonded friends share fish. Fish are never shared with Tropeognathus.

### ✦ Day and Night
Kaiwhekea are diurnal. Mornings are for travel, the middle of the day for fishing, and the afternoon for socialising and basking at a haul-out. At dusk the bale moves to its resting place.
The lead cow chooses where the bale rests: calm, shallow water near an island or near high rocks, where the bale can see the surface and the shore.
Experienced bales avoid known Sachicasaurus territories and banks where crocodylians were seen.
A bale that was attacked at a resting place does not use it again for the rest of the session.
The bale rests close together at the surface, with calves in the centre, cows around them, and the bulls and grouped Eurhinosaurus on the outer edge.
**Night watch:**
- Up to 2 individuals stay awake and slowly circle the resting bale. These are usually the dominant bulls, relieved by non-dominant bulls. Cows only keep watch if no bulls are present.
- Watch duty is passed on with a gentle nudge: the watcher swims softly into the next one, who takes over.
- Grouped Eurhinosaurus often take on part of the watch. Their loud calls give the earliest warning.
- On a threat, the watcher gives a (:danger_call:) and slaps the water with its fluke. The bale wakes, pulls the calves into the centre and leaves as one group.

### ✦ Downtime Behavior
Night is a time for rest and bonding. The bale does not migrate, fish or court at night, but Kaiwhekea are not required to sleep for the entire night.
Kaiwhekea that wish to socialise call a bale mate over with a soft (:friendly_call:). Those who answer may rest alongside, brush along each other's bodies, or cross necks.
Calves younger than Adolescent may play quietly, for example hide and seek among the sleeping adults. Calves that keep waking others are escorted back to their mothers by the watch.
Pairs and harems may rest a little apart from the main bale, but always within sight of the watch.
Loud calls at night are corrected with a head shake.

### ✦ Migration
Bales are nomadic, but follow no fixed route. They swim back and forth between fishing grounds and haul-outs, and are always on the move.
Bales roam the warm saltwater channels between the islands: the Volcano Islands, Twisted Forest, Stillwater Bog, the Mudflats and Coastland Swamp.
From there, bales regularly head out into the open oceans of Coastal Bluffs, Kelp Vale, the Abyssal Depths, Palm Islands and Stonebed Shoal. The oceans hold the fish Kaiwhekea value most, and the richest fishing grounds of the year. Their need for large fish gives the bale its rhythm, back and forth between the channels and the oceans. See ✦ Large Fish.
A bale, and even more a colony, empties the fish around it quickly. Bales move on once the fish grows scarce, and rarely stay in one place for longer than one day–night cycle.
In the Rainy Season, bales spend longer in the channels, where the rivers bring fish. In the Dry Season, the channels grow warm, and bales spend longer in the cooler oceans.
Bales do not follow the Leedsichthys route. When a bale meets a flotilla, it may travel alongside it for a while. See Cross-Species Interaction.
Bales may leave the coasts for detours into the lakes and fresh waters connected to the channels, where land animals come to drink. Spinosaurus live there, and land carnivores wait on the banks.
The best fishing grounds and haul-outs are passed down between generations, mostly by the lead cow and the elders.

### ✦ Large Fish
Small fish keep a Kaiwhekea alive, but not healthy. Sub-Adult and older Kaiwhekea need large fish at regular intervals: kelp sharks, manta rays, tuna and river stingrays.
There is no fixed time, but every Kaiwhekea feels when it has gone too long without them.
Large fish are what draws bales out of the channels and into the oceans again and again. Bales usually set out when several members begin to need them.
A large fish shared by a mother, an aunt or a bonded friend counts for the one who eats it.
Below Sub-Adult, calves do not need large fish yet, but mothers and aunts often share them.

### ✦ Nutritional Stress
Sub-Adult and older Kaiwhekea that do not eat large fish on a semi-regular basis suffer from nutritional stress.
This is a roleplay behavior, and not a built-in in-game mechanic.
They grow lean and lose their blubber. They feel the cold, and spend longer basking in the sun at a haul-out.
They tire quickly, swim slowly, dive for shorter times and fall behind the bale.
They lose interest in play, greetings and courtship. Lean cows are rarely chosen, and rarely accept a bull.
The only cure is to eat large fish again. The Kaiwhekea slowly regains its strength.
Like Steller sea lions that lived on lean fish instead of fatty fish, a bale that never leaves the channels grows thin, even with full stomachs.

Kaiwhekea are a highly social species, and live in mixed-sex bales that migrate together.

### ✦ Greetings
Every Kaiwhekea has its own greeting. It tells others who it is, and, for a cow, which bull she has chosen.
Greetings are inspired by bottlenose dolphins, whose allied males match their signature whistles to each other, and by budgerigars, whose mated pairs match their calls.
**Making a greeting:**
- A greeting holds no more than 2 emotes and 2 abilities.
- The only call allowed is (:friendly_call:), which counts as an emote.
- The only abilities allowed are Bite and Neck Swipe, aimed into open water. A bite or swipe that lands counts as an attack.
- If a greeting holds 2 emotes, one of them must be cut short by a Bite or a Neck Swipe.
- Greetings are quick, and made almost on the spot. A turn in a circle is the most movement allowed. There are no lunges or sprints, and no Barrel Roll, Fluke Slap or area abilities.
- Greetings never contain a (:shake_emote:), (:threaten_call:), (:danger_call:) or (:fear_call:). These remain warnings.
- A greeting can be as simple as a nod. Every greeting is individual.
**Remembering:**
- Players choose their greeting freely, and must remember it. It is not written down.
- A Kaiwhekea that carries another greeting for a season must still remember its own, and returns to it afterwards.
**Using it:**
- Both greet when two Kaiwhekea meet, or when bale mates meet again after time apart. When initiated, the greeting must be returned.
- Kaiwhekea recognise friends by their greeting, and remember the greetings of bale mates for life.
- Similar greetings attract each other. Kaiwhekea often feel drawn to those whose greeting resembles their own.
**Taking on a greeting:**
- A cow who chooses a bull in courtship takes on his greeting until the end of the season. Then she returns to her own.
- In a lifelong bond, the pair matches their greetings to each other, and the cow takes on the bull's greeting for life. Same-sex pairs agree on whose greeting they share.
- The lead cow always keeps her own greeting, even when she courts or bonds.
- A cow whose lifelong partner has died may keep his greeting for the rest of her life, or return to her own.
**The dominant greeting:**
- Dominant bulls share one fixed greeting, the same for the whole species. Only dominant bulls, and the cows who chose them this season, may use it.
- A bull who wins a dominant place takes on the dominant greeting. A bull who loses his place returns to his own.
- Every Kaiwhekea knows the dominant greeting, so dominant bulls are recognised in any bale. Male humpback whales all sing the same song, and in the courting season, half a bale may carry the dominant greeting in the same way.
**Mistakes:**
- Only clear mistakes count: a wrong or missing element, the wrong order, a pause of more than 3 seconds, or starting over. When in doubt, it was not a mistake.
- Outside courtship, a mistake means nothing. In courtship, it is a sign of weakness. See ✦ Courtship.
**Young Kaiwhekea:**
- Calves copy their mother's greeting at first. From Adolescent on, every Kaiwhekea has its own.
- Some keep a greeting close to a parent's, but they do not have to, and siblings sometimes share the same greeting.
- Kaiwhekea do not form large family groups, and greetings are not passed down a bloodline.

### ✦ General Communication
Kaiwhekea are expressive, and use calls and body language to communicate with each other.
A greeting is the first thing two Kaiwhekea exchange when they meet.
A member separated from the bale gives a (:broadcast_call:), and the bale answers. Once they see each other, they greet.
Contentment and affection are shown with soft (:friendly_call:) chatter, swimming side by side, brushing along each other's bodies, and crossed necks.
Discomfort and unease are shown with a head shake (:shake_emote:), looking around above the surface (:question_emote:), or swimming restlessly at the edge of the bale. These are not signs of aggression.
Alarm is a (:danger_call:) and a hard fluke slap on the water. Every Kaiwhekea that hears it raises its head and looks for the threat.

### ✦ Bale Behavior
Bale members share space peacefully and treat each other with respect, regardless of sex.
Bonded Kaiwhekea, such as the cows of one harem, pairs, close kin or tight friends, swim side by side, breach in sync and rest with their necks gently crossed.
Cows who carry the same greeting in a season often swim together.
Gift-giving is a common sign of affection, for example a shell, a bright stone or a piece of kelp.
Close friends of any sex share fish and groom each other by brushing along each other's bodies.
Bulls let cows and calves through to the fish first, and swim on the outer edge of the bale to shield them. Cows value this, and remember it when they choose a partner.
Adults play too. Leaping contests, bow-riding in the wake of a Leedsichthys and chasing games are common. Play never turns into real biting, and anyone who bites in play is corrected by the others.
Kaiwhekea also enjoy time alone, sunbathing on beaches and rocks or swimming spread out, careful not to crowd one another.
Injured Kaiwhekea swim in the middle of the bale, flanked by bonded friends. The bale slows for them, but does not stop in a POI where an Apex carnivore was seen.

### ✦ Bale Structure
Bales are loosely structured. They are guided by a lead cow and escorted by up to 2 dominant bulls, and aunts help raise the young.

### ✦ Lead Cow
The lead cow is an experienced cow, often the oldest, who knows the fishing grounds and haul-outs best. She swims at the front, and decides where the bale travels, fishes and rests.
She leads through experience, not strength, and rarely takes part in engagements. She only becomes an active defender if no dominant bull is present, and defends her own calf and nest like any mother.
She is the first to welcome newcomers into the bale, and the first to correct over-aggression.
**Keeping the peace:** If a quarrel breaks out in the bale, about anything, the lead cow swims between the two and shakes her head once (:shake_emote:).
- Both stop at once, move at least 2 body lengths apart, and do not pick the quarrel up again that day.
- Even a bull twice her size gives way to her at once.
- Ignoring her counts as over-aggression. See ✦ Correcting Over-Aggression.
- Spin fights in courtship, over free places and in escort challenges are not quarrels. They follow their own rules, and she does not stop them, unless a bull breaks those rules, for example by biting more than twice in a pass, or after a (:heart_call:).
The bale leaves her the highest place and the best spot in the sun, without being asked.
Her birth beach is the bale's nesting beach. She is the only Kaiwhekea who always keeps her own greeting, and every member of her bale knows it.
Other cows may disagree with her choice of route. A cow shows this by swimming in front of the lead cow and slapping the water with her fluke. If several cows disagree, the bale follows the choice of the majority of cows. On an even split, the lead cow decides.
The role is not fought over. When a lead cow dies, leaves or grows too old, the cow the others follow most often takes her place.
Bulls guide the bale only when no cow is willing to lead.

### ✦ Dominant Bulls
A bale has up to 2 dominant bulls. They carry the dominant greeting, escort the bale on its outer edge, and drive away rival bulls.
They do most of the bale's work. They are the usual active defenders, keep most of the night watch, defend resources and make the mock charges.
When there is no room on a rock, or the calves cannot reach it yet, they stay in the water and keep watch.
They dance first in courtship, and most cows prefer them.
The 2 dominant bulls need not be friends, but they do not fight each other over cows. A cow chooses between them, and the other accepts it.
A dominant bull who forms a lifelong bond gives up his place at the next courtship ritual, and returns to his own greeting.

### ✦ Non-Dominant Bulls & Free Places
Every other bull of the bale is non-dominant. They live in the bale like any member, take part in the watch and the defence, and dance in the second round of courtship with their own greeting.
Unbonded non-dominant bulls may challenge a dominant bull who makes a mistake in courtship. See ✦ Courtship.
A place falls free when a dominant bull dies, leaves the bale or bonds. A new bale starts with both places free.
The unbonded bulls of the bale may fight for a free place at any time by day, using the procedure for spin fights. If only one unbonded bull is present, he takes the place without a fight.

### ✦ Escort Challenge
Foreign bulls may challenge the dominant bulls of a bale for their places. One or two challengers swim to the front of the bale, give a (:threaten_call:) and slap the water with their flukes.
The dominant bulls must answer within 10 seconds, or step aside.
Each place is fought for bull against bull, one fight at a time. See ✦ Spin Fights.
Two challengers who win both fights take both places. If each side wins one, the cows decide which pair escorts the bale, by swimming alongside the pair they choose.
The winners take on the dominant greeting, and beaten challengers leave the bale.
Beaten dominant bulls return to their own greetings. They may stay as non-dominant bulls, or leave.
Cows who chose a beaten dominant bull this season decide for themselves. If they follow him, they take on his own greeting. If they stay with the place, they keep the dominant greeting.
A dominant bull may be challenged no more than once per session, whether in courtship or for the escort.

### ✦ Aunts
Cows without calves of their own often help raise the calves of others. They stay near the nesting pools while the mothers fish, and watch over juveniles during fishing.
Aunts may correct unruly calves when the mother is absent, and support new or overwhelmed mothers.
An aunt who stands guard at the pools may take part in the protective ring.

### ✦ Other Bales
When two bales meet, their members greet each other warmly, one by one, and may exchange members.
Dominant bulls of different bales recognise each other by the dominant greeting, and keep a respectful distance.
At a rich fishing ground, two bales may fish together for that day, but never group beyond the group limits.
Bales share a POI, or a haul-out as a colony, for no longer than one day–night cycle. Once one bale moves on, the other may not follow it. A bale that tries to follow is turned away with head shakes.

### ✦ Colonies
Kaiwhekea are colonial, like sea lions. Wherever bales haul out together, a colony forms.
Colonies form on beaches and rocks along the coasts of the channels and oceans, close to good fishing grounds. Any coast can hold one, and the best are known to the elders.
A colony is loud and lively. Kaiwhekea chatter, greet each other, bask side by side and cross necks. Calves play at the water's edge under the eyes of the aunts. Members slip back into the water whenever their skin needs it, and haul out again.
Members returning from fishing greet their bale mates on arrival. Like fur seal mothers that find their pup among hundreds by its voice, Kaiwhekea find their own by their greeting.
Bales in a colony greet each other, rest side by side and may fish the same grounds. They do not group beyond the group limits.
Each bale keeps its own defenders and its own protective ring, and no bale defends another.
A colony empties the fish around it quickly. Bales stay at a haul-out for no longer than one day–night cycle, then each bale goes its own way.
Colonies draw hunters. Tylosaurus and Sachicasaurus wait in the water below, and land carnivores come from the shore. Kaiwhekea rest well above the waterline, and the high rocks remain the safest place.

### ✦ Solitary Behavior
Solitary Kaiwhekea are rare. Most are young bulls between bales, or individuals shunned from a bale.
Solitary Kaiwhekea give a (:broadcast_call:) often, hoping to hear a bale answer. They only do this when they can see far around them.
Long, unwanted solitude makes them restless and wary. They swim close to the shore, and flee earlier than Kaiwhekea in a bale.

### ✦ Albino & Melanistic Behavior
Both albino and melanistic Kaiwhekea are accepted and cared for by their bales.
Albino Kaiwhekea are sensitive to bright sunlight. They bask less, rest in the shade of cliffs and kelp at midday, and find the Dry Season especially hard.
Melanistic individuals suffer no health problems, but warm up faster at the surface, and cool off in deeper water more often.
%% banner Aggressive Interaction
In order to express frustration towards another Kaiwhekea, a Kaiwhekea begins with a head shake (:shake_emote:). Greetings never contain one, so the two cannot be mistaken.
After the head shake, a Kaiwhekea may also:
- snap at the water in front of the other; it may NOT land this bite;
- slap the water beside it with a Fluke Slap; it may NOT land this hit.
These motions are only done once, and are immediately respected by the offending Kaiwhekea.
A Kaiwhekea shows recognition of these corrections by moving away at least 1 body length.
A head shake from the lead cow ends any quarrel in the bale at once. See ✦ Lead Cow.
Kaiwhekea avoid conflict with their own species, except in courtship, over free places and in escort challenges.
Ignoring is always their first response to aggression from another Kaiwhekea. Bulls that drift too close to another bull's harem quickly slip away.
Kaiwhekea do not (:threaten_call:) bale mates, except in courtship, in challenges or when correcting over-aggression.

### ✦ Correcting Over-Aggression
Over-aggression is rare and not tolerated.
1. Correction begins with a head shake and a hard slap of the flipper on the water surface.
2. If it is ignored, nearby members block the aggressor with their bodies and give (:threaten_call:).
3. If it continues, they follow up with firm nips. Other members quickly rally to help push the aggressor away.
4. If the behavior continues despite this, the bale shuns the individual.
The lead cow can end it at any step with her head shake.
Over-aggression is never tolerated or defended by bale mates regardless of relations, and any attempt to defend the aggressive individual will lead to both individuals being shunned from the bale.

### ✦ Spin Fights
Every fight between bulls, whether in courtship, over a free place or in an escort challenge, follows the same procedure.
1. The two bulls circle each other at the surface, giving 3 (:threaten_call:) between them.
2. They stop face to face, and each performs his greeting. The dominant greeting can be intimidating, and a challenger may back down here.
3. If both then give one more (:threaten_call:), the fight begins. A bull who does not give it within 10 seconds backs down.
  - If the challenger backs down, his challenge is used up.
  - If a dominant bull backs down, he loses his place.
4. They use only their Bite. Each makes no more than 2 bite attempts, then they swim past each other, turn, and meet again for 2 more.
5. This repeats until one bull drops below 50% health, or gives up with a (:heart_call:). Either counts as a loss. A fight usually takes 5 or 6 passes.
Losing a fight does not lower a bull's standing in the bale. Self-preservation is valued.
These fights rarely end in death, but leave deep scars on both bulls.
%% banner Cross-Species Interaction
Kaiwhekea are tolerant towards other species and avoid conflict at all times. When they meet a new species they approach it with curiosity, though experienced adults keep a safe distance from carnivores.

### ✦ Eurhinosaurus
Kaiwhekea welcome Eurhinosaurus as bale mates. Up to 4, usually one friend group, may group with a bale. Most Eurhinosaurus travel with a Leedsichthys instead.
Eurhinosaurus stay with a bale because they gain more from it than they give: safety in numbers, and the fish the bale drives together. Their speed and loud voices make them excellent sentries.
The two species fish together and share fishing grounds. Eurhinosaurus scouts often find the fish first, and the lead cow decides whether the bale follows their call.
When Kaiwhekea haul out or nap on high rocks, grouped Eurhinosaurus stay in the water below and keep watch.
An orphaned Eurhinosaurus calf is welcomed kindly, like any Eurhinosaurus. It is not adopted, and has no place in the protective ring.
Kaiwhekea do not defend grouped Eurhinosaurus, but call out in grief if a bonded one is lost.
Eurhinosaurus watch the courtship dance closely. It excites them and puts them in a happy mood, and they often breach along with the bulls. Kaiwhekea do not let this distract them.

### ✦ Leedsichthys & Flotillas
Kaiwhekea do not group with Leedsichthys. A Leedsichthys travels with its own flotilla of Eurhinosaurus, and follows its own route without exception.
When a bale meets a flotilla, Kaiwhekea may swim alongside it for a while. They ride the giant's bow wave, race its Eurhinosaurus and fish the shoals that gather around it.
The Leedsichthys never changes course for them, so the bale turns back once the route leads away from its own way. Like two bales, they stay together for no longer than one day–night cycle.
Neither side defends the other. Kaiwhekea may hide behind or beneath the giant, but the Leedsichthys never moves to block a predator on their behalf.
Kaiwhekea may not lead a predator to a Leedsichthys on purpose to save themselves.
A passing Leedsichthys loves the splashing and banter of a courting bale, and often stops to watch, even though it is not grouped with it. Its Eurhinosaurus breach along with the bulls.
When the bale leaves for its nesting beach, the Leedsichthys goes its own way with its Eurhinosaurus. Only if the beach lies on its route do they travel on together for a while.

### ✦ Tropeognathus
Tropeognathus follow flotillas from the air and dive in wherever fish gather, also where a bale is fishing.
Fish are the one thing a bale does not share, so Kaiwhekea may drive them off. They give a (:threaten_call:) first, then charge and snap at the Tropeognathus until it leaves the water.
This is not a hunt. Once the Tropeognathus is airborne, they let it go.

### ✦ Herbivores at the Shore
Kaiwhekea are fascinated by Lurdusaurus and other herbivores at the shore. They follow them along the shore, go ashore to greet them, and like to present them with trinkets from the seafloor. See ✦ Time on Land.

### ✦ Shore Carnivores
Austroraptor and other shore hunters are a danger to calves in the pools, and to Kaiwhekea resting on beaches.
Aunts and mothers keep watch, and calves hide deeper in the pool when one wades in.

### ✦ Xiphactinus
Single Xiphactinus are watched closely. Batteries of 3 or more are treated as an active threat.
The bale stays close together, because Xiphactinus try to cut single Kaiwhekea off from the group.

### ✦ Sarcosuchus
As a Large carnivore, Sarcosuchus are met with the protective ring rather than flight.

### ✦ Apex Carnivores
Kaiwhekea flee from adult Tylosaurus, Sachicasaurus, Livyatan, Spinosaurus and Deinosuchus on sight. See Hunted by Apex - Not Nesting.
Kaiwhekea do not flee from Leedsichthys, which do not hunt them.
%% banner Predator Reaction
Kaiwhekea are naturally alert, and highly attuned to signs of nearby predators.
At the first sign of danger, Kaiwhekea raise their heads above the surface to look around, and alert the bale with a (:danger_call:). Nearby bale mates mirror the movement.
The bale gathers, calves move into the centre, and the bale either forms a ring or flees.
If a member gives a false alarm, the others look around, then calm it with soft calls.
Small tier carnivores are ignored in small numbers, but treated as threatening when they reach numbers of 4 or more. Xiphactinus are treated as threatening from 3.
If a hunt begins, defenders do not rush out to an attacked bale member. The attacked individual must swim back to the bale for protection, within 30 seconds.

### ✦ Solo Predator Reaction
Without the protection of a bale, a Kaiwhekea becomes nervous, and may flee or become defensive when a predator is spotted.
If it opts to fight, it may move to adjust its position, but will not chase its attackers.
This does not apply to Apex and Giant carnivores, as they must still flee from them on sight.

### ✦ Duo Predator Reaction
A pair of Kaiwhekea is more confident together, but may still opt to flee when a predator is spotted.
Once both Kaiwhekea have turned to face the threat, they remain together until the hunt is over.
They drift no more than 1 body length apart to land a hit, and do not chase their attackers.
This does not apply to Apex and Giant carnivores, as they must still flee from them on sight.

### ✦ Hunted by Non Apex
Up to 2 adults, usually the dominant bulls, step forward to act as active defenders. The lead cow only steps forward if no dominant bull is present.
Active defenders cannot be swapped out once the engagement starts, and cannot be accepted into the protective ring.
An attacked individual must swim back to the bale for protection, and may receive defenders if it reaches the bale within 30 seconds.
Reaching the bale is considered within 2 body lengths of the bale.
- If it does not reach the bale in time, or turns to fight, it counts as a defender.
- Any Kaiwhekea may use Fluke Slap in self-defence while being hunted without being counted as a defender. If it turns to bite or barrel roll, it is an active defender.
Active defenders move freely and chase within 3 body lengths of the bale.
However, there are some exceptions:
- If a predator sits or rests at the surface;
- If a predator stays still for more than 15 seconds;
- or, if a predator does not come back within reach of the active defenders after three (:threaten_call:) and 10 seconds.
The active defenders may then charge the attacker. This is a "mock charge" paired with snapping and fluke slaps in an attempt to intimidate the predator.
**Protective ring:**
- A maximum of 6 adults then work together to form a protective ring around the calves. Mothers join the ring if they get there in time, but otherwise the ring is made of the nearest adults.
- Members stay side by side, facing outwards. They may leave the ring by 1 body length to land a hit, then return to it.
- An attacked bale member outside a full ring is considered part of the fleeing bale.
The rest of the bale flees the area as quickly as possible, using Hustle, and pushes stragglers ahead of it. The active defenders move to block the path for predators to pursue them.
If the predators pursue the fleeing bale, a new protective ring may form from it, forcing the original ring to disengage. Defenders who lose sight of the bale may be replaced by new defenders at the new ring.
Mothers can always land a hit if a predator attempts to attack their calf under Sub-Adult, if the calf is within a body length of her. The mother is not counted as an active defender unless she is in the ring.
If the predators forfeit, Kaiwhekea respect it and do not pursue.

### ✦ Hunted by Apex - Not Nesting
Unlike other predators, Kaiwhekea flee from Apex and Giant carnivores on sight.
While fleeing, Kaiwhekea may defend themselves, but must prioritize escaping the engagement. They may not pace themselves to tire out with another member, approach the Apex in any way, or intervene in an Apex's chase of another Kaiwhekea.
**Where to flee:**
- **Livyatan:** into the shallows. Livyatan do not follow prey into shallow water.
- **Tylosaurus:** along the surface at full speed, turning sharply. Kaiwhekea are faster than Tylosaurus, but a Tylosaurus can keep up the chase for longer. When basking or nesting on beaches, Kaiwhekea keep an eye on the water, because Tylosaurus lunge out of it.
- **Deinosuchus and Spinosaurus:** away from banks and shores, into deeper, open water.
- **Sachicasaurus:** out of the kelp and the reef, into open water as one tight group, or up onto a high rock. Sachicasaurus move quickly on land, so a flat beach is no refuge.
**Seized by a Sachicasaurus:**
- Sachicasaurus are the only hunters strong enough to seize a grown Kaiwhekea, and a seized Kaiwhekea is lost. Like a seal in the jaws of an orca, it cannot count on its bale.
- The bale does not attack the Sachicasaurus, and flees.
- The seized Kaiwhekea may fight back. If the Sachicasaurus lets go, it flees at once.
- The bale's defence lies before the grab: the night watch, resting away from kelp and reefs, and high rocks out of reach.
- A nearby Leedsichthys can offer cover to hide behind, but no protection.
Kaiwhekea do not flee from Leedsichthys, as this giant does not hunt them.

### ✦ Hunted by Apex - Nesting
The only time Kaiwhekea will stand their ground is at a nest with hatchlings, and against just 1 Apex predator.
Only 2 parents may defend one nest and engage with the Apex at a time. A bull may move between the nests of his harem in order to protect his calves.
They must stay within a body length of the nest, but may charge down predators that provoke them, for example by resting at the edge of the pool.
They must quickly return to the nest. The goal is to protect the calves and drive the predator away.
If Apex pairs approach to hunt, Kaiwhekea still flee, leaving the calves behind to hide in the shallows.
%% banner Courtship & Parenthood
Kaiwhekea form harems anew each season. A bull's harem is made up of the cows who chose him in courtship, and who carry his greeting until the season ends.
Dominant bulls usually hold the largest harems.
The harem bull holds the nesting rights with the cows of his harem for that season.
A bull may accept several cows. A bonded bull accepts only his partner.
Same-sex pairs may form. They do not nest, but may adopt.

### ✦ Courtship
Courtship is a ritual, held by day in open, clear water where the bale can watch for predators. It is inspired by elephant seals, whose cows gather where the dominant bulls hold their place.
During the courting season both sexes become extremely vocal underwater, and use long-distance calls (:broadcast_call:) to find a mate.
Infertile Kaiwhekea, such as those with lasting skin damage, do not court.
The ritual follows these steps:
1. **Cows show interest:** Cows ready to court breach the water again and again in high leaps, and give loud (:broadcast_call:).
2. **The dominant dance:** The dominant bulls dance first. They breach at regular intervals, and perform the dominant greeting and (:broadcast_call:) in turn.
3. **Challenge:** Faltering in the greeting is a clear sign of weakness.
  - Right after the dance, an unbonded bull may challenge a dominant bull who made a mistake in his greeting during the dance. Without a mistake, there is no challenge.
  - Non-dominant bulls of the bale, and unbonded foreign bulls present at the ritual, may challenge. Bonded bulls may not.
  - Each dominant bull may be challenged only once per ritual.
  - The fight follows the procedure for spin fights. See ✦ Spin Fights.
  - The winner is dominant. He keeps the dominant greeting, or takes it on if he was not dominant before. He does not need to dance again.
  - The loser returns to his own greeting, and may dance in the second round.
4. **The closing calls:** The dominant bulls give 2 (:friendly_call:), then swim a short distance away from the cows to their places, and wait.
5. **Choosing a dominant bull:** Cows who want a dominant bull swim to him and perform the dominant greeting. They carry it until the season ends.
  - Most cows prefer the dominant greeting.
  - A cow who has gone to a dominant bull cannot change her choice in this ritual.
6. **The second round:** The other bulls now perform the same dance, each with his own greeting.
  - Cows who have not yet chosen watch, and may choose a bull whose greeting they like. They swim to him and perform his greeting. Similar greetings attract each other.
  - Bulls who are not chosen do not hold it against the cow.
  - Bonded bulls dance too, but accept only their partner, who already carries their greeting.
7. **The lead cow** chooses like any cow, but always keeps her own greeting.
8. **The journey:** Once courtship is over, the lead cow leads the bale to the nesting beach. The harems of the dominant bulls lay their nests first.
Eurhinosaurus and passing Leedsichthys often watch the ritual. See Cross-Species Interaction.

### ✦ Lifelong Bonds
After 5 successful clutches together, or by mutual agreement, a bull and a cow may become a lifelong pair.
Lifelong pairs are monogamous: a bonded bull accepts only his partner.
The pair matches their greetings to each other, and the cow takes on the bull's greeting for life.
A lifelong pair always rejoins the same bale, and travels together even when the rest of the bale is not around.
Bonded bulls still dance for their partner in the second round, and bring her gifts.
Bonded bulls cannot challenge for a dominant place. A dominant bull who bonds gives up his place at the next ritual.
If another Kaiwhekea courts a bonded partner, the partner may place itself between them and shake its head once or twice. This never escalates to biting. The courted partner then decides: stay with the bond, or open it.
A bonded Kaiwhekea who does not want a suitor turns away from it. If the suitor persists, it shakes its head and slaps the water between them. After this, the suitor stops.
A cow who is abandoned or ignored by her lifelong partner may leave him, and returns to her own greeting.
After a lifelong bond ends, she does not court or nest for a while.
A widowed cow may keep her partner's greeting for the rest of her life.

### ✦ Nesting
Like sea turtles, a bale returns to nest at the beach or pool where its lead cow hatched, if it lies in one of the preferred POIs.
If the lead cow does not know her birth beach, for example as an orphan, the bale chooses one peacefully, by the majority of cows.
A nesting is successful if at least half of one clutch, rounded up, survives to Juvenile: 2 calves from 4 eggs, or 1 from 2.
- After a successful nesting, the beach becomes the lead cow's birth beach, even if she hatched elsewhere. It also becomes the birth beach of every cow present who may lead one day, if she has none of her own.
- The bale keeps its beach as long as at least one clutch there is successful.
- If no clutch at the birth beach succeeds, the lead cow gives it up. The bale chooses a new beach for the next nesting, by the majority of cows.
Like black-legged kittiwakes, Kaiwhekea stay where they succeed, and move on where they fail.
The cows of a bale nest on the same day, side by side along the same shore.
Cows place their nests at least 1 body length apart. Mothers, sisters and daughters nest close together.
A full bale often splits into smaller nursery groups to nest, so as not to empty the fishing grounds near the pools.

### ✦ Parenthood
When preparing to nest, cows head to coastal shallow pools and build simple nests.
After all young hatch, mothers return to deeper water nearby. Aunts take turns guarding the pools while the mothers fish. The bale stays close to the nesting area until the calves reach Juvenile.
Mothers come back to the pools often, and greet their calves with their greeting. Anxious new mothers who check the pool too often are gently calmed by the other cows.
At Juvenile, the calves make their run into deeper water in daylight, and the bale travels on, with the bulls on the outside.
Bulls protect their bale and their calves until the calves reach Adolescent.

### ✦ Offspring Maturity
Once offspring reach Sub-Adult, they are recognized as adults within the bale. This includes night watch, the protective ring and active defence.
Young cows usually stay in their natal bale for life. Young bulls often leave, but do not have to.
Bonded bulls stay with the bale after the calves reach Adolescent. Unbonded bulls may leave to find another bale.

### ✦ Adoption
Cows and bulls may adopt orphaned Kaiwhekea, and same-sex pairs often raise a calf together.
An adopted calf usually copies the greeting of its new mother.
Eurhinosaurus calves are not adopted, but welcomed kindly. See ✦ Eurhinosaurus.
%% banner Elderly
When Kaiwhekea reach old age, they slow down and swim at the back of the bale, with bonded friends or a lifelong partner beside them. The bale slows its pace for them.
Elders remember the oldest fishing grounds, haul-outs and resting places. Many lead cows are elders, and young cows follow them to learn the way.
Elders lose most of their wish to court. Some still nest, but often lay infertile eggs, or only one that hatches. Many take on the role of an aunt instead.
As they near the end of their lives, they become less vocal, and may shed into darker or duller coloration.

### ✦ Death
A dying elder swims to the front of the bale, nudges the lead cow and gives soft, tired calls. The lead cow lets it set the pace, and the bale follows it to a place of meaning: a favourite fishing ground, its own nesting beach, or where a bonded friend died.
There, bonded friends, offspring and partners stay close, and hold it up at the surface so it can breathe, until they can no longer do so.
Most Kaiwhekea die when they no longer have the strength to resurface. The bale lets the body sink, and its friends perform its greeting over the place for a while after.
A mother who loses a calf may push it along at the surface for some time before she lets it go.
Elders who have no bale left seek deeper, quieter waters, and live out their days alone.

Kaiwhekea form seasonal harems that can turn into monogamous lifelong bonds.
Every Kaiwhekea has its own greeting. Cows carry the greeting of their chosen bull for a season, or for life in a lifelong bond.
Kaiwhekea flee from all Apex and Giant carnivores, unless they have a nest with hatchlings. In that case, the parents, if within a body length of the nest, defend it against a single Apex, only fleeing from Apex pairs.
A Kaiwhekea seized by a Sachicasaurus is lost.
%% banner Rules & Clarifications
Kaiwhekea return to the water once their thirst drops to 75%, or 50% in great danger. A Kaiwhekea whose thirst drops below 50% on land becomes infertile, and this is noted in its bloodline log.
Kaiwhekea may only use rocks they can reach with a normal leap from the water. Kaiwhekea with heavy deathscars, or bleeding to death, may not flee onto a high rock (4.6).
In a colony, each bale defends only itself. Bales share a haul-out or a POI for no longer than one day–night cycle.

### RULE EXEMPTIONS
Kaiwhekea may group with up to 4 Eurhinosaurus, as piscivores. There is no defence between the species. (8.1)
In a mixed group, only the active defenders may use Call to Arms. (8.1.1)
Kaiwhekea do not claim bodies that have fallen in an engagement, bodies that they come across, or bodies that fall in their own defence, as they do not feed from them. (5.3)

## Preferred POIs
%% image Preferred POIs map: saltwater channels (Volcano Islands, Twisted Forest, Stillwater Bog, Mudflats, Coastland Swamp) and oceans (Coastal Bluffs, Kelp Vale, Abyssal Depths, Palm Islands, Stonebed Shoal); nesting at Palm Islands, Castaway Isle and the Volcano Islands
## Profile - Quick View
Kaiwhekea are Medium Diurnal Aquatic Piscivores.
Kaiwhekea can group in bales of up to 16 Sub/Adults, with unlimited offspring and adoptees. Up to 4 Eurhinosaurus may join, without any defence between the species. They do not group with Leedsichthys.
Kaiwhekea have a passive nature. They feed only on fish, shellfish and squid, and never hunt other players. From Sub-Adult they need large fish at regular intervals, or suffer nutritional stress.
Kaiwhekea can have up to 2 active defenders in an engagement, usually the dominant bulls. Up to 6 other adults can form a protective ring around the calves. The rest of the bale flees.
Kaiwhekea flee from all Apex and Giant carnivores on sight, except at a nest with hatchlings, which a pair defends against a single Apex. A Kaiwhekea seized by a Sachicasaurus is lost.
An experienced lead cow guides the bale. She rarely fights, but ends any quarrel with a single head shake, and the bale leaves her the best place on the rock.
Up to 2 dominant bulls escort the bale and do most of its work: defence, the night watch, and staying in the water when there is no room above. Aunts help raise the calves.
Every Kaiwhekea has its own greeting, which players must remember. Physical dimorphism is mandatory, because greetings do not show sex.
Like sea lions, Kaiwhekea haul out on land many times a day, often in colonies of several bales, and nap on high rocks. They return to the water at 75% thirst, or 50% in great danger. Below 50%, they become infertile.
Courtship is a ritual: cows breach and call, the dominant bulls dance first and may be challenged after a mistake in their greeting, and cows choose a bull by taking on his greeting.
Bales nest at the birth beach of their lead cow, and change it after a failed nesting.
Kaiwhekea roam by day, back and forth between fishing grounds and haul-outs in the saltwater channels and the oceans. They may swim alongside a passing flotilla for a while, and a Leedsichthys often stops to watch their courtship.
Kaiwhekea are fascinated by land animals. They follow them along the shore, leap in excitement, and go ashore to greet herbivores.
