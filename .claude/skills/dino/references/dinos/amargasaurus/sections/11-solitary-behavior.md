# Amargasaurus – Solitary Behavior

When traveling alone, Amargasaurus instinctively seek out dense woodlands,  similar to the environments where they hatched. The close-packed trees and thick undergrowth provide excellent camouflage, helping them avoid detection by predators. On occasion, solitary Amargasaurus may attract the company of other herbivore species. While these are not their typical herd mates, they enjoy the presence of these strangers until they reunite with a sauropod group. Amargasaurus do not show aggression toward their own kind unless protecting offspring. Those caring for young do not tolerate another moving too quickly around their fragile young and may rear their head and whip their tails to demand space. Uncharacteristic behaviors in an individual, however, can result in shunning. This is often marked by repeated tail slaps, and backing away in visible displeasure. When aggressed by another herbivore species, Amargasaurus always moves away to avoid conflict. ✦ Ampelosaurus, Apatosaurus, Argentinosaurus, Camptosaurus, Citipati, Struthiomimus Amargasaurus are generally sociable toward their own kind and other herbivores. When approached by a welcoming herbivore, both males and females greet with gentle neck shakes and friendly calls. They are especially receptive to Sauropods and Citipati, often inviting them to stay nearby. As a smaller sauropod species, Amargasaurus frequently follow the migratory patterns of larger sauropods for safety and resources. However, Amargasaurus with adoptive young can be far less tolerant of other herbivores. Protective caretakers quickly use their tails to whip or rear their heads to signal displeasure. This behavior stems from the fragility of their young, who are at risk of being trampled. Until their offspring grow sturdy enough to safely navigate within the herd, caretakers keep other creatures at a safe distance. When faced by Allosaurus or Torvosaurus specifically, Amargasaurus immediately issue fear calls - raising their heads while walking or running from the known danger. All Amargasaurus flee from these species. They may defend themselves as they flee, but must focus on escape. If another predator approaches, Amargasaurus only respond if they know the predator is a threat. This varies based on individual experiences and some might not respond with fear until attacked. Once the alarm is sounded for a threat, Amargasaurus mimic the cries of their herdmates and coordinate. 2 Amargasaurus emerge as the primary defenders, while the others try to get out of the way of the engagement, desperate to avoid accidental friendly fire from their herdmates. Those on the outskirts of the group rear their heads in displeasure and whip their tails if the predator approaches within smacking distance. The herd either stands their ground or flees. The behavior shifts depending on what hunts them, or how fearful their herdmates are. The 2 defenders differentiate between pacing anxiously and standing their ground. They smack their tails and stomp their feet, each performing the role of aggressor and defender while the predator is close by. Amargasaurus do not chase fleeing predators, but may charge forward to try and intimidate the predator into backing away. The intent here is to create distance. Sometimes this is accompanied by a smack of the head, but more often than not is employed to get predators to back off. When an individual is targeted and severely wounded, the sound of pain and sight of vicious predation causes Amargasaurus to panic. The wounded might be left in a stampede or shoved behind defending bodies. Young are often trampled in these situations and sometimes the herd is split between caretakers luring their babies away from danger and those choosing to stand their ground and fight.
