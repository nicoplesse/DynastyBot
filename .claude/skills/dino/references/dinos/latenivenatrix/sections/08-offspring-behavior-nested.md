# Latenivenatrix – Offspring Behavior (Nested)

Latenivenatrix offspring, known as chicks, hatch vulnerable and utterly reliant on their parents. They tumble out of their eggs, wet and sticky, and cry out in light squeaking noises to draw their parents to them. They hatch with feathers and basic abilities to thermoregulate, but instinctively huddle close to their siblings and brooding parent for better warmth retention. Chicks remain huddled in the nest for the first few stages of their lives, waiting for comfort and food from their parents. They pay little attention to their siblings at first, focusing entirely on eating enough to grow their frail bodies. By the end of the hatchling phase, chicks grow sturdier and more aware of their surroundings - and begin to take a sharp interest in the world around them! Parents gently encourage this curiosity, and guide their freshly Juvenile chicks  to the outer borders of the nest. Rookeries frequently lay their eggs in clusters of adjoining nests, and parents permit their young to traipse between neighbouring nests. Once chicks are mobile in this way, parenting becomes communal. All members of the rookery contribute to help rear and protect young - though chicks still show a clear preference for seeking comfort from their own parents. Clutches are merged together into a bustling creche - much to the delight of chicks, who seem to revel in the noise, movement, and excitement of having additional playmates and caregivers. Within the creche, chicks begin to socialize and develop essential communication skills. They mimic the calls of each other and nearby adults, and even - on occasion - attempt to copy sounds made by other dinosaurs. At Juvenile, chicks begin latching - often on each other, adult Latenivenatrix, and bonded Achillobator companions. Chicks seem to enjoy holding on to a caregiver, and can frequently be seen being carried by adults. Playfighting becomes a favorite pastime, with chicks tugging at each other's feathers and exchanging gentle nips and swipes. These playful scuffles double as valuable hunting practice, and help to hone their reflexes and agility. Playfighting is never taken seriously - the moment a chick bites too hard or becomes too boisterous, their playmate squawks and retreats in protest. Serious injury is rare, as overly rambunctious individuals are swiftly corrected by adults watching over the creche. When rookeries have a sufficient number of adults present, one is always stationed as a dedicated babysitter to supervise the creche. This is a necessary precaution, as chicks hatched into large groups are endlessly curious and prone to wandering out of the territory. However, chicks raised in smaller rookeries tend to be more cautious and reserved. They seem to pick up on the stress of their parents, and remain quieter, and less inclined to stray far from the safety of the territory. As they mature, chicks are granted greater freedom to roam - though still under the supervision of adults. The newfound sense of independence fills chicks with boundless excitement - often displayed through racing and chasing sessions, with chicks calling out in bright chirps and yips. Hunting is a skill learned rapidly, and appears to be seemingly instinctual for this highly intelligent species. Chicks observe first and act after - often witnessing multiple hunts or how different species behave before jumping in for the kill. Critters are frequently caught and killed by chicks, who receive practically aggressive praise from the adults in the group, which reinforces the behavior. At adolescence, chicks are relatively self-sufficient. More than capable of hunting small prey to sustain themselves, they are also able to navigate their surroundings carefully - and learn to hide themselves effectively from known danger. Despite this growing independence, the rookery continues to dote on them - offering food, grooming and vocal reassurance well into adulthood. Adolescents remain deeply affectionate towards their elders - often bringing trinkets such as bones, flowers, or scraps of meat to them as tokens of affection. These exchanges strengthen the social bonds of the rookery, helping maintain order among the group. As they approach Sub-Adult, their restlessness grows. Rookeries with room for offspring to remain welcome them fully into the rookery, and they begin participating in group hunts and defence of the group. When there's no space, they leave to find a new rookery, but maintain close bonds and recognize one another if they meet again. Whether they stay or leave, all Latenivenatrix retain the deep social instincts forged in their communal upbringing. Even after dispersing, adults may return to visit - or even reintegrate - into their natal rookery.
