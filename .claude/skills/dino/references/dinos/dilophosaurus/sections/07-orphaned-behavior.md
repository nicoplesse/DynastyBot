# Dilophosaurus – Orphaned Behavior

Dilophosaurus pups who hatch alone hatch cold and lonely, weakly calling out for a mother or for siblings. Without the warmth of another Dilophosaurus, fragile hatchling pups risk dying to the cold - especially if born into the rain, without the coverage of a den or tree to hide them. As a result, they tend to develop slower, especially in growing strong enough to stand. These pups will grow slowly and struggle with regulating their body temperature in their adulthood, as well as with maintaining a healthy weight. Dilophosaurus pups do not begin to grow their teeth in until the Juvenile stage - which means that they struggle with eating. An orphaned pup will desperately attempt to use their claws to shred meat into chunks they can swallow. Orphaned Dilophosaurus pups are only adopted by mask groupings if a same sex pair is present. With leashes, however, they risk being cannibalized by the dominant male. Orphaned Dilophosaurus pups are never accepted into a leash unless they create their own. With the support of other Dilophosaurus around them, they quickly develop the social skills needed to thrive as a member of a group and gain proficiency in their hunting skills. Dilophosaurus are a highly social and coy species. Through their hunting strategies and daily behavior, they show a good deal of intelligence. Dilophosaurus are highly coordinated with other members in their familial groupings, and willingly share kills with one another. Dilophosaurus like to scavenge or make easy meals out of the resources that they can find - robbing other species of their kills or eating bones or carrion, and hunting critters. All hunts shared amongst Dilophosaurus are done with constant chirping and calling, both as a means to confuse their targets and communicate to their group mates. Excess food is buried or stored in their nests for later consumption, or to be eaten by offspring. Dilophosaurus tend to be lazier during the day, preferring to find rocks or clearings to lay and sunbathe on. Entire leashes of Dilophosaurus can be found sleeping alongside one another or in piles on a warm rock, absorbing as much warmth as possible. OPTIONAL: Dilophosaurus have chromatophores in their skin cells that adjust to better align with their current environment. Dilophosaurus may flare into brighter colors at the courtship grounds, drawing more attention to themselves when it comes time to court. Other than exclusively using these colorshifts for better camouflage, Dilophosaurus are ectothermic, meaning that they additionally change their colors to regulate their body temperatures. In the rainy season, they may shift a darker hue to absorb warmth, and in the dry season, they may shift to a lighter hue to reflect it. Dilophosaurus have preferred biomes - two or three POIs that they travel through regularly. These POIs tend to be all clumped together. A Dilophosaurus will only ever leave these areas if exiled or going to the courtship grounds. Dilophosaurus like to have places within their preferred biomes where they take part in specific activities; for example, they may have a specific place (or places) where they go to hunt, drink, bathe, sunbathe, nest, and socialize within their groupings. While a leash may not stay together in one clump, the communal areas are passed down from clutch to clutch, and those within a leash easily find one another by passing through these common areas. The largest of Dilophosaurus groupings, known as leashes, are led by a breeding group of bonded individuals. The breeding group consists of the dominant male and 4 subordinates - either males or unrelated females. Dominant males are extremely possessive of their females and bonded subordinate males, and will swiftly aggress or even hunt solitary rivals if they approach his leash. He readily chases off competition to ensure that his leash will not be sneakily courted behind his back. Outside of the breeding group, other Dilophosaurus are offspring to the breeding group. Dilophosaurus are masters of deception, and sneaky nesting is extremely common within Leash groupings. The acceptance of these illegitimate offspring depend solely on if the mother manages to court with the dominant male as well as a rival mate. This is further detailed under Courtship and Parenthood. Masks, small groups of all same-sex individuals, can take many forms and are a bit rarer than leashes. They tend to be sibling groups, same-sex pairs or groupings, or unrelated individuals banding together to make the trip to the courtship grounds. Some individuals in masks may attempt to sneakily court individuals in existing leashes, as detailed in Courtship and Nesting. Solitary Dilophosaurus Solitary Dilophosaurus are dispersals, exiles from their family groupings, or orphans. Some Dilophosaurus actually prefer a solitary life, and keep to themselves, migrating throughout some favored POIs in Gondwa in search of individuals to sneakily court. Solitary Dilophosaurus males willingly court any female that indicates interest in him. He chooses to abandon the area shortly after to avoid detection by the dominant breeding group. Dilophosaurus have other options of finding a social group, and travel to their courtship grounds to properly establish a breeding group, find a same sex pairing, or to temporarily group with other individuals of the same sex. Albino & Melanistic Individuals Albino Dilophosaurus are prone to sunburn due to their lack of pigment, and as a result are much more fragile. Without camouflage patterning, they are a liability in hunting groups and are chosen last for hunting patrols. Melanistic Dilophosaurus have limited chromatophores and their skin changes are barely noticeable as they move between biomes. Melanistic Dilophosaurus overheat much more easily, as they are unable to utilize their chromatophores to regulate their temperature. During peak times of day, Melanistic Dilophosaurus hide within shade while the rest of their group basks in the warm sun.
