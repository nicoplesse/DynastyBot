# Dilophosaurus – Downtime Behavior

Dominant males lead their groups in when it comes time to sleep; otherwise, it most commonly occurs at the hottest time of day in the Dry Season (noon) and the coldest time of day in the Rainy Season (midnight), or immediately after a successful hunt. Vixens build decorative nests close together and lay on them to rest, socialize, and sleep. These nests are not protected, unless offspring are present. Reynards in the group often rest on the outskirts, lifting their head up at any sign of movement or noise. If offspring are present, they play in the center of these nesting structures, tussling with one another, sleeping in piles, and bickering over toys. Dominant reynards use downtime as a form of maintaining healthy bonds with their breeding groups; if the group does not rest enough, and during this period the male does not dote on his females, they may opt to leave the group. Dominant reynards may posture, present scavenged bones or AI corpses, hunt critters, or bring other suitable gifts in order to maintain relations with his females. Dilophosaurus are social creatures, with each grouping having their own pattern of calls to communicate. Calls vary to signify prey nearby, affection, seeking attention or to alert imminent danger approaching. Sometimes these call patterns are taught through generations, and other times they are observed from other groups. Dilophosaurus are adaptable and learn social cues quickly. Breeding groups, known as a leash, and consisting of a mixture of males and females, form very strong bonds with one another. This is similarly seen in same-sex pairs within masks. Other relationships within masks - where individuals often do not share family ties and have friendships -  can become strongly bonded after spending time basking or successfully downing prey in hunts.
