# Sarcosuchus – Social Interaction

All Seasons Members of a float - regardless of gender composition - are highly protective of one another, knowing that the presence of a Deinosuchus - or worse, a Spinosaurus - could spell the end of their dynasty. Sarcosuchus do not have a strict dominance structure, but instinctively learn each others' behaviors and move as one unit. When traveling, they follow whichever individual initiates movement, shifting their course as needed to stay together. However - when migrating through water, floats adopt a more structured formation. They swim in a triangular pattern with one leading from the front, and two just behind them, positioned left and right of the leader. New members to a float are met with cautious observation. They begin circling the newcomer, swimming slowly and deliberately, while scanning for signs of illness, infection or parasites. Two solitary Sarcosuchus will enact this behavior with each other, with both individuals moving around the other in a circular motion. When a solitary individual is met by a pair, the newcomer remains motionless as the already grouped Sarcosuchus inspects them. Grumbles and low rumbles signal hunger within the group. When one member vocalizes, others join in, acknowledging the need to hunt or fish. Afterwards, the float may begin a coordinated search for prey. Same sex floats tend to fragment over time. All male or all female groups may begin to wander, seeking members of the opposite sex to form a new float. These groups become restless, traveling further in search of potential mates. Rainy Season With water sources abundant and fish stocks plentiful, Sarcosuchus may engage in food sharing behaviors with their groupmates. Individuals will occasionally offer fish to their companions, helping to reinforce social bonds within the float. When basking, they rub against one another and against large boulders, helping to exfoliate their tough scales. This helps maintain skin health, while also serving as a social interaction. Floats become more tolerant of other floats in their vicinity. The plentiful resources reduce conflict, and Sarcosuchus allow other floats to linger nearby - only acting aggressively if provoked. Gentle nuzzling and body contact are signs of companionship, and exhibited across all genders. While basking on land, Sarcosuchus press their snouts against each other in quiet displays of familiarity and trust. Dry Season As water reserves shrink, tempers flare. Sarcosuchus become irritable, frequently snapping at any float members that come too close to them in this period. Minor disputes break out over space, with a light snapping of jaws to display their annoyance, but never escalate beyond a bite or two, with the aggressed party quickly backing off. Mud pools become contested resources. Individuals claim a specific pool of mud, and prevent others from using them - including their fellow groupmates. Should another float - or species entirely - approach, they may be met with aggression. Should the intruder persist, the dispute escalates into a physical confrontation. Regardless of species or grouping, Sarcosuchus always abandon this if they drop below half health, bitterly giving up their prized mud pool. At this time, other floats are not tolerated in line of sight in the water. If a rival float enters their vicinity, Sarcosuchus will either opt to move on themselves, or push them out of the POI entirely. This is the only time Sarcosuchus become territorial. Solitary Sarcosuchus always back down from a float with 2 or more members. Sarcosuchus in a float of just 2 members may consider adding a solitary individual to their group, depending on gender and preferences.
