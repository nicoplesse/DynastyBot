# Sarcosuchus – Offspring Behavior

Hatchling Sarcosuchus emerge from their eggs with hazy, blurred vision and are essentially blind. The vulnerable youngsters are entirely dependent on their float for protection from predators. They remain hidden in the tangled roots of their nest as much as possible. Only leaving to follow their parents into the shallow water nearby, guided by the low rumbles and grunts of their caregivers. Survival during the hatchling stage is precarious. Hatchlings are timid and strive to remain unnoticed, slipping beneath debris or clustering together when startled or alerted to danger. Their best chance lies in stillness and silence. Their world is based around sound and touch, and they navigate by the vibrations in the water, and gentle nudges from the members of their float. Hatchlings remain close together, often resting their head on their sibling’s back for reassurance. It is not until they reach Juvenile that their eyesight develops and their vision clears, opening up the potential for exploration of the world around them.  With new confidence, they venture out curiously through vegetation and submerged logs, hoping to blend against the foliage to reduce detection from predators. When in water, these curious juveniles prefer to sink right to the bottom of the riverbed to better remain hidden. Juvenile Sarcosuchus continue to be dependent on their parents, and return to the nest with loud chirps to beg for food, which is usually brought to them promptly. While still wary, they begin to assert themselves among their siblings, forming loose bonds with those closest in size (and temperament), while showing hints of aggression towards smaller and weaker nestmates. If a youngster drifts too far during their quest for exploration, an adult may seize them in their jaws and carry them back to the safety of the nest. By adolescence, their confidence grows even further, and with it comes the more independence. They begin hunting small prey such as fish or critters in earnest, and hone their hunting skills for future opportunities on more dangerous prey. At this stage, the adolescent Sarcosuchus develop more of a pecking order among sibling groups. Larger and more dominant siblings may bully their more passive siblings away from food, or steal their prey. However - against adults, even adolescent Sarcosuchus remain submissive, careful not to push their luck too far. When the adults begin leaving the water at night to explore on land, adolescent Sarcosuchus keenly follow them, but are careful to remain far enough behind that they do not disturb any potential hunts. Though they are not permitted to join the adults in hunting, they watch carefully from hidden bushes. It is common for adolescents to mimic the adults in their float - watching hunting behavior curiously, and improving their stalking and ambushing abilities. They lay motionless in wait, hidden in foliage, before charging towards unsuspecting frogs, reptiles and small mammal species. Interactions with adults are kept cautious and respectful. They call out with soft, appeasing chirps and move slowly. Once they reach sub-adult, offspring are chased out of the float by the adults in the group - most typically the female, who progressively shows less tolerance to her offspring as they grow. Siblings disperse in same sex groups of up to 3, or leave alone. Though considered adults when they reach sub-adult, Sarcosuchus do not fully mature until Adult, and cannot court or nest until then.
