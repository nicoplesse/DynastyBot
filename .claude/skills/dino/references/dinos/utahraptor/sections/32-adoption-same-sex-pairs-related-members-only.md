# Utahraptor – Adoption - Same sex pairs & related members only

Same-sex pairs can be accepted into a family pack, depending on the skins and individual chosen as a potential mate. Just as opposite-sex pairs do, same-sex pairs prove their dedication to each other each season. They participate in nest building together and reaffirming their bond. While they cannot reproduce offspring together, same-sex pairs are trusted packmates, and particularly broody pairs will linger near the nesting site to help raise offspring in the family. Sometimes when a clutch is overwhelming for parents - especially when there's more than 2 eggs - new parents will nuzzle one of their smaller chicks over to the nest of a same-sex pair they trust; willingly allowing a single child to be raised by them instead. Shaking their feathers passively, the broody same-sex pair happily accept the chick given to them, and devote their attention and time to raising the chick as their own.  The chick will mature with knowledge of its biological parents, but develops a closer bond towards its caregivers who spent more time and energy raising it than its parents did. When raising the chick given to them, or participating in the seasonal nesting rituals, same-sex pairs take turns bringing meat chunks for expectant parents in the pack and standing guard over the nesting site. It's often these dedicated packmates are the first to ward away rivals in the territory, fiercely protecting the pack’s newest generation. When the related partner dies, the unrelated mate is forced to leave. While the initial jab is painful and mourned deeply, oftentimes a sibling to their deceased mate may take the opportunity to find them at the edge of the territory, hoping to catch their interest and form a lifelong bond with a known provider. This is the perfect situation for a widow who was a trusted ally to the bloodline. (Offspring raised by other packmates must still be listed under their biological parents in the bloodline post. A note can be made that they were raised by a same-sex pair/or other, but ultimately, their rank is decided at birth. When pack ranks change, so too will their role - regardless of who raised them, they are still biological offspring to their parents.) Utahraptor form unbreakable bonds with their chosen partner, a connection tested from the very start. Once two raptors commit, whether within an established family pack or forging their own path, they remain steadfast. Even as the day turns to night, they stay close, reinforcing their choice through quiet companionship. When hunger calls, they leave the warmth of their nest not for themselves, but to ensure their partner is fed, solidifying their loyalty. Offspring may come or not - it makes no difference. The bond, once forged, lasts a lifetime. Relationships may face rocky times, with one partner being left without the other for days and weeks on end. But the reunion between the pair is a beautiful sight to witness. Eager to reestablish themselves into the pack, and show devotion to their mate, bonded Utahraptor will act out the courtship rituals with their mate as soon as the pair is reunited. A separated Utahraptor does not seek another. Instead, they wait at their nest or track the last known scent of their mate, relentless in their search. Even when hope dwindles, they refuse to believe the bond is truly severed. Only death can break this devotion. When a mate is lost - whether through tragedy in battle or the character deleted - the survivor grieves in ways both subtle and profound. Their nest, once a place of shared warmth, crumbles into neglect. Their energy shifts, pouring into the well-being of the pack's young rather than their own future. This death must be logged in the bloodline. Elder individuals play a crucial role in training the younger Betas and Omegas, sharing decades of experience on how to organize coordinated hunts and navigate territorial conflicts. While they are less physically able to participate in the strain of daily pack life, elders observe from a safe distance, teaching skills to younger members on small, weak critters. During downtime with the pack, they mentor juveniles, leading them around the territory borders.
