# Utahraptor – Aggression towards family members:

During their juvenile phases of life, Utahraptor chicks playfight to practice hunting skills on each other and adults. Once reaching adolescence, their behavior is regulated by higher ranking packmates. An aggressive call or a nip on the back forces these rambunctious chicks to submit. As they mature, Utahraptor playfights become more risky. What begins as harmless sparring can quickly escalate into real injuries if boundaries aren't respected. A misplaced bite or poorly judged swipe can turn a friendly scuffle into a bleeding wound. While playfighting remains common among adults as essential hunting practice, it is never permitted to drop a packmate below half health. If a bout begins to go too far, the attacker is expected to immediately disengage, retreating with a visible display of submission, such as shaking out their feathers, lowering their head, and stepping aside. In return, the wounded raptor will instinctively distance themselves from their aggressor, a silent acknowledgment that the line was crossed. Social rank plays a key role in how these interactions unfold: Alphas sometimes engage in playfights, but lower ranking packmates never initiate these spars against them but happily take part. If an Alpha observes an individual engaging in playfights that goes beyond half health more than once, they will begin to exile this packmate for over-aggression. Other ranks may playfight among each other, but lower ranks are expected to submit first when sparring with higher ranks. Even within the fiercest pack, Utahraptor packs are deeply rooted in mutual respect and the preservation of group cohesion.
