# Camptosaurus – Offspring Behavior (Nested)

Baby Camptosaurus, known as joeys, hatch out of their eggs blind, deaf and extremely fragile. They remain asleep in the nest for all of their hatchling stage, silent and barely moving - and most importantly, void of parental figures. Joeys at this stage are incapable of standing; their muscles and bones having yet to fully develop. Any attempt to rush this process will prove fatal to a joey's survival and cause immediate rejection from all other Camptosaurus. This additional period of rest allows for their bodies to finish growing - and by Juvenile, joeys are alert and aware, reacting to movement and sound. It’s at this time that mothers return to the nest to greet their young, urging them to their feet quickly and nipping if she deems their progress too slow. Having been immobile for so long, joeys often find this process difficult, but not painful. They stumble and fall many times in short progression, trailing after their mother on unsteady feet, reacting enthusiastically to praise and affection. Joeys follow their mothers closely, mimicking her movements and calls. They spend the majority of their youth travelling between their mother’s home range and the colony’s community grounds. They interact with objects with great interest, performing a series of tasks to investigate the taste, smell and feel of anything within reach. Items they find favorable become carried around for short periods of time, or brought back to hoard in their mother’s home range. In addition, joeys become incredibly fond of their newfound ability to run and hop. They participate in large games of chase, racing around an area for extended periods until exhausted, and forced to rest. Joeys are oblivious to the social norms expected from partaking in colony life, and are incredibly friendly and playful with others. They encroach on home ranges and personal space of neighboring Camptosaurus, mistaking their aggression displays as invitations to play. These misunderstandings usually lead to brutal - but much-needed - lessons in manners. All Camptosaurus in a colony may partake in correcting a joey's behaviour, through shoving or light nips. Dominant males are known to overstep and react more harshly, intent on displaying their dominance even to young. These frequent attacks, while alarming to watch, help joeys understand expectations from an early age. Joeys attacked by older Camptosaurus will instantly yield, calling out in distress and returning to their mother’s side. Mothers rarely intervene in these situations, but will defend her offspring from another Camptosaurus if she feels their life is in danger. Upon reaching Adolescence, female and male joeys begin demonstrating strong differences in social behavior and expressing a need for independence. Female joeys begin wandering the outskirts of her mother’s home range, searching for an area free of claim. A mother will encourage this behavior, leading her daughter to various locations deemed suitable for inhabitation, and showing her how to maintain a territory. It is not uncommon for a female joey to disperse at this age, especially if she had a peaceful upbringing with little threat. Some mothers may attempt to persuade her daughter to neighbor with her, willing to compromise some of her territory in the process. Daughters typically accept this proposal, eager to remain in close proximity. Male joeys are quick to venture far away from their mother, and begin investigating residents with far more curiosity, demonstrated by their now purposeful encroaching and challenging of home ranges. Their ultimate goal in this is to understand the social hierarchy of residing males, determining which individuals fall under the subordinate and dominant status by their reaction. Subordinate males are quick to submit as to maintain calm, forfeiting and pacing just outside the borders of their home range. Male joeys accept this easily, and may take a moment to investigate the territory before leaving to interrogate the next male, allowing the subordinate male to reclaim their home. Dominant males, however, react with vicious aggression at the first show of intent, performing a series of challenging displays and calls to ward the young joey away. If their threats are not heeded and the joey enters their home range, the dominant male will rush forward and attack the joey, treating them as an intruder. Joeys instantly retreat when they are attacked, calling out repeatedly in forfeit and rushing away from the danger, having learnt to steer clear of the male until older. Any territories won during this phase are void, as males do not claim territory at adolescence. Male joeys who demonstrate more submissive or meeker personalities are treated similarly to female joeys, with the mother guiding her joey to various areas suitable for claiming. However, a mother will never give up part of her territory for male offspring, and shows less preference for neighboring sons, aware that their constant bickering for hierarchy may attract unwanted attention. Instead, she may attempt to group him with other males, far from her own territory. At Sub-Adult, mothers force their young to disperse, eager to return to her solitary nature. All joeys generally separate easily, having already made peace with the concept in their adolescence, and grown accustomed to their roles within a colony. Female joeys make their final decision for a home range, and begin preparing the area for long-term inhabitation, with a Camptosaurus’ preference for what they deem suitable differing between individuals - though often most impacted by what their mother’s home range looked like. For their first few days of independence, a mother and her daughter communicate in short calls from their home ranges, reassurance that eases out as the daughter grows more adjusted to solitary life. More anxious daughters may attempt to return to their mother’s home range, often bringing gifts or food as persuasion tactics, but are promptly chased out and regarded as intruders. Male joeys leave in a similar fashion, but return to either the most desirable home range already owned by a male, or a home range owned by the most submissive male within the colony. From there, they will challenge for the territory, and if unsuccessful in obtaining it, will move onto the next best option. Only male joeys who have already demonstrated being more submissive in their youth will partake in claiming a new home range instead of taking a current male’s over.
