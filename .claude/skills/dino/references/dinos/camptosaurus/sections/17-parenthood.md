# Camptosaurus – Parenthood

Once all her eggs have hatched, the mother will leave her home range completely, spending her days and nights at the community ground instead, where she may bond with other nesting females going through the same process. This action is done to ensure predators do not track her scent to her vulnerable young. Some mothers may still risk returning at brief intervals to ensure their young are alive and to ensure the surrounding area is still secure. Mothers return to her offspring once they reach Juvenile, a stage in which they have gained the ability to see, hear and walk. She works quickly in encouraging her young to their feet, often through shoving and impatient nipping. Mothers are incredibly anxious about their surroundings during this process, frequently pausing to survey the environment before returning to the task at hand. Joeys that take too long to stand and walk are at risk of being abandoned, seen as sickly. Joeys are awarded with immediate affection and praise upon getting on their feet, and once they’ve mastered their first steps, mothers will lead her offspring to the community grounds. There, they will gain access to food and water, and be introduced to other Camptosaurus. Mothers aren’t particularly involved in a joey's social development at this stage. She allows for her offspring to wander off and explore, to learn and experience situations for themselves, calling them back to her side only when they wander out of sight. She rarely intervenes in conflicts between her joey and other Camptosaurus, and only steps in if she feels others are being too harsh. If her offspring are hunted, mothers will not defend them and flee the area instead, valuing her own safety and expecting her offspring to do the same. As joeys age into adolescence, mothers become more involved with their daughters, and less so with their sons. Daughters are prioritized, with their increased independence, mothers hurry in showcasing various locations suited for her daughter to claim her own home range in. In addition, mothers show her daughters how to properly maintain a home range. These techniques and expectations vary between families, but lessons in clearing and trampling overgrown vegetation, gathering materials suited for nests, and scent-marking the territory are common. If space allows it, mothers often encourage daughters to claim a home range next to theirs, willing to give up part of her territory in the process. Over the seasons, a female Camptosaurus may accumulate a mass of closely related neighbours, daughters from previous clutches. Though solitary, kinship is recognized, and mothers in particular often remember the faces of their offspring even years after separation. Male adolescent joeys are still cared for, and tolerated in their mother’s home range, but receive far less attention overall. Their growing interest in the male hierarchy brings unnecessary noise and conflict, aspects that the female Camptosaurus will prefer to avoid. On occasion, a male joey may display subordinate behaviour from an early age, lacking an interest in challenging and provoking other males. In these instances, a mother will begrudgingly treat him with the same expectations as a daughter, showcasing home ranges and lessons in maintaining a territory. However, mothers never give up their territory for a son, and typically try to encourage him to neighbour other males, further away from her own home range.
