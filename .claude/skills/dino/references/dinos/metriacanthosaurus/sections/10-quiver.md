# Metriacanthosaurus – Quiver

A quiver consists of either a pair of siblings, or a mated pair and their offspring. Outside of this, Metriacanthosaurus do not tolerate others of their own species and will cannibalize younger individuals, or begin a territory challenge. Between a mated pair, they are seen closest together when they have offspring, finding it easier to defend their polliwogs if they are attacked. Otherwise, a mated pair without offspring may remain together out of the convenience of having a partner. They distance themselves from each other, traveling together loosely and often sleeping in their own spots. Low chattering can be heard between the two on occasion, though these pairs hardly look as though they acknowledge the other’s presence until they find it necessary to hunt together. Only then does their dynamic swiftly alter, moving together and becoming highly aware of each other's position. If one Metriacanthosaurus in mated pair is attacked, the other rushes to defend the other, preferring to remain with their companion. Sibling pairs remain highly aloof of each other while traveling, keeping to themselves unless they spot potential prey. Oftentimes, sibling pairs exhibit a higher success rate in taking down prey, having watched their parents and understanding the roles each other can play. Metriacanthosaurus siblings often still feel the need to compete with each other even in adulthood. When attacked, some siblings defend the other quickly, though others may hang back, taking a moment to decide if it is worth the risk. At any time, these groupings may dissolve. Failure to raise clutches or repeatedly failed hunts are common factors that cause these pairs to break apart. Siblings may separate to seek out a mate, traveling together to the courting grounds before ungrouping once they arrive.
