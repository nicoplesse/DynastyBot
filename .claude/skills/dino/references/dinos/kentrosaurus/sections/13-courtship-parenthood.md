# Kentrosaurus – Courtship & Parenthood

Courting is initiated entirely by females, who enter a period of broodiness, and signify this by collecting a round object. This round object - such as a nut, pine cone, mushroom etc - is carried around in her mouth, and protected fiercely by the female, who becomes very aggressive if another member of the bristle tries to take it from her. Males circle the herd during rest stops to look out for signs of these broody females. If lucky enough to find one, he will begin circling her, affectionate calling and shaking his spines to gauge her interest. If she is not interested in him, the female will call aggressively at him - sometimes even escalating to spitting at him if he is overly persistent, and not listening to her cues. If she is interested in him, she will instead call affectionately to him, and from there be lead over to a bush by the male, where she will place a nest to rear her young. Female Kentrosaurus usually lay 4 eggs, no matter the season. Once she has placed a nest beside the bush, she conceives two eggs by that male. If no other males approach her after 10 minutes, the other two eggs can then be sired by the first male - or the female may opt to only lay 2. However, males can and often do court multiple females at a time, so when the first female has placed a nest, that male will rush over to court a second female, constantly looking back to make sure no other males approach his female on the nest. Time is of the essence, and males may seem more rushed when courting subsequent females so that he can get back to drive out other males. He will always lead more females back to the same bush, so that they can place their nests next to each other - making it easier for him to guard them from rival males. Males that spot a female on a nest - unguarded - might opt to try and court her sneakily to sire the other half of her eggs. He will do so by circling her while she rests upon the nest, courting her in much the same way as the first male did in the beginning. Females are very open to these prospects if the other male impresses them enough, keen to have genetic diversity for their offspring. These males can attempt to court females in this way that are not even part of their own bristle. If she accepts, she will call affectionately back, and the male will quickly sire the final two eggs before leaving quickly - not wishing to be caught by the original male. If she rejects him, or her entire clutch is already laid, she will aggressive call towards him and spit in his direction. Males always take the hint in this instance, and move on. The original male may sometimes "catch" this sneaky male and rush in to fight him off before he can sire the clutch. He will not give any warning on his return - instead immediately racing over to spit at the male, before turning and swinging his tail, smacking his spines fiercely against the rival male's body. The other male can opt to either forfeit and flee immediately, or fight back for his right to sire the other half of the eggs. The loser is the first one to reach deathscars - and is forced to forfeit. If the original male is the loser, he must step aside and permit the courtship begrudgingly. Knowing that half the eggs are still his, he will continue to care for the female and his offspring, unable to determine which offspring are biologically his and which are not. The other male, meanwhile, doesn't offer much parental input - leaving this up to the original male. If the other male is from another bristle, they might never encounter each other again. If the original male is the winner, the sneaky male is chased out, and the rest of the eggs in the female's nest are fathered by the original male. Once eggs hatch, the original male spends time with all females he has successfully courted and aids them in rearing offspring. Though babies instinctively prefer to stick to their mothers, fathers still offer valuable input, especially as they grow. At Sub-Adult, newly mature offspring are driven out of the bristle by the adults in the group unless there is room for them. However, if there are no future breeding prospects for them (and they wish to nest in future), they may opt to leave of their own accord. Both parents willingly allow Miragaia to gather some - or all, if they are overwhelmed - of their offspring if part of a stegosaurid herd with them. This can be temporary or permanent - with those having low parental drive happy to offload them, and others content with just having temporary breaks from their energetic offspring. Females will happily accept adoptees into their brood. In contrast, males do not adopt - but will tolerate them to remain in the bristle.
