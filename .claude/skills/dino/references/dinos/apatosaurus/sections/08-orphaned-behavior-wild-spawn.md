# Apatosaurus – Orphaned Behavior (Wild Spawn)

Freshly hatched orphans begin life much like parented calves by rushing towards cover and relying on concealment, feeding, and rapid growth for survival. In early life, little distinguishes them from calves with parents. They may still form nursery groups with other Apatosaurus calves, and avoid the open ground until older. It is at adolescent that a difference emerges - while calves with parents rush to be greeted by them, orphaned calves often linger at the edge of open ground, calling for parents that never respond, before eventually accepting their solitude. Some fortunate orphans born near an active herd may be adopted at this stage, with prospective parents stepping forward to guide and accept them. Those that are not, however, are forced to either migrate alone, or seek companionship among other sauropods and compatible groupables. Even when solitary, orphaned Apatosaurus instinctively seek out herds wherever they roam, and rarely abandon the hope of finding and joining a pod. Apatosaurus are bold yet calm creatures, and are rarely hurried unless provoked. Even while migrating, they move with seeming patience, drifting between scatterings of berry bushes and refusing to rush without reason. Despite their huge size, they are highly aware of their surroundings and difficult to surprise. They listen carefully for distant calls, watch for subtle movement and feel the vibrations through the ground to maintain awareness. They are creatures of habit and routine, with pods preferring familiar paths and showing disdain at any disruption to their regular routines. Apatosaurus are fond of mud wallows and take every opportunity to indulge whenever they encounter a suitable patch. They settle down and begin rolling and rubbing the mud against their skin, keen to coat it as a form of protection against biting insects, and also cool it from the baking heat. Mud wallowing can be a major determinant of where herds and pods migrate. Matriarchs remember especially favourable places for wallowing and make sure to revisit these places in future. It also serves as a social activity for pods, with groups of Apatosaurus moving to settle and coat themselves in mud together. These times can be especially exciting for adolescents, who keenly copy the adults in the group. Apatosaurus can become somewhat "fidgety" - both while migrating and at grazing or rest stops. They may swish and flick their tails in a whip-like motion, give loose shakes of their heads and necks and strip barks from nearby trees with their teeth. Apatosaurus continue to pick up items with their mouths well into adulthood, though these are usually confined to branches and rocks. Rocks are chewed on, with smaller pieces swallowed to aid in grinding the tough vegetation they consume. Branches serve as a form of toy for Apatosaurus, who seem to take delight in tossing them across the terrain. Though generally tolerant, Apatosaurus become cantankerous when irritated by drought, overcrowding, or persistent scavengers. They rumble out low growls, barge past others and may even charge at those they deem to be the source of their irritation.
