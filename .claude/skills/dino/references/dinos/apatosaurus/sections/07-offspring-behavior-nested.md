# Apatosaurus – Offspring Behavior (Nested)

Apatosaurus young, known as calves, hatch in the warm, ashy substrate of Volcano Island & Burned Forest. Their parents remain nearby in the area, and come forward to briefly nuzzle their newly hatched offspring, before ushering them towards the foliage. Due to their tiny size, calves are too fragile to migrate alongside adults. Instead, they spend the earliest part of their lives clustered together within dense foliage, relying on staying hidden for survival. Calves are mobile shortly after hatching, and instinctively move towards areas of thick plant cover, where trees and ferns provide shelter from both predators and the elements. Apatosaurus young form loose nursery groups, remaining close to one another while the adults in the herd occupy the open ground nearby. Smaller, alert sentry species often take the opportunity to pass through these nursery areas, serving as ideal alarm systems for calves. Citipati and Struthiomimus are especially cherished, thanks to their gentle and affectionate natures. Calves feed almost constantly to support their rapid growth. This growth is an essential defence against predation, and offspring must grow quickly in order to rejoin and migrate alongside the rest of the herd. If predators attack, calves do not run towards adults for protection. Instead, they freeze, and rush between different cover levels to hide. If hiding is no longer an option, calves may rush to defend each other - up to a maximum of 4 in the engagement. Calves are highly social with one another, and maintain close proximity where possible, preferring to graze in pairs or more for security. Play consists of light chasing games through the undergrowth, with both sexes taking it in turns to act as both the chaser and the chasee. Calves are notorious for lifting things with their mouths. Items such as branches, rocks and miscellaneous foliage are collected and held by young that nibble on them idle-mindedly, before the object is discarded in favour of something new. At Juvenile, particularly dominant calves - usually bulls - begin practising mock charges at one another. This type of play is usually mutual - though rougher calves may occasionally overstep and barrel into another, or frighten a playmate away. Calves remain highly attuned to the nearby calls and sounds of any sentry species they have with them. The alarm call of a Struthiomimus or Citipati is enough to scatter calves into hiding, who then only re-emerge when the danger has passed. At Adolescent, calves begin broadcasting for their parents in loud - yet somewhat shrill - bellows. They move towards the open ground to be warmly greeted by both mother and father, and immediately nuzzle against them, ready to join the herd and begin migrations. Once reintegrated with the herd, calves stick closely to both parents - with cows preferring to mirror their mothers, and bulls following behind their fathers. However, this behavior is not wholly gender exclusive - some cows bond particularly strongly with their fathers, and some bulls remain forever close to their mothers. By Sub-Adult, calves are considered mature enough to no longer receive the protection of their parents, but do not begin courting until they reach full Adult. They rarely - if ever - disperse from the herd, and remain in their familial pod until they find partners. Cows remain in their familial pod indefinitely, while bulls transfer into their future mates' pod.
