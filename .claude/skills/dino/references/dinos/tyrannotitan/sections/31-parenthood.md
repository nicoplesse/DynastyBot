# Tyrannotitan – Parenthood

Upon hearing the sound of shells breaking open, a mother will stick her large nose into the nest, calling to the new whelp softly. With gentle care, she identifies the new scent of her offspring, a bond immediately forming. Sows are extremely maternal while their offspring are small. Any threats near the nest are immediately warded off and aggressed. During their early life, boars remain at a distance, unwilling to face the temper of the protective sows. Some younger, more curious fathers may attempt to steal glances at the offspring at a distance between the trees and foliage, but never wander too close, not wishing to provoke the ire of the mother. Once a mother notices her offspring able to steady themselves, strips of meat are offered to the whelps. Often this is an attempt to curb their need to bite and adjust to the frequent irritation in their mouths as new, larger teeth quickly begin to push the others out. Sows often make small trips from the nest with her offspring in tow, roaming small areas in the boars territory near the nesting site to expend the boundless, newfound energy of her growing whelps. Mothers often expect her whelps to engage each other in sparring matches and encourage the rough, and oftentimes bloody, play. Some sows, especially younger and inexperienced will interrupt the play fighting as it starts - overwhelmed by the chaos. More often, sows will interrupt this play if their offspring are overly wounded, to which she expects all biting to end until the wounded young are healed enough to continue. Whelps who continually push the limits of play may even be aggressed by their own mother. By the time her whelps are adolescent, her overprotective nature begins to fade as they begin to take more interest in a bonded sibling or their surroundings. It is around this time that a sow will introduce her offspring to the father. The boar, finally called over by the sow, will greet his offspring, looking over the whelps and taking particular interest in any sons he may have. If he is unpaired, he may select a submissive son, chattering and bowing to him. Should the whelp take interest at this stage, the boar will take the whelp with him on his patrols and become his main protector as he grows. Some sows may even encourage a son to move on with their fathers instead, nudging him forward with encouraging calls. An adolescent male who is selected will group with their father at this age. Fathers may be granted temporary tolerance around the whelps at this age, though his interactions are monitored and limited by the sows who eventually push him away for periods at a time. While sows begin to expect independence at this age from her whelps, she will still interrupt any sparring that appears to be taken too far. Otherwise, she will ignore them, simply wishing to be out of range of any stray nipping. A sow without a pair may take particular interest in a female whelp, paying extra attention to her daughter which often remains closest to her.
