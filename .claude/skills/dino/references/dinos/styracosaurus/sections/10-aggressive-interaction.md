# Styracosaurus – Aggressive Interaction

Styracosaurus are very noisy when becoming aggressive. Growling and headshaking behaviors are common. Sparring matches between stags: Stags frequently engage in sparring matches with one another in a group - both those that have harems, and those that are bachelors. Sparring matches seem to be a consensual and almost enjoyable behavior for stags - beginning with aggressive growling and head shaking to signify their desire to begin a match. Once the opponent accepts, they use their hard heads and spiked horns to butt against their opponent, alternating between blocking and landing blows. These battles continue until one individual reaches half health - signifying them as the loser of the match. These fights never go further - except if two stags are fighting over a particular unclaimed doe. Should both stags desire the same doe, the fight is extended until one opponent reaches deathscars. Stags never fight for does that are already in a harem, and respect each other's boundaries. The loser of the sparring match must walk with his harem (if he has one) behind the winner the next time the herd migrates - a signal of their submission, and a position that changes next time they pause at a rest stop and begin sparring again. Stags do not begrudge this, and fall into line willingly, knowing that next time the roles could be reversed. The dominant stag is never challenged by another stag, but may opt to spar playfully with other stags. If the dominant stag loses these playful spars, he does not lose his position. Emerging as the dominant stag: The dominant stag position can change each play session, and is not set in stone. When a new herd forms, the stag with the largest amount of does in his harem naturally becomes the dominant stag, and all other stags (and does) within the herd are happy to follow his leadership. This stag leads all others in the herd from the front, with other stags and their harems falling in line behind them during migrations. Should there be a situation where a herd forms, and two or more stags have the same number of does (including no does, in an all bachelor group), this position is decided by a sparring match. Only stags with the same number of does in their harem participate, and the winner of all these sparring matches ends up as the dominant stag. Dominant stag challenges: Other stags do not challenge the dominant stag for his position. His position remains all whilst he has the highest number of does in his herd, and is respected by other stags. Stags do not begin sparring matches with the dominant stag, but will accept if the dominant bull begins the match. The dominant stag losing a sparring match in this manner does not remove him from his position. However, if a subordinate stag ends up with more or equal the number of does to the dominant stag, the position can change - effectively making does the "king makers" of the herd group. In this instance, the dominant stag must rush to try and claim another doe to ensure he has the highest number of does. If he cannot do so within 15 minutes, he must battle for his position: Both stags will engage in a sparring match, signified by loud broadcasting by the current dominant bull directly towards the rival stag. The entirety of the herd gather around, forming a circle around the two opponents to define out a battle ground. Stags fight down to deathscars, with the winner becoming (or keeping) the dominant stag position. If the new dominant stag has less or equal number of cows to his bested opponent, he must take does from this other stag to ensure he keeps the highest number of does in his harem. If they are of equal number of does, he may take 1 doe. If the other stag has more does, he may take whatever number will take the other stag to have 1 less doe than the dominant stag. Preference is given to the does themselves - in these takeover situations, the new dominant stag always opts to allow the does to decide amongst them which of them will join the dominant stag. Does always accept these changeover situations, and at least one doe will volunteer herself - usually together with a close friend or relative if she has one with her in her current harem. Should these does have fawns, the fawns join the dominant stag in the new harem. Afterwards, the herd resumes as normal. Doe aggression: Aggression between does is rare within an established harem - though can be common place when a new doe is integrating into an existing harem, with does using shunning behavior until the dominant doe decides to stop. However, does may opt to drive away does from other harems from food resources when they have fawns, ensuring that their young get to eat first. This is displayed by using their bodies to block the other does from the food resource, and aggressively shaking their heads. Other does that do not back down - especially those that also have fawns - may opt to begin a short fight, ending only when one of them reaches half health or less. The dominant doe from each harem is usually the one to intervene and move other does on, preferring to avoid these quick fights where possible.
