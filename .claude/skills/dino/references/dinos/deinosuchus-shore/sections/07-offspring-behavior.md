# Deinosuchus (Shore) – Offspring Behavior

When Deinosuchus hatch they have an innate instinct to swim, but are too weak to immediately enter the water, and stay within the confines of their nest for the first few moments of their life. Their mother guards both them and the nest closely, returning affectionate calls to her chirping young. During these early hours, Deinosuchus hatchlings communicate constantly through soft peeps and chirps. Not only reassuring for their mother, these sounds help draw siblings closer together as they wriggle in the nest to huddle in tightly for warmth. Young that stray too far to the edge of the nest may get nudged back in gently by their mother's snout - or even carried back into it, should they tumble out of it. Midway through the hatchling stage - where offspring have gathered enough strength - their mother slips back into the water and calls for her offspring to follow her. Hatchlings struggle onto their feet immediately and make a dash after their mother into the water, where they swim quickly behind her to keep up, letting out a series of high pitched chirps to both her and their siblings. These vocalizations help them determine where the rest of their family is at all times, and enables their mother to quickly track down offspring that lag too far behind. They remain following after their mother for the entirety of this time, only stopping to eat when she provides a meal for them. As they get to juvenile, Deinosuchus offspring begin to dart after fish and other fast moving creatures beneath the surface. Though their attempts may be clumsy at first, they soon refine their abilities and begin feasting themselves on whatever they can catch in the water. This is sometimes even other hatchling creatures if they are quick enough about it. Deinosuchus will not share these kills with their siblings or their mother, but they needn't worry - mothers do not intervene in any sibling quarrels, and it is rare for siblings to bully another for their food. Each individual develops their own techniques and hunting methods, branching out from the group slowly as their confidence grows. Fathers have little to no input on their offspring's lives. They do not bring their offspring any food and nor do they defend them - their contribution is only that they allow them to grow within the confines of their territory without being chased out. Thus, babies pay little attention to their fathers - except not to bother them or get too close. As they age, and begin to stray further from their mother, they learn not to linger too near their father, lest he decide they are a rival that must be driven out of the territory. Once adolescence approaches, Deinosuchus develop a stronger independence from their mother and siblings - though some same sex individuals grow close bonds with a particular sibling. They begin hunting more substantial prey that ventures close enough to the water, and often test their strength against one another in short tussles over basking spots or food. These tussles are never serious, and never take an individual below half health. Their mother stops providing food for them at this point, and no longer tolerates them sharing her meals - forcing them all to learn how to hunt and catch fish fully, if they haven't already refined their abilities. Adolescents begin to become more exploratory, venturing into deeper and faster moving waters, and straying further from their mother. Their vocalizations become deeper, and less frequent, used only in close contact with familiar individuals. By Sub-Adult, Deinosuchus offspring are expelled by both parents. Their mother becomes increasingly aggressive towards them - snapping or charging if they stay too close, while their father drives them out entirely. Mothers may leave the territory and resume her sedentary lifestyle should she not wish to nest again with the same male, but will not tolerate offspring following her. There is a rare exception, however, of a solitary daughter, should she be alone and wish to have a traveling companion. Fresh Sub-Adults disperse either in same sex pairs or entirely alone, and fan out in different directions, each choosing different water sources to scope out as potential territory or hunting grounds. Though dispersing from their parents at Sub-Adult, Deinosuchus are not fully physically mature until Adult, and cannot court or nest until then.
