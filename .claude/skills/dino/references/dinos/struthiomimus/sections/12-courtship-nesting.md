# Struthiomimus – Courtship & Nesting

During the breeding season, male Struthiomimus become notably more aggressive toward other males and even small creatures. They also grow more vocal and display their feathers more prominently. When the flock comes to a stop, the males seize the opportunity to claim a small area for their mating ritual. These areas are fiercely defended from other males. In this space, the cobs will collect rocks and various objects they find appealing, using them to build a decorative scene. Rival males may attempt to steal these decorations in an effort to sabotage each other and improve their own displays. Once their area is prepared, the males will begin calling out, attempting to catch the attention of the hens. This display not only highlights their vocal abilities, such as sounding alerts, but also showcases their vibrant, contrasting feathers through elaborate posturing. If a female is interested, she will approach and respond with her own vocalizations. If the male reciprocates her interest, they will interlock necks in a bonding display. After a female has accepted the male and joined his harem, she is allowed to rest within his claimed temporary territory, along with the other females in the group. The male will not permit other females to rest in his area unless they have officially joined his harem. Once a female has joined, the male will return to his display, attempting to attract more hens if space in his harem allows. When it’s time for nesting, the male Struthiomimus takes the lead by building a communal nest where all the females of his harem will lay their eggs. Once the eggs are laid, it is the male who takes on the responsibility of incubating them and caring for the chicks after they hatch. Once the chicks are born and their feathers have dried, they instinctively follow their father everywhere. The father is meticulous in his care, ensuring the chicks are well-fed and sheltered. It’s common to see Struthiomimus chicks huddled beneath their father, using him as shelter, whether it's raining or the sun is too intense. As the chicks grow into juveniles, the father begins encouraging them to forage for their own food, gradually teaching them independence. By the time they reach adolescence, the father’s involvement significantly reduces, as he starts to ignore them unless they need protection. From this point, the adolescent Struthiomimus must begin to care for themselves, relying less on their father as they approach full independence. Males may adopt orphaned chicks at any time, treating them as if they are their own.
