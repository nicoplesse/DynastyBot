# Lambeosaurus – Hadrosaurs & Iguanodon

Parasaurolophus are often some of a Lambeosaurus herd’s closest allies; while they will not defend one another, they will travel together and orphaned Lambeosaurus may even be adopted by them. Lambeosaurus enjoy singing alongside Parasaurolophus and will often carefully watch over young Parasaurolophus (even if only from a distance). Iguanodon are a fierce addition to a Lambeosaurus herd. They are much more loud, boisterous, and fiercely defensive than a Lambeosaurus, and therefore are valued as potential distractions or better targets to predators. Barsboldia are large hadrosaur cousins that may at times follow Lambeosaurus herds when solitary. They are tolerated in their own herds or when following the herd, but some mothers may become nervous when the much larger species get too close to their nesting sites or offspring. This never turns to full blown aggression, and typically results in mothers leading their offspring away. Up to three Lambeosaurus may rush to the defense of the herd against Large tiers and below. These defenders rush forward with fierce kicks and sweeping tails to deter the predators from the rest of the fleeing herd. If there is 5 or less adult members in the herd, and the herd is confronted by 3+ Large tiers, Lambeosaurus will flee. Lambeosaurus in mixed species herds will do this only if there are less than 5 adult members of Medium tier or higher. Regardless of herd size, Lambeosaurus will always flee from all Apex carnivores. They do so even when in a mixed species herd. A Lambeosaurus may flee from any engagement at any time. They will move closely together with their herd, attempting to use synchronous motion and movement to confuse predators as well as protect more fragile or injured members in the herd. Offspring, elderly, and injured are typically ferried to the center of the fleeing herd - but others may panic and attempt to shove their way into the middle, resulting in further injuries and greater risk. A Lambeosaurus will use water to its advantage by crossing in order to deter predators. They swim nearly as fast in water as they do bounding over land, meaning that it puts larger gaps between them and their hunters. Lambeosaurus tend to show great fear when faced with Daspletosaurus, their natural predators. Daspletosaurus develop strategies passed down through generations in hunting Lambeosaurus herds, herding herds in order to corner them and hunt them. Even defenders panic with pressure from Daspletosaurus. If a member in the fleeing portion of the herd is attacked, they are able to defend themselves and not be counted towards an active defender. They may not chase the predator, nor may they stop fleeing when given the opportunity. When nesting and with hatchling fawns, parents may freeze at the sight of a predator, especially if they are near her nests. Parents will attempt to call the predator’s attention to themselves and lead them away from their offspring, towards the defenders or fleeing herd, and will only return to her nest and offspring when the predators are gone. Fawns instinctively go quiet and still, hiding in the foliage around their hidden nests. They will only begin to bleat and call if attacked, or if no one returns for them.
