# Lambeosaurus – Offspring Behavior (Nested)

Hatchling Lambeosaurus, called fawns, emerge from their eggs slowly, before quickly laying down into the dense leaves and shrubbery surrounding their mother’s nest. They often hatch alone, with their parents maintaining a distance of at least a few body lengths throughout the early moments of their fawns’ lives. At this time, fawns are too weak to run and much too fragile and slow to keep up in the case of a stampede. Fawns remain quiet and still instinctively, only calling for their parents in short, panicked barks and shrieks if injured or if one of their clutchmates is hunted. Towards the end of their hatchling stage, fawns slowly stand on wobbly legs. Their parents, watching carefully, keep their distance but snort quick breaths through their noise to lure their offspring close. These chuffing calls help the baby fawn recognize their parents, especially when entire herds are nesting. The walk taken from the nest to their parents is a slow process, but an essential one– forcing fawns to develop the strength they need to migrate with their herd for the rest of their lives. Juvenile fawns stay as close as they physically can to their parents, which often means accidentally getting in their way when they are going for food or water. Experienced parents keep this in mind and move more slowly than younger parents, who rush to ensure all the needs of their fawns are met. Juvenile fawns begin to show interest in their clutchmates, as well as other fawns in the herd, and spend long periods of time quietly gazing at one another in interest. Siblings, especially those of the same sex, begin to form bonds that will last them entire lifetimes. Mimicking the behaviors of their parents, they issue soft calls and carefully groom one another. Juvenile fawns are also taught to swim by their parents along the banks of rivers, with the parents standing or swimming steadily in the depths of the river to model the behavior and ensure that there are no predators. Fawns take to swimming easily and excitedly, and enjoy splashing their parents and siblings. When fleeing from predators, parents rush their fawns to the nearest place for them to hide - whether it be a den, deep foliage, or via crossing a water source. Fawns know to go completely still and quiet in dens and foliage, and to swim closely behind and beside their parents when at odds with a threat. At adolescence, fawns begin to exhibit some gender dimorphism, with males often spending a great deal of time walking around on their hind legs and watching over the herd, and females staying low, taking care of other smaller creatures they find. Adolescent Lambeosaurus begin to play with one another and other adults with their herd in many ways. The most popular route is shoving; with fawns pushing other fawns, their parents, and even bonded adults in the herd into water ways, shrubbery, and more. This can be much to the annoyance of their parents, who quickly do mock kicks in their directions to deter the fawns. While kicks are rarely intended to land, those who receive an accidental blow from their parents quickly correct their unruly behavior, shaking apologetically. Fawns also do mock kicks towards one another, but blows being landed quickly result in loud frightened cries, and fawns scuttling back to the security of their mother - or any female in the herd- to have the other fawn corrected. Fawns enjoy climbing on top of rocks and cliffsides, much to the dismay of their parents. From there they posture and call loudly before being shooed off the cliffside and back to the herd by their mother. One misstep can result in tragedy. As fawns reach sub-adult, they begin to calm down, now more aware of the social expectations of the herd. However, young adults still happily initiate play with other young adults in the herd, and even do so as a way to flirt. Sub-adult fawns only disperse if there is a lack of resources, or if courting opportunities lead them elsewhere. Otherwise, they may spend their entire lives with their natal herd.
