# Albertaceratops – Courtship & Parenthood

Albertaceratops sows begin courtship by traveling to nesting grounds, where they work together to create a protected area for their future offspring. Females place nests close together, forming a circular "cove," surrounded by thick bushes and grass. Even females who do not breed contribute to the cove, helping to support their herdmates and being taught by the dominant sow how to construct the herd's specific cove requirements. The center of the cove is reserved for the dominant sow, who decorates her nest with trinkets as a visible symbol of her rank. Unlike the other nests, hers is exposed, signifying her status as the central figure. While the sows work on the cove, boars patrol the exterior, warding off rival species who come too close. The dominant sow is the only one permitted to nest with the dominant boar, and these two only nest with each other. For other sows, only winning boars are considered fit and strong enough to sire the next generation. These boars engage in courtship fights, with the dominant boar overseeing the battles and stepping in if a fight becomes too intense. Dominance fights Dominance fights occur at any time to establish rank or prove vitality in siring the next generation. A confident boar will boldly approach a rival, broadcasting his challenge. If the rival ignores his display, he may be met with a sharp smack and an aggressive bellow. Boars that choose to submit will perform a body shake, diffusing the tension and establishing their lower rank beneath the victorious dominant. However, if the rival refuses to back down, he will respond with a roaring challenge and stomp the ground loudly, signaling the start of the confrontation. The two boars begin circling one another, bellowing loudly and stomping the ground. During this display, the dominant boar oversees the event from a safe distance near the sows. Once both rivals are prepared, they face off, issuing a challenge broadcast. Following the aggression call, they charge at each other, colliding in a powerful clash. This sequence of circling, displays, and charges continues until one boar reaches death scars or the dominant bull intervenes to end the fight. Courtship fights are not without risk. In smaller groups, challenges can result in the death of younger males with no self-preservation. The dominant boar, experienced and wise, carefully manages his aggression. He intervenes when a boar shows visible injuries (bleeding death scars), signaling that the fight has escalated too far. In such cases, the victor is determined by which boar has the most remaining health.
