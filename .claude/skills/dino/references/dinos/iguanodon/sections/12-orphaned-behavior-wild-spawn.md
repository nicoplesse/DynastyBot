# Iguanodon – Orphaned Behavior (Wild Spawn)

Orphans struggle without a mother figure in their lives and spend the majority of their youth searching for a replacement, and the comfort of a herd. If lucky enough to happen upon a herd of Iguanodon - or even a solitary individual - most orphans can find refuge with them. Though bulls will not act as a father to the orphan, they will happily welcome them into their group and offer them protection. Cows, meanwhile, might show a more affectionate attitude, which the orphan gratefully accepts. Orphans approach the herd, calling out affectionately. Some orphans will gravitate towards a particular individual they find comforting - but ultimately it is the choice of the adults that will take an orphan under their wing and decide to adopt it. Failing to find a herd of Iguanodon does not necessarily mean a lack of a mother figure for an orphan. Iguanodon will seek out herds of Lambeosaurus or Parasaurolophus for adoption - though this is always a second choice. Both hadrosaurs readily welcome orphaned Iguanodon into their herds and this can prove beneficial for the calf - especially when awarded attention and nurturing care, which can be provided by both species. Though they are not the ideal substitute for an adoptive Iguanodon mother, the orphan will remain with the herd for as long as possible - only breaking away to join other Iguanodon once they reach adulthood, or if they encounter others of their species early on, having not yet established strong bonds with their other adoptive species. The longer an Iguanodon calf remains with these species, the more traits and habits they tend to pick up. This can even extend to Iguanodon orphans attempting to copy courting behaviors exhibited by the other species once they reach sub-adult, and taking longer to integrate fully when finally meeting others of their own kind. In some extreme cases, some orphans may spend the rest of their lives preferring the company of their adopted species. Barsboldia and Camptosaurus can also provide comfort to a young orphan, but the more nocturnal stirrings of Barsboldia and the smaller, speedier build of Camptosaurus can make it very difficult for an Iguanodon calf to relate to them. They may stay by these potential "parents" for some time, but are quick to leave them in favor of better opportunities - such as Lambeosaurus, Parasaurolophus, or their fellow Iguanodon. As a nomadic, migratory species, Iguanodon are perfectly adapted to travel great distances in search of food and water resources. From as early as hatchling, young Iguanodon are equipped for the pressure of hard migrations. Iguanodon are a fully terrestrial species, but enjoy spending time in water - especially during the harsh sun in the dry season. They can be seen swimming and rolling in lakes and ponds, and especially relish coastal regions and splashing in the sea. This behavior extends even to certain groups of Iguanodon that sometimes migrate down to Red Island. Though they are not the best swimmers, Iguanodon are fully able to swim that distance, and experienced herds may choose to do so in order to hatch and raise young in the seclusion of the island. Iguanodon are particularly fond of mud baths, and take great pleasure in rolling and laying in mud to coat their skins. In the rainy season this is especially important to serve as protection against the biting insects that plague Iguanodon in the wetter months. Iguanodon shake their heads as a means of reassurance, and also as a sign of affection. When interacting with something new - and perhaps unsettling - they often take to shaking their heads after the experience, perhaps to release any stress in their bodies. When resting, bulls take turns on surveillance duty, inspecting the area around the sleeping herd, and keeping a watchful eye for any predators that might sneak up under the cloak of darkness. In busy environments, the bull stands on his hind legs and walks around the herd, swapping with another bull mid way through the night so he can get some rest. Calmer environments will see bulls walking slowly in a large circle around the herd, or if all is quiet, he will simple sit near the edge of the herd and keep guard. When migrating, Iguanodon are fairly loud, often heard before they are seen. It only takes one member to broadcast loudly before the rest of the herd follows suit. Iguanodon rise to a bipedal stance whenever something alarms them. This is a defensive gesture, but also serves to help the Iguanodon see over a greater distance. However, Iguanodon are not easily shaken. Small carnivores in low numbers are generally ignored, not representing much of a threat to these much larger herbivores.
