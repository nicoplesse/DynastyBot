# Iguanodon – Courtship

Iguanodon courtship is initiated by bulls, who excitedly flush with testosterone at a rest stop in between migrations. This rush of testosterone prompts bulls to act in a more brutish manner with each other - shoving against one another, body blocking and bellowing. This does not cause any damage or injury, and all bulls seem to enjoy the almost playful roughhousing. If cows are receptive to potential advances, they approach a bull but turn away at the last minute. Sometimes approaching slowly and carefully, before turning heel and sprinting a short distance away. The cows do this to tease the bulls, pretending to be intrigued by him, but then moving away to feign disinterest. This is employed as a form of flirting, encouraging bulls to try harder to win their approval. Should a cow not be interested in courtship, she may ignore the bulls completely and stay closer to the edge of the herd. Younger cows who are not interested will sometimes intervene with roughhousing bulls, scolding them with aggressive calls and harsh staredowns if they are being approached by them. Immediately feeling frustration at a sudden lack of attention from cows, bulls then push further to try and impress them - leading the hopeful bulls to rush ahead of allegedly unimpressed cows, and broadcasting to show their willingness to engage in bullfights. Bulls wishing to court will react to these broadcasting calls with excitement, approaching the bull calling and issuing their own broadcasting challenge call to indicate their desire to bullfight. Once a bull makes these intentions known, the rest of the herd will begin to gather around, excited to witness a potential bullfight. Cows settle down in loose groupings, keeping their eyes on the broadcasting bull and any potential challengers that may approach. The two bulls raise onto their hind legs and begin walking slowly, growling at each other. Stabbing at the air is common in bulls that try their best to intimidate their opponent into forfeiting before a fight breaks out. They do not break eye contact with each other, and do not turn their backs. Younger or more submissive bulls may take this opportunity to back down, losing their bravado when faced with the reality of a fierce fight. Forfeiting is as simple as lowering themselves back to all fours, and breaking eye-contact, walking away from the scene. If it is clear neither bull will back down, they finally move forward and stab at each other viciously with their thumb spikes. This is a brutal and short battle, where both bulls alternate between fierce stabbing and blocking to defend themselves from incoming attacks - but every bullfight ends as soon as one opponent reaches half health. The loser turns and retreats, moving to sit on the outskirts of the herd to recover and nurse his wounds. The winner, meanwhile, stays where he is and lets out a victorious broadcast. Cows that are impressed by the victor move forward and pepper him with affectionate calls and head shakes, with bulls shaking their head and returning the affection in kind. Once all individuals accept, the bull and his cows look to find a suitable location to nest together, with all cows preferring to nest close by to one another. Some cows may take pity on the loser, and approach him in much the same way. Losing a bullfight does not necessarily mean a bull loses out on courtship, and some cows are happy to accept being courted by a loser bull. Loser bulls, however, can only nest with one cow at a time. Once one bullfight has started, others often follow in quick succession, with Iguanodon preferring to nest at similar times to one another. Should there be no other bulls present for bullfighting, bulls can gain the favour of a cow in other ways. These bulls may bring the cows of his choice a variety of gifts - from fresh berries, to flowers, to roots and nuts. Food is a sure way to impress a female without engaging in bullfights, and can sometimes be employed by more submissive or elder bulls who struggle to best their opponents in combat. Older cows that have had clutches before sometimes prefer this courtship style. Younger cows, however, tend to enjoy the excitement of witnessing a bullfight. Cows have strong memories and sometimes hold grudges. If a bull failed to impress her a previous season, or upset her in any way, she may reject his advances. On the other hand, bulls that have shown to be excellent providers and fathers may get prioritized by that same cow next breeding season. Sometimes, a bull Iguanodon may decide to reject advances from a cow. This is unusual but not uncommon. This rejection is not aggressive, and consists of the bull simply turning away each time the cow approaches him, gently making it clear that he has no interest. Cows, while confused at first, accept this and move on to another bull quickly.
