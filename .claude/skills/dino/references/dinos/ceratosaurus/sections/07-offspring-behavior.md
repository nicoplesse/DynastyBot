# Ceratosaurus – Offspring Behavior

Baby Ceratosaurus are known as whelps. Offspring are born and reared in a dedicated den site. Hatchling Ceratosaurus are agile and quick to move from an early age. They rely on staying in the den area to avoid predators and spend their time indulging their inquisitive nature and exploring caves and burrows in the immediate area. Whelps that leave the close area surrounding the den are quickly herded back by patrolling adults; particularly stubborn offspring are given a stern aggressive call to remind them of their position within the pack. Like their adult counterparts, young Ceratosaurus have an insatiable hunger, and broadcast frequently at nearby adults for food. The smell of blood and bones is incredibly enticing for Ceratosaurus, and clumsy whelps sometimes accidentally bite one another in excitement as they rush to eat whatever morsel is provided. This is generally ignored, as the scent of fresh blood intermixes with the prey’s. Receiving multiple injuries while eating will cause the individual to retreat quickly and re-join the feast from a safer position. Siblings tolerate each other, rather than form close bonds. Due to their constant appetite, siblings prioritise their own hunger over sharing with others, and care little about the condition of weaker siblings. Ceratosaurus young are lazy in nature and do not actively bully one another, but will naturally start to exclude any sibling they view as weak. On reaching the juvenile age, whelps are permitted by the pack to leave the boundaries of their den, and accompany adults on their patrols in the surrounding area. Some juvenile aged youngsters take a shine to a particular adult, regardless of blood relation, and look to these adults as a sort of mentor. They often stroll alongside their chosen parental figure and mimic their behaviors. Adults indulge this mimicry and feel a sense of pride at being chosen, and will show small gestures of affection to the youngster as it grows. Whelps grow out of this by the end of their adolescence as they grow into their independence. Friendly bonds formed between these individuals sometimes stray into adulthood. When youngsters reach adolescence, budding hunters test their combat skills by initiating mock fights with each other and occasionally a playful adult. There is no real hostility in these clumsy encounters, and they only rarely draw actual blood. Injuries sustained from fighting immediately attract the attention of nearby pack adults, and the injured party is thoroughly checked over before being allowed to escape. At adolescence, keen offspring are permitted to tag along with a hunt to learn key skills. They are, however, not allowed to engage directly with the prey. Adults try their best to keep the excited offspring out of harm's way, but accidents are not uncommon.
