# Barsboldia – Offspring Behavior

Barsboldia offspring, known as calves, hatch blind in the warm cradle of a tightly compacted nest, with their mother resting close beside them. They instinctively wiggle towards her for comfort. Until their eyes open and their legs gain enough strength, Barsboldia calves remain quiet and peacefully snuggled up to their mother. This lasts until the last bar of the hatchling period. If left alone, calves cry out in distress, ignoring other adults until their mother returns. Luckily for their mother, this extreme separation anxiety disappears by Juvenile, though they remain clingy. The herd is ready to move on to continue their migrations when calves reach Juvenile. Calves stay closest to their mothers and siblings, and showcase shy and nervous behavior with any other Barsboldia that approaches. Juvenile Barsboldia take some time to warm up to the rest of the herd, and are hesitant to play typical games during migration, especially without their mother present. However, the presence of Struthiomimus - who love to play with baby Barsboldia - begins to warm them to the idea, and soon even the most timid calf can be coaxed into play. Barsboldia calves only eat fruit and berries until midway through Juvenile. Towards the end of this stage, mothers encourage calves to try new foods. Some calves can be particularly stubborn and refuse these new food sources initially, requiring more coaxing to get them to comply. Calves are skittish and nervous at first, requiring constant reassurance from their mothers and other adults in the herd when exposed to new experiences - especially those involving other species of dinosaur, coming across corpses, or sudden dramatic weather changes. Calves cry out in alarm at these novel experiences, and continue to do so until given a reassuring nuzzle by an adult. Female calves spend most of their time bonding with their mothers and sisters, learning to forage effectively and find reliable sources of food for themselves and potential future offspring. Male calves show a great deal of interest in their fathers as they age, who often greet them with affectionate calls. Adult males begin to show calves how to bugle as they near adolescence. Baby bull broadcasts delight a herd of Barsboldia, and prompt many affectionate calls in response. Male calves proudly attempt to perform mock bugles to impress other calves, which often provokes a chorus of young bulls calling out their own song in return. The most important relationship to a calf is the bond it shares with its mother. In every moment they try to please her, quickly learning when corrected on different behaviors, and take care to bring her small gifts to receive her affection. A calf may even run up to her simply for a nuzzle upon seeing her, even as they grow through adolescence and towards sub-adult. At Adolescent, calves begin to show confidence within social interactions, and show interest in greeting and getting to know other members of the herd. Often attended by their siblings, they begin to form the relationships that they need to succeed in courting when they reach adulthood. Most Barsboldia herds are built on generational relationships and connections, and families prefer to stick together. As calves reach the end of their adolescence, their mothers seem to grow even more affectionate with them. Calves quite enjoy this attention and they spend a good time snuggled up with their mother, even as they reach adulthood. At Sub Adult, Barsboldia begin requiring Salt Rocks like their adult counterparts. They require no guidance in locating these and instinctively consume them, driven by natural fluctuations in their mineral requirements. Once they reach Sub Adult, calves assess where they wish to remain. Barsboldia herds tolerate no more than 10 Sub/Adult Barsboldia at a time, due to pressure for resources and a high consumption of resources per individual. Should there be room for them to remain, cows are the most likely to want to stick with their mothers and siblings, and prefer to stay within the group wherever possible. Bulls, in general, develop more wanderlust, and desire to venture further in hopes of meeting new, unrelated individuals that they could form harems with. If bulls willingly choose to disperse, they always opt to go with a brother or close friend if possible. If a sister is especially bonded to him - or even a mother, especially when the bull has been raised as an only calf - she might leave to travel with him. If either sex has to disperse, they always leave with at least one closely bonded individual - usually a family member. Despite dispersing, Barsboldia frequently revisit their old herds and sometimes reintegrate if there is adequate room for them. They build lifelong friendships and relationships over the years, and their strong memories help them remember individuals.
