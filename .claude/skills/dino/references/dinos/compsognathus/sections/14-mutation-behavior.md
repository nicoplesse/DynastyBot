# Compsognathus – Mutation Behavior

Melanistic, Leucistic Albino Compsognathus are tolerated, however, males with these mutations will struggle to court. Albino Compsognathus are significantly weaker than their non-albino counterparts, and struggle with keeping up with swarms and when hunting. With reduced stamina, and a sensitivity to sunlight, albino Compsognathus are mainly active around dusk, nighttime, and dawn. Albino Compsognathus are at an increased risk to aggressive critters, especially when solitary. Without the protection of a swarm, their only hope of escaping such a threat is to flee to a place where the critter cannot follow them. Melanistic Compsognathus have no such disadvantages, but are considered female due to their dull coloration. Leucistic Comspognathus fare better than Albinos, with no known health conditions faced. Compsognathus prioritize vocal aggression rather than physical aggression. Screeching matches are common, with the more dominant individuals showing their displeasure at every opportunity. The dominant male and dominant females tend to be the most aggressive individuals within the swarm. They win their position by bullying the rest of the group, and claim the best nesting sites and first choice of meat because of this. The lowest ranking members may even end up being denied food by the rest of the swarm - especially if they are sickly, or upset the dominant members one too many times. Dominance battles are quick and rarely dangerous. Compsognathus will take slow, considered steps towards their opponent before snapping and kicking at them until one of them flees. The first to run is considered to be the loser - though all Compsognathus will flee before their health gets too low (before they go below half health). Compsognathus may “choose” any carnivore, or group of carnivores, to follow for the entirety of their lives. This behavior only persists with species that tolerate them, and are most common with Giganotosaurus and Tyrannosaurus. It never occurs with Quetzalcoatlus or Hatzegopteryx. They seek both protection and scavenging opportunities from their chosen carnivores, and often develop vocalization patterns unique to themselves and their chosen carnivore. Compsognathus will often help these carnivores nest. However, this does mean bringing any item that a Compsognathus believes is helpful, rather than what is actually needed for construction of the nest! Solitary Compsognathus are tolerant to other Compsognathus around their chosen carnivore(s) but may nip at other scavengers. A solitary Compsognathus may nip other scavengers up to 3 times without it being considered the start of an engagement. The Compsognathus must be following their chosen carnivore in order to display their unwillingness to share. If not following their carnivore and solitary, they do not display this behavior. Compsognathus never take part in hunts or challenges alongside their chosen carnivore, and instead quickly move to a place to watch or hide - in order to avoid becoming collateral damage. Compsognathus within a swarm will show open curiosity towards herbivores of Medium tier and above, but never get close unless actively attempting to hunt an unattended baby. If aggressed by these herbivores, they always opt to flee. Compsognathus fear Quetzalcoatlus and Hatzegopteryx, and scatter at the sight of one, always immediately attempting to find bushes or dens to hide in. Compsognathus never hunt offspring or adoptees of their chosen carnivore, and instead understand that they belong to their chosen carnivore. Other hatchlings and juveniles of the same species may still be targeted - as long as they have no connection to their chosen carnivore. Compsognathus rarely hunt and prefer to scavenge or catch easy prey, such as critters. They are highly opportunistic, however, and will not turn down an opportunity to hunt if one presents itself. They will only hunt Small tier species, unattended hatchling or juveniles of any tier, or death-scarred individuals of any tier. Up to 10 Compsognathus may form a hunting party.  They are highly strategic and intelligent, working together to bring down the prey. A common Compsognathus tactic is to attempt to lead a baby away from an attentive herd or parent by squeaking, jumping, and otherwise being a curiosity. When the baby has been led far enough from their group, other Compsognathus surge in, taking advantage of a parent’s lapse in attention in order to claim a meal. Compsognathus use latching onto prey as a form of defence. Their quick movements and ability to pounce off of their targets make friendly fire a constant risk for the hunted party, which often lends itself to a Compsognathus’s advantage.
