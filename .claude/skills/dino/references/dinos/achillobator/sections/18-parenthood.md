# Achillobator – Parenthood

Once pups have hatched, the mother inspects her offspring, cleaning their wet feathers. She introduces herself to each one with a soft call, which they return in soft squeaks and squeals. As soon as the pups are settled, she swaps places with her mate in order to fetch critters. The tod repeats the same introduction process with his pups, and curls around them snugly to keep them warm. The female then finds the nearest meal, most likely several critters, to eat before returning to her nest and mate. As pups reach Juvenile, they begin to exhibit more playfulness, both towards their siblings, any Latenivenatrix, and their parents. Parents return these tiny games in turn, but are careful not to accidentally trample pups, rarely running too quickly when near them. Pups are fiercely protected by both parents throughout their lives, though especially once beginning to move with the pups. A nipping pup, especially one who has bitten its parent, is corrected swiftly, yet gently, often with a snap in the air next to the pup. Pups are often encouraged to pounce onto the backs and sides of their parents, especially when fleeing a potential predator. Achillobator pups are always given priority to latch onto their sides over Latenivenatrix, but a parent may allow a Latenivenatrix to latch if there is room. Pups are very vocal, but if they are too vocal during a period intended to be quiet, parents quickly and gently correct the behavior with eye contact and sharp huffs. Towards the end of their Juvenile stage, pups go through a brief fear stage, where things once familiar to them can suddenly spook them. Parents gently reassure pups and nudge them back towards confronting their fear once the pups have settled. Without this reinforcement, pups may maintain these anxieties throughout the rest of their life. At adolescence, pups begin to express more independence and maturity, even going as far as to hunt critters and other small prey on their own. Parents observe this, but hardly interfere, unless the safety of the pup is at risk. Parents continue to correct over-aggressive or dominant behavior, but may even drive out a pup if these behaviors go too far. Additionally, some parents may attempt to linger and offer comfort to pups who distance themselves– but will eventually move on without them if the situation calls for it.
