# Austroraptor – Offspring Behavior (Nested)

Hatchling Austroraptor, called chicks, are born feeble, fluffy, and completely reliant on their parents. With non-waterproof feathers, weak legs, and closed eyes, they rely on the warmth and comfort of their nest, parents, and siblings to survive. Throughout the hatchling stage, chicks remain bundled together in the nests, only peeping when cold, hungry, or thirsty. Parents take turns bringing food to the chicks and sitting on the nest, especially at night or when raining. Once Juvenile, Austroraptor chicks begin to open their eyes, and start to develop the leg strength to bobble around. Carefully watched over by their doting parents, Austroraptor chicks take their first wobbly steps alongside their siblings. It does not take long for chicks to rapidly increase in confidence when it comes to walking and running, and before long chicks are waddling around their nests and up to their parents eagerly for food and attention. Chicks rarely, if ever, go further than a bodylength from the nest before quickly being ferried back by an adult. They are still unable to swim at this age; dense with fluffy feathers that serve to keep them warm on windy nesting grounds, over granting them the ability to swim. Chicks also begin to express interest in their siblings at Juvenile; initiating short games of play, preening, and even posturing contests. They typically follow around the parent they are most bonded with, but both parents will quickly carry them back to the nest if they are followed too far. Chicks soon learn how far they can - and cannot - follow. If predators come, a chick’s first line of defense is being picked up and taken to the cover of water by its parents. If not picked up in time, chicks rush to the nearest coverage, rather than water. When it is cold or raining, chicks wail for the attention of their parents, huddling closely together with their siblings in the nest. Parents rush to take care of wailing chicks, and curl up around them, snuggling them in close to their feathers. Chicks finally settle at that point, and fall asleep easily. At Adolescent, chicks finally begin to shed out of their light colored baby feathers and into their adult swimming feathers. Once all chicks in a clutch are Adolescent, the entire clutch is taken for their first swim, and is quickly taught their parents' unique hunting strategies and calls. Once chicks are confident in swimming and hunting, young adolescents fall into line, acting as nearly fully independent adults within the group. Parental protection ends once chicks reach Sub-Adult, but family members may exchange the occasional show of tenderness.
