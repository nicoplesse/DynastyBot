# Pachyrhinosaurus – Bulls

When traveling outside their nesting grounds, male Pachyrhinosaurus are passive, and tolerate traveling near other males at the rear of the migration. A father and his sons typically walk together, with the younger bulls relying on his wisdom and guidance. During times of rest, related bulls spar in mock bullfights. This tolerant behavior lasts until they step foot in their nesting grounds. A bull will only become testy and pushy when nesting. Outside of that, they work together to defend the migration, resting side by side and feeding together while they trail behind the females. A bull will only become defensive and aggressive towards other bulls when in a nesting ground. Outside of that, they tolerate each others' mutual goal in defending the migration and walk together at the rear of the females. COWS When at a nesting ground, females that participate in nesting are defensive of their nests, readily stepping in front of other species, herdmates and predators to protect her newly laid eggs and hatched young. Females rely on the bull to protect their nesting site, while she stands guard and issues warning bellows. When the Elder female calls to migrate, bulls remain stubbornly at their bush - only abandoning their vigil once their females leave his territory. On their migration, cows are not aggressive with each other and rely strongly on those they consider allies. Only females with a hormonal imbalance react differently. See Social Interaction, high T females. When migrating, Pachyrhinosaurus bulls are indifferent to the existence of everything else. Cows might call to other certatopsians they herd with, but otherwise do not form any bonds with these species. Outside of nesting grounds, Pachrhinosaurus do not approach other herbivores that do not group with them; instead, they watch from a distance, giving the occasional grunt to alert each other that another species is spotted. When challenged over resources, Pachyrhinosaurus stubbornly remain where they eat, pushing the rival herbivore away after issuing threatening bellows to ward away conflict. If solitary or grouped with other Pachyrhinosaurus, they show little concern for other species tagging along. However, when it comes to their territory within nesting areas, the dynamic shifts dramatically. Both males and females become fiercely protective of their eggs and hatchlings, aggressively using their shove ability to push away any intruders - friendly or not. When attempting to push away other species, Pachyrhinosaurus must aggressively call :threaten_call: before using any attack.
