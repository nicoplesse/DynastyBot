# Pachyrhinosaurus – Migration

The migration is led by the eldest cow - oftentimes it's the one who has the most allies in the herd. Pachyrhinosaurus females follow those they trust to guide them through rough terrain and cows will follow the majority of the group. Sometimes an older cow joins an existing migration. It is not uncommon for the herd to temporarily split for some time before regrouping at their nesting grounds. While traveling, males follow the migratory females from the rear of the group. If a female or offspring lags behind, males paw the ground :aggro_emote: in the direction they want the slow individual to move towards. If ignored, a frustrated bull may call aggressively to force the individual to listen. Oftentimes this does the trick, but bold younglings often roar defiance and charge in a game of play. However, they are quickly subdued by an aggressive roar from the irritated bull or the call of their mother. Males ignore all herbivores that aren't Pachyrhinosaurus females, related males or grouped ceratopsians. Related bulls bond closely, whilst non related bulls barely acknowledge the existence of each other, often rubbing sides accidentally during migration or permitted to sleep nearby for shared warmth. Only related bulls (brothers, or father and sons) form the closest male bonds and call to each other during their travels. A bull always prefers the company of his closely bonded sons or brothers in his shield, but other male relatives are treated with kindness in the herd. Related bulls have friendly dispositions to each other, walking together and assessing each other's strength with playful bullfighting during down time. Females, meanwhile, are passive to all within the herd and enjoy bonding through drinking and eating side by side. Cows that have remained in the same herd for many nesting seasons form close friendships and prefer to walk near those they consider allies. If an ally is trailing behind, a cow does stop to call to them, turning her head from side to side to try to spot her absent ally. When reunited, cows shake their shoulders together and grunt - a sound mimicked by nearby females, and ignored by bulls.
