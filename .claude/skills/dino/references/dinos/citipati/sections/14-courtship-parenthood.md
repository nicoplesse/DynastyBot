# Citipati – Courtship & Parenthood

Citipati form small harem groups with one rooster looking after and courting up to three hens. Hens are approached by roosters who display a feet stomping, neck waving, hooping call as the interested hen looks on. If she accepts his offer, she steps forward and gives an affectionate call, which he repeats. The two nuzzle one another and shake their feathers. Females prefer males with bright striking colours and may reject those with dull or dark coloration. Leucistic and melanistic hens are not treated any differently. Leucistic and melanistic roosters are not shunned, but it is very unlikely that they will be accepted by a hen. Hens within a harem stick closely together and spend a lot of time grooming one another, rarely straying further than a few steps away. The rooster watches over his group carefully, and is very alert to any danger. Roosters that both wish to claim the same hen will sometimes fight one another as detailed in Aggressive Interaction. Hens that do not have chicks but are within a harem are sometimes approached by rival roosters wishing to draw them away and entice them into forming a new harem, or joining his existing one. The rooster in this situation does not tolerate courting behaviour from other roosters with any of his hens. He rushes at the rival and they fight (as detailed in Aggressive Interaction). If the rival rooster wins the fight, the hen in question now has the option of choosing to leave her harem and join the new rooster, or she may prefer to decline his advances and remain in her existing harem. A hen with chicks is never courted by another rooster. Chicks are lovingly raised and nurtured all by the hens within the harem. The rooster is affectionate towards his own offspring, but does not take part in any child rearing behavior. His attention is primarily focussed on protecting and bonding with his hens. Hens love picking up and carrying their chicks, and may become distressed if they have too many chicks to divide their attention evenly between all of them. If a hen has multiple chicks, the rooster may choose to pick up and carry chicks around for her, he will focus on a hen who he has bonded with the most and his assistance is greatly valued by the exhausted hen. Roosters must take care when carrying chicks about, and must stay close to the hen that the chick belongs to. Any aggression, injury, or rough handling of a hen’s chick will outrage the hen and could result in her leaving the harem altogether. Chicks up to the age of Adolescent that wander away from the protection of their harem are sometimes killed by unrelated roosters - in the hope that a hen without chicks will soon seek to bond with a new rooster. An exception to this is if an adult hen does not have any chicks, comes across an abandoned or orphan chick, she will immediately adopt it and care for it as her own. This can result in chicks being "stolen" and adopted by broody unrelated hens. If a hen adopts an unrelated chick, the rooster within her harem will consider it his own offspring, and other hens in her harem will accept it as a child to protect. Broody Hens Hens that have not cared for chicks for a while will start to feel broody. This means the hen is very eager to raise chicks of her own, and she will start to carry around shells or nuts, or even small creatures of other species (anything they can pick up and carry) and treat them as if they were her child. The adopted creature of another species is considered her offspring for all intents and purposes and is defended from predators and other Citipati as such. While the hen is caring for a small creature that she has adopted, she will not nest with a rooster Citipati and will refuse his advances. As soon as the adopted creature reaches an age where it can no longer be carried with the grab ability, the broody hen will let it go and no longer treat it as a chick; this may be acted out as saying goodbye and moving away, and then ignoring the dinosaur completely. Some tiny creatures will never “age out” e.g. Psittacosaurus, Compsognathus, Rhamphorhynchus, Dryosaurus, and Deinonychus. After a hen has been caring for one of these species for an extended period of time, the rooster may grow frustrated and choose to take matters into his own hands. Roosters may chase out, or attack and kill these tiny adoptees, ideally while the hen is sleeping or distracted, and hope to encourage her to nest with him soon after. Hens may be distressed at first, but will overcome their sadness after a short period of time. Roosters may bring their hen gifts of berries or small curiosities to encourage her forgiveness. Aged-out adopted creatures of a different species may be treated neutrally, but the Citipati who raised it will not continue to show affection or favoritism for it after it is grown. Broody hens will be overly loving towards chicks within her harem and will help the mother to raise them. If there are no chicks within her harem, she will start to feel jealous of mothers with chicks in other harems and watch carefully to see if one strays too far from its mother, or if there are any orphan chicks to adopt. Hens may fight one another if a broody hen tries to steal a wandering chick from its mother, as detailed in Aggressive Interaction.
