# Tropeognathus – Social Interaction

Tropeognathus seem to gather in large numbers instinctively, with vast swarms of these pterosaurs being a common sight to behold in particularly fruitful coastal regions. Tropeognathus have a loose overall hierarchy in a cluster - with the most dominant individual - typically a young, bold male - bullying the others to take prime position when it comes to roosting spots, choosing a partner, and getting first dibs on a kill. Tropeognathus form very loose relationships with others. Females tend to bond more closely with a couple of individuals - usually relatives - whereas males keep a more aloof approach to other males in a group. Males will, however, show affection towards females, especially nearing courting. Tropeognathus individuals roost fairly close to one another when taking naps, with females and young aiming to shuffle more tightly into the middle of a group, and males flanking the outskirts. They enjoy playing together - with females and males both performing aerial displays and dives, and chasing after one another in the sky. Fledglings in particular adore this type of play, but mothers tend to remain more serious with their young and refuse to engage in this play with them, much to their disappointment. This is very unlike adult males, who can sometimes be too rough in their over excitement, resulting in a chick's frightened squawks. Particularly dominant males - who have proven themselves as exemplary hunters, fishers and defenders - tend to gather many admirers, both male and female alike. During rest periods these admirers flock towards the male, preening and cooing affectionately at him. Tropeognathus generally tolerate albino and melanistic individuals, though albino males struggle the most to attract a mate during courting. Females suffer no such struggle, with males happy to court almost any female that will accept him.
