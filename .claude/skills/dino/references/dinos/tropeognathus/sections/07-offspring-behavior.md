# Tropeognathus – Offspring Behavior

Baby Tropeognathus, known as fledglings, are born relatively confident and begin screaming for food as soon as they gather their senses after emerging from the egg. Able to walk within a very short period after hatching, fledglings are rambunctious, eagerly breaking out into a run for short periods, and chasing after their siblings and other fledglings at the nesting grounds. Though this playful - and sometimes irritating - behavior can lead them to stray from their nests, they are quick to return once they spot their mother returning with food for them. Fledglings playfight near endlessly with any other individual that will tolerate them. This is usually others of the same age - whether siblings or not - and adult males. Adult females tend to ignore the constant bickering, and fledglings quickly learn not to mess with their mothers. Tropeognathus - unlike many other species of pterosaur - do not imprint on an individual or parents, and will beg for food from the nearest adult of their species. However, they instinctively seem to recognize their mothers, who they direct the majority of their pestering towards. Those that are unfortunate enough to lose their mothers will quickly seek to find a surrogate, aiming to push their way into another nest and become adopted. Tropeognathus begin hopping and jumping very shortly after hatching. This helps strengthen their muscles and is a major part of play for young fledglings. Though they do not begin flight during the hatchling stage, this excitable energy helps to prepare them for lift off. By juvenile, fledgling Tropeognathus begin to practice flight. Being raised in a coastal region with high winds - this is a far easier feat for this aerial species than it is for some others. Usually fledglings take one or two attempts to feel brave enough to take off - but once they learn that they can, they develop new aerial skills quickly and efficiently. Fledglings are fairly competitive with one another and very pushy and bold. When one fledgling begins to start attempting flight, it drives all others within line of sight to imitate - often resulting in a whole batch of fledglings learning to fly at the same time. Around the same time that they learn flight is when fledglings begin to start exploring the ocean around them. This is sometimes incidental - for example, being blown into the water during flight - but otherwise deliberate - observing as the other adults in the cluster dive into the water and wishing to copy them. Fledglings quickly grow to be adept at both swimming and flying, and thoroughly enjoy dive bombing one another - and bemused adults(!) - and grabbing various items from the seafloor to keep as treasures. Once all fledglings approach adolescence, the adults in the cluster begin to travel again, leaving the familiarity of their nesting grounds to move back along the coast. Fledglings happily follow the rest of the adults, keen to keep up and be perceived as active members of the collective group. Fledglings continue to bother their mother for food even when they are more than capable of providing for themselves - a behavior that extends right up until they turn Adult. Mothers may or may not indulge these fledglings, with some preferring to encourage independence from a much earlier age, while others are happy to provide for their offspring for as long as possible.
