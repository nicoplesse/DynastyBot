# Tropeognathus – Courtship & Parenthood

Tropeognathus instinctively migrate to Ripple Beach when wanting to breed, and usually do so in a large group. Courtship is only done during the daytime, with the night reserved for other pursuits - such as hunting. Males locate a suitable patch of sand in the dappled ocean ponds, and begin clacking their beaks together. The beak clacking is accompanied with raising and spreading of their wings, before finally they lift their heads to croon out a low, rumbling song. These noises continue in a cycle, with the male occasionally turning in his spot to continue his display from all angles, and adding additional wing stretches to highlight his impressive physique. The more central the location on the beach, the more attractive the male will appear to the females. Multiple males thus push and shove each other to get the prime coveted position,  which can lead to escalations into vicious fights. If two males refuse to move aside for the other, they may begin aggressive calling, squawking, flashing out their wings and finally pecking at the other. When it is clear neither will back down, both males will take off to the sky and begin swooping and diving at each other aggressively, landing heavy attacks by slamming their bodies against each other during flight. These aerial battles continue until one of them reaches half health - marking this male as the loser, and thus the one who has to give up his position. Now shamed, this male is ousted to the outskirts of the cluster, forced to take a subprime spot while the other male moves into the more central location. Females watch all of these displays keenly, walking on all fours as they approach and observe each male on the beach. They may take several trips wandering around the displaying males before settling on their choice. When a female spots a male that she is impressed by, she will approach him and affectionately clack her beak and trill at him, and if he is happy to accept, he will return her affection. The female will then settle beside the male quietly until he has finished courting. Males may nest with several females at once each nesting season - so particularly successful males may end up with a wide array of females beside him in his courting spot, highlighting his attractiveness. When all females have chosen, the new groupings all leave Ripple Beach together - even individuals that did not end up finding a partner, as they prefer to remain with the greater cluster regardless - and travel north to Ocean Pillars, following the coastline round. On arrival at Ocean Pillars, females flutter to select a spot along the coastline to place a nest. This is usually one very close to water - if not in the water entirely - and begin flying back and forth collecting resources to build them. Males assist the females they successfully courted in ferrying resources, splitting their time between multiple females if they had multiple successes. Once nests are built, females settle on the nests to incubate eggs, only leaving for short periods to dip into the ocean to fish, but returning just as quickly. As females brood, males spend their time circling overhead, taking care to pay special attention to any females that might be incubating his eggs. Though they do not directly intervene in the nesting process, they are quick to defend the females and the nesting site from any invaders. Once fledglings hatch, all child rearing is mostly left up to the mothers. Mothers tenderly indulge their often over excited offspring and immediately start a process of preening them, showing them affection, and then leaving to begin ferrying food back to their hungry offspring. Fathers watch from afar in the young stages, careful not to risk the wrath of a protective mother - but once fledglings begin flying and initiating play they start to forge bonds with them. Excitable and playful fledglings are firmly scolded by their mothers who do not tolerate bursts of over excitement, but are hugely encouraged by their equally playful fathers - who dive and swoop with them, eager to join in with their games. Fledglings sometimes let out cries of fear when adult males indulge in such play with them. Fathers, sadly, lack a lot of restraint when it comes to playing with offspring, and can unwittingly end up knocking a youngster out of the sky in their excitement. Once the youngest fledgling in the cluster reaches adolescence, the males of the group will let out loud squawking calls and circle the group, signifying the time to fly. Mothers destroy their nests to leave no trace behind, and the entire cluster takes to the skies, resuming their nomadic lifestyle once again as the group moves on to comb the coastlines.
