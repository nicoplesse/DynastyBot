# Parasaurolophus – Orphaned Behavior (Wild Spawn)

Orphaned fawns start out in life much the same as those with parents - they stay within the confines of where they were hatched, resting silently in the coverage to avoid attracting predators. Through necessity and hunger, fawns wobble to their feet somewhat quicker than other offspring, and begin getting up to move before the end of the hatchling period. Instinct drives them to seek out similar species to themselves - other hadrosaurs and similar species are looked for as potential surrogate parents. Iguanodon, Barsboldia, Lambeosaurus, Tenontosaurus and even Camptosaurus are followed if other Parasaurolophus cannot be found. The orphan remains persistent in following their new surrogate parent. If they find a Parasaurolophus herd, they are usually adopted into it without issue - and even many hadrosauridae and similar species show considerable tolerance - perhaps even affection - to orphaned fawns. Individuals that never manage to encounter other Parasaurolophus - or friendly species that tolerate them - end up as much quieter adults, and often struggle to interpret common social cues. Over time, this behavior can be improved. Parasaurolophus are a highly social species, and group in large numbers that migrate together in mixed sex groups. Social bonds between groups are built on mutual respect. Both stags and does are considerate of each other; over-aggression from either sex is quickly corrected by others, and results in shunning from the rest of the group if these corrections are repeatedly ignored. Parasaurolophus are cautious and observant, and frequently scan their surroundings for danger, even while foraging. Their movements are typically slow and steady on all fours, allowing them to conserve energy during travel and grazing moments. When startled, they rise up onto two legs, and are capable of sudden bursts of speed. They are diurnal, active during the day when they migrate and search for new food and water sources. At night, they locate a place to settle and rest, preferring to clump together in a large group for protection. While the herd sleeps, a maximum of 2 stags (or sometimes does, especially if there are low numbers of stags in the herd) remain awake during the night. These individuals patrol around the sleeping group on their hind legs, or may opt to sit at a good vantage point to keep watch. These patrollers swap out with others throughout the night, with patrollers leaning down to nuzzle a chosen individual awake in order to pass the duty on. Some Parasaurolophus prefer to patrol for longer periods, while others do much shorter shifts, preferring to defer to others and settle down to sleep. Parasaurolophus prefer open areas where they can clearly see their surroundings. They become uneasy in forested or high coverage areas, aware of the potential of predators hiding in the dark. Parasaurolophus are extremely vocal throughout the day, calling out a chorus of songs that become infectious throughout a herd. A continuous song ensures the content status of a herd, often only dwindling to silence at times of stress or uncertainty. The opposite is true at night times, with Parasaurolophus quick to correct others that become too noisy while the herd tries to rest. Parasaurolophus are creatures of habit - they follow familiar migration routes with reliable access to food and water. These migration routes are often passed down between generations, with each herd developing and maintaining its own distinct route over time. Due to their continuous migration, some Parasaurolophus may be found laying in shallow water for a short periods of time to relax and cool off. Parasaurolophus do not drink salt water, but may take a couple of sips (no more than 5 seconds) when plagued by internal parasites. Herdmates regard this behavior nervously, and temporarily avoid the individual through fear of catching their sickness. In some instances when agitated, stags can be seen kicking their hind legs at the source of their unhappiness or staring intensely.
