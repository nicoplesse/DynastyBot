# Alioramus – Offspring Behavior (Nested)

Alioramus offspring, known as cubs, burst out of their eggs ready to take on the world. They hatch fully precocial, and stagger to their feet within moments of hatching, shaking off their damp feathers. Cubs are usually immediately greeted by at least one - if not two - females: their mother and an auntie, who coo over the fresh young and get to work nibbling and preening stuck feathers. Alioramus hatchlings do not differentiate between the adult females - both are equally regarded as a mother figure, and most cubs grow without ever knowing who their biological mother is out of the pair. Cubs are quite often a handful. Innately curious, Alioramus young take to investigating almost everything they encounter - assuming they are permitted to do so by their caretakers. Sticks, flowers, mushrooms and bones are common play toys, and often fetched up by eager cubs who guard them from their siblings fiercely. Adult females frequently have to intervene in little squabbles between cubs over these circumstances - while another sibling typically steals the possession amidst the chaos. Alioramus cubs are escorted to almost all locations they go to - or attempt to go to - until Adolescent. Adult females take it in turns to ferry rambunctious cubs to water sources, and get anxious leaving cubs alone. Cubs are very chatty, and use a wide variety of different noises with their mothers and clutchmates, mimicking the calls their parents use in hunts and throughout their adult lives. Cubs tend to be less noisy when fully satisfied and calm, when the pack has settled and they are surrounded by their siblings and friends. Investigating the world around them is vital for a healthy cub’s development; a lack of interest or obliviousness to the world indicates illness. Cubs work together to investigate things that are larger than them in a sort of “mobbing” behavior, bobbing their heads and squeaking at curious things and new experiences. However, cubs only investigate larger dinosaurs from a distance, quickly bolting away if the larger creature even slightly moves towards them. Mothers strictly prevent cubs from approaching their fathers or other adult males within the pack. Adult males play too roughly and lack restraint with young offspring, often posing a genuine risk of serious injury - or even accidental death. This protective instinct can draw out an uncommon show of aggression from females towards males that approach their young. However, as cubs reach Adolescent, mothers gradually allow them to roam further. This developmental shift coincides with heightened interest from adult males, who begin to investigate these new "playmates". Hormonal shifts during adolescence lead to a clear behavioral divergence between males and females. Females begin to settle in temperament, showing reduced interest in play and social chaos of the rest of the pack. They gravitate towards stability, usually in the form of a close bond with a sister or other similarly aged female. When no such companion is available, they trail behind the adult female pair - or their mother, if she is alone - and mirror their movements, as closely as they are tolerated. If a female cub lacks sisters and her mother already has a female companion, she begins to experience increasing emotional - and physical - distance from the adult female pair. This aloofness usually escalates into aggression as she matures, frequently resulting in solitary females dispersing from their natal pack earlier than their peers. Males, meanwhile, experience a rapid rise in testosterone - resulting in bold, dominant and increasingly aggressive behavior. They engage in frequent bouts of roughhousing with their brothers and other males within the pack, using physical play and sparring as a means of testing strength and hierarchy within the group. At Sub-Adult, females are driven out by the adult female pair to prevent future competition - unless the mother lacks a bonded female companion. In such cases, she finds a new companion in a daughter, allowing her to remain as her permanent bonded female companion. By contrast, Sub-Adult males are permitted to remain within the pack indefinitely, provided there is room for them. However, during periods of female dispersal, unrelated males to these females within the pack may choose to leave voluntarily, following these females to form new packs and courting opportunities elsewhere.
