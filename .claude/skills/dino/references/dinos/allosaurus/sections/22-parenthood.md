# Allosaurus – Parenthood

Once eggs have hatched, the presence of cubs results in a hormonal change within the female Allosaurus, in which she no longer tolerates the male near her nest. She will react with aggression if he attempts to approach the nest or enter the den site, biting and clawing if she must to get the message across. If the pair already have a chosen offspring grouped with them, then her tolerance will depend on their sex. Female offspring are tolerated within the den site and by the nest, and often encouraged to look after her younger siblings. Male offspring are condemned to the same treatment as their father, and kept away from the nest, expected to aid in patrolling the territory instead. Mothers often refuse to leave the den site for days at a time, focusing all their time and energy in caring for their cubs. However, if alerted to a groupmate being attacked, mothers are permitted to leave the den to assist in their defence. They cannot leave the den to join in on hunts. Fathers are expected to continue patrolling the surrounding area, and bring back meals for his mate, leaving them at the entrance of the den site before quickly retreating, so as not to provoke further attacks. Once cubs have reached mid-hatchling, the mother’s guarded instincts begin to relax and stabilise, especially when the cubs begin running and playing. She will first wait for night-time before guiding her cubs out of the den site for the very first time, quietly leading them to the nearest source of fresh water. The father will either follow from a distance, or remain waiting at the den site for their safe return. Male Allosaurus, upon witnessing the success of the clutch, may choose to abandon their family at this stage, having deemed himself as free to roam once more. This is only ever seen between pairs that have formed a temporary pairbond to nest, and more commonly seen in orphaned or solitary individuals. If the father does not abandon his family, then cubs will be introduced to him after their fresh water visit, with intense supervision conducted by the mother. The father is expected to remain as non-threatening as possible. By either sitting or crouching, he must remain almost perfectly still, with only occasional slow movements of his head permitted when he inspects his young. If a cub is at all spooked, she will first comfort the cub, before turning her irritation back to the father, warning him with a hiss - and more, if necessary. Once the mother is satisfied with her mate’s temperament, she will call to him softly, providing him the freedom to move around the den site as he pleases once more. Fathers do not take much interest in their offspring despite the introduction, nor are they expected to provide them with much care. Should the mother die at any point, the father takes no interest in childrearing, and abandons his offspring. At adolescence, both parents reward the offspring with greater independence, and begin distancing themselves from the clutch. Mothers take the time to encourage young cubs to practise hunting on small critters, rewarding any effort with affection, and continue to encourage this behaviour by stealing their catches away from them. Once she is satisfied with their progress, she loosens her supervision of them, focussing on patrolling the POI more often. Parents understand that rival Allosaurus, particularly males, will not turn down the chance to cull or chase out adolescent Allosaurus, and as such, must work tirelessly to ensure the territory remains safe. Parents rarely linger by their offspring at this stage, conducting only brief check-ins when passing by, or if they hear the cubs crying for help in the distance. Particularly noisy cubs are scolded for this reason and warned against making noise, with parents growing frustrated upon realizing their rush was for nothing. Despite this, they will never ignore their cub’s call for help. If parents return to find their cubs in danger, be it due to a rival male Allosaurus or a hunt gone wrong, only 1 parent may step forward to defend the cubs. In order to protect cubs during a hunt, the cubs must have already forfeited the engagement on their side through affectionate calling the other party. Cubs must make this obvious to the parent by affectionate or fear calling again once the parent arrives to witness the ongoing engagement. The parent may step in to defend the cub immediately after recognizing this fact. This limit does not apply to engagements where cubs are hunted by Medium Tier and above species. All Sub-Adults/Adults within a group may come to the defence of an adolescent in this instance. If the territory is lost while offspring are being raised, then outcomes vary based on the cub’s ages. *If offspring have not reached mid-hatchling, or can not be easily collected on the way out, then they are abandoned. Parents are not allowed to double back into the territory to collect their offspring, or remain stationary in a single location to do so. Offspring must be able to meet and/or follow their parents after forfeit to be safely escorted out. If abandoned, offspring must find their own way back to their family, either through following any remaining scent, or being collected by the parents once the territory has been won back. Adolescents are considered a separate group from their parents during territory challenges if separated from them. In the circumstance that parents lose a territory challenge, and have no way of alerting the Adolescents, then the Adolescents may remain in the territory until they realize, or are challenged out themselves.
