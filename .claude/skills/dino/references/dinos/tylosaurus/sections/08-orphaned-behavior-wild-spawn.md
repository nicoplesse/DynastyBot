# Tylosaurus – Orphaned Behavior (Wild Spawn)

Tylosaurus calves who hatch alone quickly flee to a kelp bed or heavy object floating in the water to treat as a pseudo-mother. At times, these calves can be observed following other aquatic species in an attempt to have food provided to them, and comfort offered. Rejection by these other species results in a Tylosaurus calf fleeing, and never seeking comfort from an unfamiliar species again. These calves take much longer to befriend, even within their own species. Tylosaurus calves are adopted at any time by willing cows, and while the bonding process may take some time with particularly fearful or skittish calves, they do eventually warm up to the idea. They eagerly return their newfound mother’s gentle licks and stay close to her, refusing to leave her side. Orphans that have been adopted may remain as a part of their mother’s matriline; however, they must make a bloodline post to log their adoption. If a Tylosaurus calf is orphaned while a part of a matriline, they will be quickly adopted by another cow in the matriline, and may still remain with their matriline once Sub-Adult. Orphans who grew up without contact with other Tylosaurus may struggle with socialization and courtship. These individuals may form their own matriline, or join a rover if a bull. Tylosaurus are nomadic mosasaurids who form strong familial bonds, with groups typically consisting of a mother and her adult offspring. Tylosaurus migrate through the oceans of Gondwa and Riparia. Tylosaurus have good eyesight both above and below the water, and often partake in a behavior called “spyhopping” - briefly lifting their faces out of the water to look around at what lies above. Tylosaurus have excellent hearing, especially within the water. Tylosaurus develop unique vocalizations and sounds that are shared within their matrilines, and can expand across entire pods, especially if they are all from one mother. Tylosaurus are nearly constantly vocalizing, primarily below water when it is not the breeding season. These loud, powerful calls can span leagues beneath the waves, and are quickly picked up on by other Tylosaurus. While Tylosaurus can withstand great depths, Tylosaurus pods are often seen partaking in surface behaviors such as breaching (jumping completely out of the water), tail-slapping, and sleeping amongst kelp. These are popular methods of bonding between Tylosaurus, as well as methods of removing parasites. Tylosaurus additionally have a great sense of touch, which they use to identify other Tylosaurus and their offspring in deep, dark water. Tylosaurus often bond through close proximity and physical contact, such as swimming nearby one another and brushing against each other. Tylosaurus often spin through the water nearby other Tylosaurus to initiate play. The most straightforward way for a Tylosaurus to express affection for another Tylosaurus is through mutual grooming, done by continuous flicks of their tongue against the scales of their friend, sibling, offspring, or parent. Tylosaurus also use their tongues to detect changes in the water, such as vibrations, temperature changes, and scents. Tylosaurus often display stress by opening their mouths as widely as possible, both as a way to show their strength and to quickly turn the movement into a strong bite if required (letting Bone Snap fully charge up). Particularly bonded individuals exhibit synchronous surfacing, a behavior where all members in a matriline, rover, or pod rise to the surface of the water together and float for a while. Tylosaurus prefer to hunt other aquatic species, but will hunt terrestrial or aerial species if they are swimming in water or within a close length of the water and easy enough to grab. Tylosaurus will avoid staying beached on land for too long, in order to avoid being hunted by opportunistic predators. The further a Tylosaurus goes on land, the higher the risk they have of becoming dehydrated and dying to the heat.
