# Tylosaurus – Offspring Behavior (Nested)

Tylosaurus offspring, known as calves, hatch fully capable of movement - and begin swimming, diving, and biting shortly after hatching. They hatch within carefully constructed kelp nests, and must quickly move from the nest and into the water. Calves - despite their physical abilities - are extremely fragile, and receive gentle cleaning by their mothers before they are tucked along her side or beneath her fins in the water. Calves reciprocate this affection towards their mother, even if the cleaning gesture does not land. This often means babies continuously flick their tongues in the water, regardless of if anyone is in front of them. Calves instinctively stay close to their mothers and siblings, but pay their clutchmates very little attention during the hatchling stage. Their only priority is their mother, meaning that other calves may be shoved out of the way in effort to be close to her. At Juvenile, Tylosaurus calves develop more of an awareness for the world around them, and begin to explore different things with their mouths - particularly their siblings. This takes the form of playful nips and continuous “licking”.  They flick their tongues to catch the scent of other Tylosaurus, critters, or curious objects in the water. Tylosaurus calves at this point can be seen breaching the water often, jumping high out of the water in efforts to display to other calves, as well to their mothers and relatives, who call to them affectionately. When frightened, Tylosaurus calves will flee to hide beneath a suitable adult in their matriline, or their mothers, who shield them with their bodies. Tylosaurus calves show a natural interest in hunting at this point, and begin following critters, Tropeognathus, Thalassodromeus, Rhamphornhychus, and other small creatures through the water. Tylosaurus calves below Adolescent are extremely susceptible to heat and direct sunlight, and will die quickly if beached for an extended period of time (more than 5 minutes). Once beached, they require an adult to push them back into water - something which carries considerable risk for the adult Tylosaurus. Beached Tylosaurus calves are extremely susceptible to Hatzegopteryx, who take the opportunity to grab the calf from the sands and fly away with it, much to the panic of their mothers. Mothers - or other adult caretakers - are sometimes unable to move quick enough on land to save the calf. Tylosaurus form strong bonds with their siblings in their youth, working together to chase aquatic critters and fish down, and playing by charging and spinning past one another. By adolescence, calves begin to exhibit behavioral dimorphism, with cows preferring the bonds of their sisters and mothers, while bulls begin to show more independence and desire to roam. Calves quickly pick up on hunting skills as they age. Watching from a safe distance as their family hunts prey, they observe the vital skills and strategies they will need to feed their matriline. These skills are well practised, with young hunting down rays, small sharks, and fish. Calves show a strong curiosity towards their surroundings, and rush to grab items of interest within their jaws. They take time to inspect these items before investigating them further with their tongues and mouths, and present particularly favored items to their siblings, mother, and grandmother. Adolescent calves also mimic the calls of the adults in their matriline, repeatedly echoing the sounds until it has the perfect and unique tonation to match the rest of their matriline. At Sub-Adult, if there is room, calves may remain in group with their natal matriline. Otherwise, they disperse from the group but may remain nearby, either within a rover or - in the case of daughters - dispersing into a sisterhood, or going alone until they can court and form their own matriline.
