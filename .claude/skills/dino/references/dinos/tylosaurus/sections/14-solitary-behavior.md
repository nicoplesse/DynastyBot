# Tylosaurus – Solitary Behavior

Solitary Tylosaurus typically exhibit higher anxiety or aggression. They are unable to join matrilines as they only consist of a mother and her offspring, and may only form rover groupings if they are male. A solitary cow may travel very far in search of a suitable matriline to follow, preferably one with bulls, so that she may form her own matriline. Cows thrive in social situations, and are often seen traveling very quietly rather than with the typical noisiness of groups. Cows who are solitary with offspring are extremely protective of them, and show aggression towards rovers that get too close, tucking their offspring beneath them. Solitary Tylosaurus often take short naps in caves for shelter, and prefer to stay out of shallow waters in order to avoid the risk of being hunted by terrestrial and aerial predators. When threatened, it is very common for solitary Tylosaurus to rush into the engagement, calling aggressively and loudly to attempt to intimidate the threat away from them. They use their powerful heads and tails to whip threats away from themselves. If they become too injured, they quickly flee from the engagement, using underwater caves or tunnels to their advantage. Solitary bull Tylosaurus often charge into objects to release energy and stress, sending pebbles and dirt to the sea bed. Bulls are significantly more aggressive when solitary than when they are within groups, and completely reject offspring that approach them. These bulls often enter into fierce challenges with creatures like solitary Spinosaurus and Deinosuchus. Tylosaurus that are solitary exhibit the most stress out of all Tylosaurus, often opening their mouths wide and rubbing up against objects in an attempt to soothe themselves. Solitary Tylosaurus who were raised by a matriline will display the unique hunting behaviors of their matriline, while solitary Tylosaurus who grew up orphans will develop their own hunting behaviors based on their own experiences.
