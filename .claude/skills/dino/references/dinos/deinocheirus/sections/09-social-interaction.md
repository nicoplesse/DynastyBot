# Deinocheirus – Social Interaction

Deinocheirus are aloof creatures that form monogamous pairs for life. Despite their aloof nature, they have a strong craving for social interactions. They spend nearly all of their time with their mate, engaging in activities such as grooming each other, foraging for food, basking in the sun, and chewing on lakeweed together. This close companionship helps them develop a deep bond that strengthens their partnership. Throughout the year, it is very common to observe multiple pairs of Deinocheirus congregating around bodies of water, especially during the breeding season. These gatherings are often peaceful and harmonious, with adult pairs relaxing on the shore while their young play together in the shallows. This shared environment fosters a strong sense of community among the offspring, who learn important social cues and behaviors through their interactions with one another. These playful exchanges not only strengthen their bonds but also prepare them for future social encounters. Males tend to be louder and more energetic than females, moving around more frequently and being more vocal in their behavior. They often vocalize loudly at any potential threat to alert their mate and other Deinocheirus in the area, acting as protectors of their shared space. This heightened alertness helps ensure the safety of their family unit and reinforces their role as vigilant guardians. In contrast, females are generally calmer and more reserved than males. They often prefer to stay in one place for longer periods, taking time to relax and enjoy their surroundings. This difference in behavior allows them to create a stable environment for their offspring while still engaging in nurturing activities with their mate. Deinocheirus can sometimes be seeming gathering together in massive groups, consisting of multiple different adult pairs, in a grouping known as a gathering. These groups are relatively harmonious, with pairs socializing with other pairs without many squabbles. Deinocheirus split from these groups when looking to nest or adopt young, and swim off with their partner to form a smaller group while they build a nest. Unpaired females congregate together inside the gathering, and tend to be more social and affectionate between each other than small pools of bachelor groups. Though social, if attacked, they will not defend one another. Unpaired males do similarly to unpaired females, forming small bachelor groups of no more than 5 males at a time within the gathering, and choosing to spend time together, until they find a mate of their own. They spend their time posturing, and practicing for eventual courtship. They do not defend one another if attacked.
